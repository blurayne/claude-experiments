## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

*Penicillium chrysogenum* is a **filamentous ascomycete mould** (Eurotiales, Aspergillaceae) — a **multicellular** fungus, and therefore the opposite of its set-mate *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, which is a single yeast cell. Its body is a **mycelium**: a spreading, branching mat of **hyphae** roughly **2–4 µm wide**, **hyaline** (colourless and translucent) and **septate** — divided at intervals by **cross-walls (septa)**, each pierced by a **central pore** through which cytoplasm, and even nuclei, flow from compartment to compartment (damaged septa are plugged by Woronin bodies). The hyphal wall is fungal chemistry: **chitin microfibrils embedded in β-1,3-/β-1,6-glucan and mannoproteins** — there is **no peptidoglycan** anywhere, which is exactly why the antibiotic it makes cannot hurt the fungus itself.

**The brush is the whole point.** From the vegetative mycelium a hypha turns upward as an **erect conidiophore** — a smooth-walled, hyaline **stipe** typically **200–300 µm long** (up to ~500 µm) and 2.5–4 µm wide. At its tip the stipe does **not swell**. Instead it **branches**, usually **terverticillately** (three tiers: stipe → **rami** → **metulae** → phialides; bi- and quaterverticillate penicilli also occur). The final branch cells, the **metulae** (~8–12 µm long), sit in whorls of three or more and each carries a whorl of **phialides**: **flask-shaped (ampulliform)** conidiogenous cells about **7–10 × 2–2.5 µm** with a short tapered neck, typically two to three per metula, and always shorter than the metulae that bear them. The whole splayed, symmetrically branched apex looks like a tiny painter's brush — Latin ***penicillus***, "little brush" — and that is where both the genus name and, via it, the word *penicillin* come from.

**Spores are pinched off, not shed from a sac.** Each phialide is a **phialidic** conidiogenous cell: it repeatedly **pinches off** a spore from its own open neck, and because each new spore is produced *underneath* the previous one, the spores stack into a **long, dry, unbranched chain in basipetal succession** — the **oldest conidium sits at the free tip of the chain and the youngest at the base**, on the phialide. These **conidia** are asexual spores, smooth-walled, subglobose to ellipsoidal, **~3–4 µm**, and they carry the fungus's only real pigment: a **blue-green to grey-green** colour that makes a mature colony velvety blue-green with a white growing margin. **They are not enclosed in anything** — no sac, no membrane, no sporangium; they simply crumble away into the air, which is why a mouldy orange puffs green dust when you touch it. *P. chrysogenum* additionally exudes a **bright yellow soluble pigment** into the agar and often forms yellow droplets of exudate on the colony surface; *chrysogenum* means "golden-producing".

**Why it makes penicillin.** Penicillin is a **secondary metabolite** — a chemical weapon in the crowded, competitive world of soil, damp bread and rotting fruit, where the mould's neighbours are bacteria after the same sugars. Three enzymes do the work: **ACV synthetase** condenses L-α-aminoadipate, L-cysteine and L-valine into the tripeptide ACV; **isopenicillin N synthase** closes the strained **four-membered β-lactam ring** fused to a thiazolidine ring; and a peroxisomal **acyltransferase** swaps the side chain (with phenylacetic acid in the medium, giving **penicillin G**). The finished β-lactam is exported. In a target bacterium it mimics the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of the peptidoglycan precursor and **acylates the DD-transpeptidases (penicillin-binding proteins)** that cross-link the wall; a growing cell then keeps cutting its wall open without stitching it shut, and bursts under its own turgor. This is also the sharpest teaching point in the whole set: **peptidoglycan exists only in bacteria**. Human cells do not have it, which is why penicillin is remarkably safe; and **viruses do not have it either — or any cell wall at all — so an antibiotic can never work against a cold or flu.**

**The accident.** In **September 1928** Alexander Fleming returned from holiday to his laboratory at St Mary's Hospital, London, and found a *Staphylococcus* plate he had left out spoiled by a stray blue-green mould — and around that mould colony a **clear ring in which the staphylococci had dissolved**. Instead of discarding the plate he cultured the mould, showed that its broth filtrate inhibited many bacteria, and in 1929 named the active substance **penicillin**. He could not purify or stabilise it; that took **Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and Norman Heatley** at Oxford, who produced enough to treat the first patients in 1941, after which wartime production moved to the United States. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the **1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine**. A taxonomic footnote worth being honest about: Fleming's own isolate — long labelled *P. notatum* — and the high-yielding Peoria "mouldy cantaloupe" strain NRRL 1951 that founded the industrial lineage (via Wisconsin 54-1255) were **re-identified in 2011 as the sibling species *Penicillium rubens***, while the name *P. chrysogenum* is retained for the closely related species that this plate depicts. The two are microscopically near-identical brush-headed moulds and both are penicillin producers; only DNA sequencing (ITS, β-tubulin) separates them.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **No swollen vesicle at the top of the stalk.** This is the single most important error to avoid: *Aspergillus* ends its stipe in a **globose vesicle** with phialides radiating from it like a dandelion clock. *Penicillium* has **no vesicle at all** — the stipe simply branches into rami and metulae. A round knob at the apex = wrong genus → re-render.
- **No sporangium, no closed sac or bag of spores, no columella, no sporangiophore.** Conidia are pinched off **naked, in the open air**, from the phialide neck. A sac full of spores is *Rhizopus*/*Mucor* territory.
- **No rhizoids and no stolons** running along a substrate (again *Rhizopus*).
- **No aseptate / coenocytic hyphae** — *Penicillium* hyphae **are** septate; the cross-walls must be visible.
- **Not a mushroom.** No cap, no stem-and-cap silhouette, no gills, no toadstool, no visible macroscopic fruiting body of any kind.
- **No ascocarp, cleistothecium or ascus with ascospores.** The asexual (anamorph) brush is the whole archetype here; the rarely-observed sexual cycle must not appear.
- **Not a single yeast cell.** No round or ovoid budding cell, no bud, no bud neck, no bud scars — that is the sibling plate *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* in this same set.
- **Conidial chains must be UNBRANCHED** — one straight chain per phialide, never a chain that forks halfway up.
- **Do not reverse the chain order** — the youngest, palest spore sits **at the base**, on the phialide; the oldest at the free tip.
- **No bacterial features whatsoever:** no peptidoglycan wall, no nucleoid, no plasmids, no flagellum, no pili, no capsule.
- **No chloroplasts and no chlorophyll green.** The green is a conidial wall pigment; this fungus does not photosynthesise.
- **No props in the scientific plates** — no Petri dish, agar plate, pills, capsules, syringes, bread, oranges or cheese, and no drawn "inhibition zone". Those belong only to the kids' coloring page.
- **No cartoon face, no anthropomorphism, no text, no letters, no numbers, no scale bar, no watermark, no arrows.**
- **Not a shapeless fuzzy green blob** — if the brush architecture (stipe → metulae → phialides → chains) is not readable, the plate fails its teaching job.

### Colour note

Unusually for this atlas, the colour here is **partly real**. The **conidia** carry a genuine **blue-green / grey-green wall pigment** (that is the colour of mould on bread), and *P. chrysogenum* exudes a **bright yellow soluble pigment** into its medium — the "golden" in *chrysogenum*. Everything else is **hyaline**: the hyphae, the conidiophore, the metulae and the phialides are colourless and translucent, and any tint given to them in these plates is teaching convention. Kept consistent across all four styles: hyphae and conidiophore pale translucent ivory with a thin dusty-ochre wall line, septa as slightly darker teal-grey cross-lines, metulae pale sage, phialides warm cream, conidia sage to blue-green spheres (palest at the base of each chain, deepest at the free tip), on a neutral dark charcoal background — warm aged paper for the watercolour plate.

🇬🇧 Penicillium chrysogenum is an ordinary mould with an extraordinary side effect. It grows as a mat of fine, colourless threads through damp bread, soil and rotting fruit, and when it is ready to spread it raises upright stalks that branch at the tip into whorls of little flask-shaped cells. Each flask pinches spore after spore off its own neck, so the spores pile up into long dry chains and the whole head splays out like a paintbrush — penicillus in Latin, which is where both the mould's name and the word penicillin come from. A growing bacterium keeps unpicking its wall to expand and can no longer close it up again, so it bursts. That is why a mould colony on an agar plate sits in a clear halo where no bacteria grow. The three shared the Nobel Prize in 1945. That is not a technicality — taking antibiotics for a viral infection cannot help you and does breed resistant bacteria, which is why prescriptions have become deliberately more sparing.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 Penicillium chrysogenum is a filamentous ascomycete (Eurotiales, Aspergillaceae) whose vegetative body is a mycelium of hyaline, septate, branching hyphae 2-4 um wide, the septa perforated by a central pore and plugged on injury by Woronin bodies; the wall is chitin embedded in beta-1,3-/beta-1,6-glucan and mannoproteins, with no peptidoglycan. Asexual reproduction is by conidiogenesis from erect, smooth-walled, hyaline conidiophores 200-300 um long (stipe 2.5-4 um wide) that terminate without a vesicle in a typically terverticillate penicillus: stipe to rami to metulae (8-12 um) to whorls of two to three ampulliform phialides (7-10 x 2-2.5 um). Each phialide is a phialidic conidiogenous cell producing conidia basipetally from its open neck, so conidia accumulate in long unbranched dry chains with the oldest distal; conidia are smooth, subglobose to ellipsoidal, 3-4 um, and pigmented blue-green, giving colonies a velutinous blue-green surface with a white margin, a yellow soluble pigment and yellow exudate droplets. The absence of a swollen vesicle is the primary morphological discriminator from Aspergillus. Penicillin is a secondary metabolite assembled by a three-step pathway: the nonribosomal peptide synthetase ACV synthetase (pcbAB) condenses L-alpha-aminoadipate, L-cysteine and L-valine to the tripeptide ACV; isopenicillin N synthase (pcbC) performs oxidative bicyclisation to the beta-lactam-thiazolidine nucleus; and peroxisomal isopenicillin N acyltransferase (penDE) exchanges the side chain, yielding penicillin G with phenylacetate as precursor. The beta-lactam acylates the active-site serine of DD-transpeptidases (penicillin-binding proteins), abolishing peptidoglycan cross-linking so that autolysin-driven wall remodelling in a growing cell proceeds unchecked and the cell lyses osmotically. Selectivity follows directly from target distribution: peptidoglycan is bacterial, absent from human cells and absent from viruses, so beta-lactams have no antiviral activity whatsoever. Fleming's 1928 observation of staphylococcal lysis around a contaminating colony initiated the field; his isolate and the industrial Wisconsin lineage derived from NRRL 1951 were re-identified in 2011 as the sibling species P. rubens, the two being separable only by sequencing (ITS, benA, CaM, RPB2). Industrial strains carry amplified penicillin-gene clusters and yield tens of grams per litre.  
🇩🇪 Penicillium chrysogenum ist ein filamentöser Schlauchpilz (Eurotiales, Aspergillaceae), dessen vegetativer Körper ein Myzel aus hyalinen, septierten, verzweigten Hyphen von 2-4 um Durchmesser ist; die Septen tragen einen zentralen Porus und werden bei Verletzung durch Woronin-Körper verschlossen. Die Zellwand besteht aus Chitin in einem Netz aus beta-1,3-/beta-1,6-Glucan und Mannoproteinen und enthält kein Peptidoglycan. Die asexuelle Vermehrung erfolgt über Konidiogenese an aufrechten, glattwandigen, hyalinen Konidienträgern von 200-300 um Länge (Stiel 2,5-4 um breit), die ohne Vesikel in einem meist terverticillaten Penicillus enden: Stiel, Rami, Metulae (8-12 um) und Wirtel aus zwei bis drei ampulliformen Phialiden (7-10 x 2-2,5 um). Jede Phialide ist eine phialidische Konidiogenzelle und schnürt an ihrem offenen Hals basipetal Konidien ab, sodass lange, unverzweigte Trockenketten mit der ältesten Konidie distal entstehen. Die Konidien sind glatt, subglobos bis ellipsoid, 3-4 um und blaugrün pigmentiert; Kolonien erscheinen dadurch samtig blaugrün mit weißem Rand, dazu ein gelbes lösliches Pigment und gelbe Exsudattropfen. Das Fehlen eines geschwollenen Vesikels ist das wichtigste morphologische Unterscheidungsmerkmal gegenüber Aspergillus. Penicillin ist ein Sekundärmetabolit aus einem dreistufigen Weg: Die nichtribosomale Peptidsynthetase ACV-Synthetase (pcbAB) verknüpft L-alpha-Aminoadipat, L-Cystein und L-Valin zum Tripeptid ACV; die Isopenicillin-N-Synthase (pcbC) bildet durch oxidative Bicyclisierung den beta-Lactam-Thiazolidin-Kern; die peroxisomale Isopenicillin-N-Acyltransferase (penDE) tauscht die Seitenkette aus und liefert mit Phenylacetat als Vorstufe Penicillin G. Das beta-Lactam acyliert das aktive Serin der DD-Transpeptidasen (Penicillin-bindende Proteine) und unterbindet die Quervernetzung des Peptidoglycans; der autolysingetriebene Wandumbau einer wachsenden Zelle läuft dann ungebremst weiter, und die Zelle lysiert osmotisch. Die Selektivität folgt unmittelbar aus der Zielverteilung: Peptidoglycan ist bakterienspezifisch, fehlt menschlichen Zellen und fehlt Viren vollständig, weshalb beta-Lactame keinerlei antivirale Wirkung besitzen. Flemings Beobachtung der Staphylokokken-Lyse um eine Kontaminationskolonie im Jahr 1928 begründete das Feld; sein Isolat und die industrielle Wisconsin-Linie aus NRRL 1951 wurden 2011 als die Schwesterart P. rubens neu bestimmt, die sich nur durch Sequenzierung (ITS, benA, CaM, RPB2) abgrenzen lässt. Industriestämme tragen amplifizierte Penicillin-Gencluster und erreichen Ausbeuten im Bereich von mehreren zehn Gramm pro Liter.
