## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

*Giardia lamblia* (synonyms *G. duodenalis*, *G. intestinalis*; phylum Metamonada) is a **binucleate, flagellated protozoan parasite** of the small intestine and one of the most basal-branching eukaryotic lineages studied in cell biology — it is essentially **amitochondriate** (only vestigial mitosomes remain) and lacks a conventional Golgi stack in the trophozoite stage. It alternates between two life stages: the motile **trophozoite**, which does the feeding and the damage, and the dormant, environmentally-resistant **cyst**, which does the spreading.

The **trophozoite** is one of the most recognisable shapes in microbiology: **bilaterally symmetric**, a **teardrop or pear outline** roughly **12-15 µm long by 5-9 µm wide**, broad and rounded at the anterior (front) end and tapering to a point at the posterior (rear) end. The **dorsal surface is convex** (domed) and the **ventral surface is flat to gently concave** — a mirror-image organism, and getting that symmetry right is the single most important shape cue. It carries **two nuclei**, morphologically identical, lying symmetrically either side of the long midline near the broad anterior end, each with a central **karyosome** and no peripheral chromatin — together they give the classic **face-like appearance** ("two eyes") that makes *Giardia* instantly recognisable in stained smears. The **ventral adhesive (sucking) disc** is a large, concave, spirally-ridged array of microtubules and disc-specific proteins (giardins) set into the flat underside; it is the organ *Giardia* uses to clamp onto the apical brush border of duodenal and jejunal enterocytes — non-invasively, by suction and mechanical grip, not by penetrating the cell. A pair of **median bodies**, claw-hammer- or comma-shaped bundles of microtubules of still-uncertain definitive function, sit on the midline dorsal to the caudal axonemes and are a further diagnostic feature. Locomotion and part of the attachment hydrodynamics come from **eight flagella in four bilateral pairs** — anterior, posterolateral, ventral and caudal — arising from four pairs of basal bodies clustered between the two nuclei. Eight is a checkable, diagnostic fact, not an artistic flourish.

Attachment by the disc mechanically coats and blunts the intestinal villi and disrupts tight junctions and brush-border enzymes; because *Giardia* stays in the intestinal lumen and never invades tissue, it causes malabsorption — especially of fat and fat-soluble vitamins — rather than bloody or invasive diarrhoea. As trophozoites are carried toward the colon they **encyst**, triggered by cholesterol deprivation, bile and rising pH, into an **oval, thick-walled cyst roughly 8-12 x 7-10 µm** with a fibrillar wall. The mature cyst contains **four nuclei** (produced by one round of nuclear division without cytokinesis), internalized/retracted axonemes, and typically two (immature) to four (mature) median bodies — but **no free flagella** beating outside the wall. The cyst is the tough, infective, environmentally stable stage: it resists routine chlorination, survives cold water for weeks to months, and an infectious dose as low as 10-25 cysts is enough — which is exactly why hikers and campers are told to boil or filter backcountry water rather than trust its clarity, and why municipal water treatment relies on filtration rather than chlorination alone to remove it.

Sources: Adam RD, "Biology of *Giardia lamblia*", *Clin Microbiol Rev* 2001 (PMC88984) — trophozoite/cyst ultrastructure, nuclei, median body, ventral disc, encystation triggers; CDC DPDx bench aid, *Giardia intestinalis (lamblia)* (https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/resources/pdf/benchaids/giardia_benchaid.pdf) — diagnostic trophozoite/cyst morphology and sizes; microbewiki, *Giardia lamblia* (https://microbewiki.kenyon.edu/index.php/Giardia_lamblia) — general morphology overview; CDC Public Health Image Library, image 8698 (https://phil.cdc.gov/Details.aspx?pid=8698) — SEM reference image, caption, credit, license.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **A single nucleus, or asymmetric/off-centre nuclei** — the two nuclei must be a mirror-image pair either side of the midline, both visible together giving the face-like look.
- **An amoeba with pseudopods, a blobby shape-shifting outline, or crawling amoeboid motion** — *Giardia* keeps a fixed bilaterally symmetric pear/teardrop outline; it does not flow or extend pseudopods.
- **A ciliate covered in a dense short fuzzy coat of cilia all over the body** (that is *Paramecium*/*Balantidium*) — *Giardia* has exactly **eight discrete long flagella in four pairs**, not a ciliated surface.
- **More or fewer than eight flagella** on the trophozoite — the count (four pairs: anterior, posterolateral, ventral, caudal) is a checkable diagnostic fact.
- **A bacterial cell wall, peptidoglycan layer, capsule, pilus or nucleoid** — *Giardia* is a eukaryote with a nuclear membrane, not a bacterium.
- **A virus capsid, icosahedral shell or envelope with spikes** — *Giardia* is a single-celled eukaryotic protozoan, not a virus.
- **Prominent classical mitochondria or a large single Golgi stack** — the trophozoite is amitochondriate (only reduced mitosomes) with minimal/absent Golgi.
- **An asymmetric, lopsided or radially symmetric body plan** — the defining feature is bilateral symmetry down the long axis.
- **The adhesive disc as a flat sticky pad with no internal spiral structure, or placed on the dorsal instead of the ventral surface.**
- **A flat two-dimensional silhouette with no dorsal/ventral distinction** — the dorsal surface is convex and the ventral surface is flat/concave, and this should be readable in 3D renders.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 Giardia lamblia (also called G. duodenalis or G. intestinalis) is one of the most common intestinal parasites worldwide and a familiar hazard on hiking trails: 'beaver fever' picked up from untreated stream or lake water. Unlike many gut pathogens, it does not invade the intestinal wall or spread through the bloodstream — it stays in the lumen of the small intestine, its adhesive disc clamped onto the surface of the mucosa in such numbers that it can carpet the villi, physically blunting them and interfering with the enzymes and bile salts needed to digest fat. That is why giardiasis so often causes greasy, foul-smelling diarrhoea, bloating and cramps, sometimes lasting for weeks, alongside poor absorption of fat-soluble vitamins. Infection starts with swallowing surprisingly few cysts — as few as ten — which is why it spreads easily through contaminated drinking water, swimming pools, and person-to-person contact in childcare settings. The cyst is the tough, dormant stage that makes Giardia a genuine water-treatment problem: it resists chlorine at the concentrations used in routine disinfection and survives for weeks in cold water, so filtration (not just chlorination) is what actually removes it from a water supply. For travellers and hikers, boiling water for a minute or running it through a fine filter both destroy or remove the cysts reliably. Diagnosis is usually by stool antigen test or PCR, and treatment with a short course of an antiparasitic drug such as metronidazole or tinidazole clears the infection in the great majority of people.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 Giardia lamblia (syn. G. duodenalis, G. intestinalis; phylum Metamonada, order Diplomonadida) is a binucleate, flagellated protozoan parasite of the small intestine and one of the most basal-branching eukaryotic lineages studied for cell-biology comparison, being essentially amitochondriate (retaining only vestigial mitosomes) and lacking canonical peroxisomes and a conventional Golgi stack in the trophozoite. The motile trophozoite is bilaterally symmetric, pyriform in outline, roughly 12-15 um long by 5-9 um wide by 2-4 um thick, dorsally convex and ventrally flattened/concave. It carries two morphologically identical, anteriorly-paired nuclei, each with a central karyosome and no peripheral chromatin, lying symmetrically either side of the sagittal axis and giving the classic 'face-like' appearance in stained smears. Eight flagella arranged in four bilateral pairs (anterior, posterolateral, ventral and caudal) arise from four pairs of basal bodies clustered between the nuclei; the anterior and ventral flagella contribute to the disc-associated cytoskeleton and are implicated in the hydrodynamic component of attachment, while flagellar beating is chiefly responsible for motility rather than adhesion per se. The single most diagnostic structure is the ventral adhesive (sucking) disc, a concave, spiral array of microtubules and associated disc-specific proteins (giardins, including alpha-, beta- and gamma-giardin) supported by a peripheral ring of microribbons and crossbridges that generates the suction/mechanical clamp onto the apical brush border of duodenal and jejunal enterocytes; attachment is non-invasive, and pathology (villus blunting, disaccharidase deficiency, malabsorption, particularly of fat and fat-soluble vitamins) arises from mechanical coverage of the mucosa, disruption of tight junctions and host and parasite factors rather than tissue invasion. A pair of median bodies, claw-hammer- or comma-shaped microtubule aggregates of unknown definitive function lying dorsal to the caudal axonemes on the midline, is a further diagnostic feature whose morphology varies taxonomically between Giardia species. Encystation, triggered by cholesterol deprivation, bile and alkaline pH as the trophozoite transits toward the colon, produces an oval, thick-walled cyst (~8-12 x 7-10 um) with a fibrillar wall of GalNAc-homopolymer material; the mature cyst contains four nuclei (formed by a single round of nuclear division without cytokinesis) plus internalized, retracted axonemes and typically two (immature) to four (mature) median bodies. The cyst is the environmentally stable and infective stage: it resists routine chlorination, tolerates cold water for weeks to months, and an infectious dose as low as 10-25 cysts is sufficient, which together with faecal-oral, waterborne and zoonotic transmission (multiple host-adapted assemblages, A and B being the main zoonotic/human-infecting groups) makes Giardia one of the most common protozoan causes of diarrhoeal disease and a WHO-listed neglected disease. Standard water treatment relies on filtration (the cyst's chlorine resistance being well above that of most bacteria) alongside boiling (>1 min) for point-of-use disinfection; first-line therapy is a 5-HT/nitroimidazole antiparasitic (metronidazole, tinidazole) or, second-line, nitazoxanide or albendazole.  
🇩🇪 Giardia lamblia (syn. G. duodenalis, G. intestinalis; Stamm Metamonada, Ordnung Diplomonadida) ist ein binukleärer, begeißelter Protozoen-Parasit des Dünndarms und zugleich eine der basalsten eukaryotischen Linien, die in der Zellbiologie vergleichend untersucht werden: Der Trophozoit ist praktisch amitochondrial (nur rudimentäre Mitosomen sind erhalten) und besitzt keine klassischen Peroxisomen sowie keinen konventionellen Golgi-Stapel. Der bewegliche Trophozoit ist bilateral symmetrisch, birnenförmig im Umriss, etwa 12-15 um lang, 5-9 um breit und 2-4 um dick, dorsal konvex und ventral abgeflacht bis konkav. Er trägt zwei morphologisch identische, anterior gepaarte Zellkerne mit je einem zentralen Karyosom ohne periphere Chromatinverdichtung, die symmetrisch beiderseits der Sagittalachse liegen und in gefärbten Ausstrichen den klassischen 'gesichtsähnlichen' Eindruck erzeugen. Die Zyste ist das umweltstabile und infektiöse Stadium: Sie widersteht der routinemäßigen Chlorung, übersteht kaltes Wasser über Wochen bis Monate, und schon eine infektiöse Dosis von 10 bis 25 Zysten genügt — zusammen mit fäkal-oraler, wasserassoziierter und zoonotischer Übertragung (mehrere wirtsangepasste Assemblagen, wobei A und B die wichtigsten zoonotischen bzw. humanpathogenen Gruppen sind) macht dies Giardia zu einer der häufigsten protozoären Ursachen von Durchfallerkrankungen und zu einer von der WHO gelisteten vernachlässigten Krankheit.
