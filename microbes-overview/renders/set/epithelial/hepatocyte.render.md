## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The hepatocyte is the parenchymal cell of the liver, making up roughly 80% of the organ's mass and about 60% of its cells. It is a large **polyhedral** cell — typically six or more flat faces, never a sphere — measuring **20–30 µm** across (this poster uses 25 µm). Hepatocytes are stacked into anastomosing **laminae/plates one cell thick** that radiate between the portal tracts and the central vein, with **hepatic sinusoids** running between the plates. Blood flows inward from the portal triad (portal vein + hepatic artery + bile duct) toward the central vein while bile flows outward in the opposite direction — a **counter-current** arrangement. The nucleus is large, round and euchromatic ("open-faced") with one or more **prominent nucleoli**, and anisokaryosis is normal. Critically, **up to ~25% of adult human hepatocytes are binucleate** and 30–50% are polyploid (2n–8n) as a result of incomplete cytokinesis, the fraction rising with age and oxidative stress — so a scientifically honest render should show a cell with **two nuclei**.

The organelle load is extreme. Each cell carries roughly **800–2,000 mitochondria** (the source of the eosinophilic granular cytoplasm on H&E), abundant **rough endoplasmic reticulum** in basophilic patches that synthesises the plasma proteins — albumin, fibrinogen, prothrombin and the other clotting factors, transferrin, apolipoproteins — and an extensive **smooth endoplasmic reticulum** carrying the cytochrome P450 mono-oxygenases and UDP-glucuronosyltransferases for phase I/II biotransformation of drugs, alcohol and hormones, bilirubin conjugation, and the glucose-6-phosphatase step that terminates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis. There are **200–300 peroxisomes** per cell for fatty-acid β-oxidation, catalase-mediated H₂O₂ disposal and ethanol oxidation, numerous **lysosomes** clustered near the bile canaliculus, and on the order of **50 separate Golgi stacks**. The cytosol stores sugar as free **glycogen β-rosettes** hugging the smooth ER and fat as membrane-free **lipid droplets**, both fluctuating with nutritional state; lipofuscin accumulates with age.

Above all, the hepatocyte is **polarised between two very different faces**, and this is the whole point of the figure. Its **basolateral (sinusoidal)** domain — roughly 70% of the surface — bears irregular, short **microvilli** projecting into the **space of Disse (perisinusoidal space)** beneath a discontinuous, **fenestrated sinusoidal endothelium** that has no true basal lamina, giving the cell direct access to plasma via transporters such as NTCP, OATPs and GLUT2. Its **apical (canalicular)** domain is not a free surface at all: the **bile canaliculus** is a tiny groove or gutter, ~0.5–2 µm wide, formed where the membranes of **two adjacent hepatocytes** appose, sealed on both shoulders by **tight junctions (zonulae occludentes)**, lined by short microvilli, and carrying the ABC export pumps BSEP/ABCB11, MDR3/ABCB4 and MRP2/ABCC2 that drive bile formation; it drains toward the canals of Hering and the portal triad's bile ductule. Lateral faces additionally carry **gap junctions** for metabolic coupling and desmosomes/adherens junctions for cohesion. Function is zonated along the porto-central axis (periportal: gluconeogenesis, ureagenesis, β-oxidation; pericentral: glycolysis, lipogenesis, CYP-mediated xenobiotic metabolism). Hepatocytes are long-lived and normally quiescent, yet retain a famous **regenerative capacity**: after loss of up to ~70% of liver mass, differentiated hepatocytes re-enter the cell cycle and restore the organ.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **No bacterial features whatsoever** — no cell wall, capsule, nucleoid, plasmids, pili or flagellum. This is a eukaryotic human cell.
- **No cilia on any face.** Hepatocytes carry short, irregular **microvilli**, never a beating hair carpet. (Cholangiocytes, not hepatocytes, carry a primary cilium.)
- **No single large central vacuole and no chloroplasts** — that is a plant cell.
- **Not a smooth round free-floating ball.** It is **polyhedral** and wedged into a plate between neighbouring hepatocytes; parts of the neighbours must be shown or implied.
- **The bile canaliculus is NOT a tube through the cytoplasm** and not a lumen inside one cell — it is a **groove on the shared surface between two adjacent cells**, sealed by tight junctions.
- **Do not put the bile canaliculus and the sinusoid on the same face** — apical (canalicular) and basolateral (sinusoidal) domains are on *different* faces, separated by the tight junctions.
- **Not a tall columnar cell with a neat apical brush border** — that is an enterocyte. Hepatocyte microvilli are short, irregular and on the *basolateral* side.
- **No mucus droplet or secretory mucin granules** — that is a goblet cell. No ciliated or goblet-cell neighbours, no villi or crypts.
- **Do not show only one nucleus dead-centre** — binucleation is a hallmark and should be visible.
- No basal lamina under the sinusoidal face (there is none — only the loose matrix of the Disse space).
- Glycogen rosettes and lipid droplets are **free in the cytosol**, not membrane-bound vesicles.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 Hepatocytes make up roughly 80% of the liver's mass and are arguably the most metabolically versatile cells in the body. Each is a polyhedral block about 25 micrometres across, packed into plates one cell thick, and each faces two completely different worlds. One face borders a sinusoid — a wide, leaky blood channel — and is covered in microvilli that dip into a narrow fluid gap, the space of Disse, giving the cell direct access to everything arriving from the gut and the general circulation. The opposite face is pressed against a neighbouring hepatocyte, and where the two membranes meet they leave a tiny sealed gutter: the bile canaliculus. Material therefore moves in both directions at once — taken up from and released into the blood on one side, secreted as bile on the other, with blood and bile flowing in opposite directions through the lobule. The interior reflects this workload. An extensive smooth endoplasmic reticulum carries the cytochrome P450 enzymes that chemically modify drugs, alcohol and hormones so they can be excreted; abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum manufactures albumin, clotting factors and transport proteins for the plasma; a thousand or more mitochondria and a few hundred peroxisomes supply energy and break down fatty acids; and the cytoplasm stores sugar as glycogen rosettes and fat as lipid droplets, buffering your blood sugar between meals. Many hepatocytes carry two nuclei, and a large fraction are polyploid — an unusual arrangement that appears to support their heavy synthetic output and their tolerance of chemical stress. Unusually for a specialised cell, the hepatocyte can also re-enter the cell cycle and divide, which is why a damaged liver can regrow substantial lost tissue — a capacity that is remarkable but not unlimited, and that repeated injury from alcohol, chronic viral hepatitis or fatty liver disease eventually exhausts as scar tissue takes over.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The hepatocyte is a polyhedral, epithelial-derived parenchymal cell of ~20-30 µm diameter that constitutes roughly 80% of liver mass and about 60-65% of its cells. Hepatocytes are arranged in anastomosing laminae one cell thick, radiating from the portal tracts toward the central vein and separated by fenestrated, discontinuous sinusoids. The cell is functionally polarized without a classical apical brush border: the basolateral (sinusoidal) domain, comprising roughly 70% of the surface, projects irregular microvilli into the space of Disse and mediates bidirectional exchange with plasma via transporters including NTCP, OATPs and GLUT2; the apical (canalicular) domain is a narrow groove formed between adjacent hepatocytes and sealed circumferentially by tight junctions, bearing short microvilli and the ATP-binding-cassette export pumps BSEP/ABCB11, MDR3/ABCB4 and MRP2/ABCC2 that drive bile formation. Lateral domains carry gap junctions and desmosomes. Approximately 25-30% of adult human hepatocytes are binucleate, and a substantial fraction are polyploid (4n or higher), generated by failed cytokinesis; ploidy increases with age and oxidative stress. The cytoplasm is exceptionally organelle-rich: 1,000-2,000 mitochondria per cell, extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum supporting secretion of albumin, fibrinogen, prothrombin and other coagulation factors, transferrin and apolipoproteins; a prominent smooth endoplasmic reticulum housing cytochrome P450 mono-oxygenases, UDP-glucuronosyltransferases and glucose-6-phosphatase for phase I/II biotransformation and the terminal step of glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis; several hundred peroxisomes performing fatty-acid beta-oxidation, catalase-mediated H2O2 disposal and ethanol oxidation; numerous lysosomes; and multiple Golgi complexes near the canaliculus. Cytosolic glycogen rosettes and lipid droplets fluctuate with nutritional state. Hepatocyte function is zonated along the porto-central axis, with periportal cells favouring gluconeogenesis, ureagenesis and beta-oxidation and pericentral cells favouring glycolysis, lipogenesis and CYP-mediated xenobiotic metabolism. Although normally quiescent (G0), hepatocytes retain the capacity to re-enter the cell cycle and restore liver mass after partial hepatectomy or injury, driven by HGF, EGF-family ligands, IL-6 and TNF signalling.
