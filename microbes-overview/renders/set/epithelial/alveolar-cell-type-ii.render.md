## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The alveolar type II cell (AT2, "type II pneumocyte," "great alveolar cell," or "septal cell") is one of the two epithelial cell types that line the alveolus, the terminal gas-exchange sac of the lung. Unlike its neighbour the alveolar type I cell (AT1) — an extremely thin, flattened squamous cell that covers most of the alveolar surface area (~95%) purely to minimise the diffusion distance for O₂/CO₂ — the AT2 cell is small, roughly cuboidal to rounded, and sits tucked into the corners of the alveolar septa, often in small clusters of two or three. Although AT2 cells cover only about 3–7% of the alveolar surface area, they are more numerous than AT1 cells by count (their small, compact shape means many fit into the space one large flattened AT1 cell occupies). The AT2 cell has a large, central, round-to-oval, euchromatic nucleus with a prominent nucleolus, and an apical (luminal) surface studded with short, sparse microvilli — very different from the tall, dense brush border of an intestinal enterocyte. It is joined to neighbouring AT1 and AT2 cells by apical tight junctions and desmosomes that seal the air-facing epithelium.

The single most distinctive and diagnostic organelle of the AT2 cell is the **lamellar body**: a large (0.2–2.4 µm), membrane-bound, lysosome-related secretory organelle containing pulmonary surfactant tightly packed as concentric, onion-like whorls of phospholipid bilayers (mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, DPPC) together with the hydrophobic surfactant proteins SP-B and SP-C. Lamellar bodies typically make up a substantial fraction of the AT2 cytoplasm and cluster near the apical pole and around the nucleus. To build and package this surfactant the cell is metabolically very active and carries abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum (synthesising surfactant proteins SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, SP-D) and a well-developed Golgi apparatus, plus numerous mitochondria to fuel the energetically expensive lipid synthesis and constant exocytosis. On reaching the apical membrane, lamellar bodies fuse with it and release their contents by exocytosis; the extruded lamellar material unravels in the thin fluid layer lining the alveolus into a criss-crossing lattice called **tubular myelin**, which then spreads into the monomolecular surfactant film at the air-liquid interface. That film dramatically lowers surface tension, following the Young–Laplace law, which prevents the smaller alveoli from collapsing into larger ones and from collapsing completely at the end of exhalation (atelectasis) — the same phenomenon underlying infant respiratory distress syndrome when surfactant production is still immature in premature babies. Beyond surfactant secretion, the AT2 cell also plays a defensive/immune role (SP-A and SP-D are collectins that help opsonise inhaled pathogens) and, critically, acts as a **facultative stem/progenitor cell** for the alveolar epithelium: a subset of AT2 cells can self-renew and differentiate into AT1 cells to repair the epithelium after injury, a role demonstrated by lineage-tracing studies (e.g. Barkauskas et al. 2013; Desai, Brownfield & Krasnow 2014).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids or bacterial flagella** — this is a human epithelial cell, not a prokaryote.
- **No cilia** — AT2 cells are non-ciliated (unlike the ciliated cells of the bronchi/bronchioles higher up the airway); do not give it beating cilia.
- **Not thin and flattened like a type I pneumocyte** — the defining silhouette is small, cuboidal/rounded and compact, NOT a wide, paper-thin squamous sheet with long cytoplasmic extensions; that is the neighbouring, different cell type (AT1).
- **No dense, tall brush border of long microvilli** (that is an intestinal enterocyte) — AT2 microvilli are short and sparse.
- **No goblet-cell mucous granules** and **no large, pale, single mucin globules** — that is a different airway cell type.
- **Lamellar bodies must look like concentric onion-like whorls / stacked membrane lamellae**, not like tidy round clear vesicles, not like electron-lucent empty bubbles, and not scattered as tiny dots — they are one of the largest, most conspicuous organelles in the cell.
- **No biconcave-disc red-blood-cell shape** and **no large central plant-style vacuole or chloroplasts**.
- A single specimen (optionally shown snug against a sliver of the thin neighbouring type I cell membrane for context), not a dense confluent monolayer — individual morphology and lamellar bodies must stay clearly readable.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The type II alveolar cell (AT2, or "great alveolar cell") is a small, cuboidal cell tucked into the corners of the alveoli — the microscopic air sacs where oxygen enters the blood. Its defining job is manufacturing pulmonary surfactant, a lipid-and-protein film it packages inside distinctive organelles called lamellar bodies and secretes onto the alveolar surface. Surfactant dramatically lowers surface tension, which is what keeps the delicate air sacs from collapsing at the end of every exhale — a process that fails in premature babies whose lungs haven't yet matured enough to make it, causing infant respiratory distress syndrome. AT2 cells sit alongside the much thinner type I cells that do the actual job of letting gases diffuse across, and they act as the lung's built-in repair crew: after injury, AT2 cells can divide and differentiate into new type I cells, making them a key focus of current lung-regeneration research.  
🇩🇪 Die Alveolarzelle Typ II (AT2, auch "große Alveolarzelle") ist eine kleine, würfelige Zelle, die in den Ecken der Alveolen sitzt — den mikroskopisch kleinen Lungenbläschen, in denen Sauerstoff ins Blut übertritt. Ihre Hauptaufgabe ist die Herstellung von Lungensurfactant, einem Film aus Lipiden und Proteinen, den sie in speziellen Organellen namens Lamellenkörperchen verpackt und auf die Oberfläche der Lungenbläschen abgibt. Surfactant senkt die Oberflächenspannung erheblich, wodurch die empfindlichen Lungenbläschen am Ende jeder Ausatmung nicht kollabieren — ein Vorgang, der bei Frühgeborenen versagt, deren Lunge noch nicht reif genug ist, um genug davon zu bilden, was das Atemnotsyndrom des Neugeborenen verursacht. AT2-Zellen liegen neben den viel dünneren Typ-I-Zellen, die den eigentlichen Gasaustausch übernehmen, und sie fungieren als eingebautes Reparaturteam der Lunge: Nach einer Verletzung können sich AT2-Zellen teilen und zu neuen Typ-I-Zellen ausdifferenzieren, weshalb sie derzeit ein zentraler Forschungsschwerpunkt der Lungenregeneration sind.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The alveolar type II epithelial cell (AT2 pneumocyte) is a cuboidal cell occupying roughly 3–7% of the alveolar surface area but constituting the majority of alveolar epithelial cells by number, distinguished ultrastructurally by numerous lamellar bodies — lysosome-related, multivesicular organelles that store surfactant phospholipids (predominantly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) complexed with the hydrophobic surfactant proteins SP-B and SP-C, synthesised via an extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus. Upon apical exocytosis, lamellar-body contents unravel into tubular myelin and spread as a surface-active monolayer that reduces alveolar surface tension per the Young–Laplace relationship, preventing end-expiratory alveolar collapse and stabilising alveoli of differing radii against each other. AT2 cells additionally secrete the collectins SP-A and SP-D, which contribute to innate immune opsonisation of inhaled particulates and pathogens, and are joined to neighbouring AT1 and AT2 cells via apical tight and adherens junctions. Lineage-tracing studies (Barkauskas et al. 2013; Desai et al. 2014) established AT2 cells as facultative alveolar epithelial progenitors capable of both self-renewal and transdifferentiation into squamous AT1 cells during homeostatic turnover and post-injury repair, making AT2 biology central to current research on pulmonary fibrosis, ARDS and regenerative therapy.  
🇩🇪 Die alveoläre Typ-II-Epithelzelle (AT2-Pneumozyt) ist eine würfelige Zelle, die etwa 3–7 % der Alveolaroberfläche einnimmt, aber zahlenmäßig den Großteil der alveolären Epithelzellen stellt. Ultrastrukturell ist sie durch zahlreiche Lamellenkörperchen gekennzeichnet — lysosomenverwandte, multivesikuläre Organellen, die Surfactant-Phospholipide (überwiegend Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholin) zusammen mit den hydrophoben Surfactant-Proteinen SP-B und SP-C speichern, welche über ein ausgedehntes raues endoplasmatisches Retikulum und den Golgi-Apparat synthetisiert werden. Nach apikaler Exozytose entfaltet sich der Inhalt der Lamellenkörperchen zu tubulärem Myelin und breitet sich als oberflächenaktive Monoschicht aus, die gemäß der Young-Laplace-Beziehung die Oberflächenspannung der Alveolen senkt, ein endexspiratorisches Kollabieren verhindert und Alveolen unterschiedlichen Radius gegeneinander stabilisiert. AT2-Zellen sezernieren zudem die Kollectine SP-A und SP-D, die zur angeborenen Immunopsonisierung eingeatmeter Partikel und Erreger beitragen, und sind über apikale Tight- und Adhärens-Junctions mit benachbarten AT1- und AT2-Zellen verbunden. Lineage-Tracing-Studien (Barkauskas et al. 2013; Desai et al. 2014) etablierten AT2-Zellen als fakultative alveoläre Epithelvorläufer, die sowohl zur Selbsterneuerung als auch zur Transdifferenzierung in squamöse AT1-Zellen während des homöostatischen Zellumsatzes und der Reparatur nach Verletzung fähig sind, weshalb die AT2-Biologie ein zentraler Forschungsschwerpunkt bei Lungenfibrose, ARDS und regenerativer Therapie ist.
