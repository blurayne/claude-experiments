# Chlamydia felis (feline conjunctivitis) — render log

**Set:** `pet-pathogens` · **Microbe key:** `chlamydia-felis`
**Short description:** An obligate intracellular bacterium with no free-living form at all: it exists only as a tiny, tough, infectious elementary body (EB, ~0.2-0.3 µm) or as a larger, fragile, dividing reticulate body (RB, ~0.5-1 µm) growing inside a host-cell inclusion. The two-form cycle IS the subject — no other microbe in this atlas has this shape. It attacks the conjunctiva above all, causing weeping, gummed-up eyes; it is NOT the same species as *C. trachomatis* (the human STI/trachoma agent) even though it shares the same genus-level biology.

## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The subject is ***Chlamydia felis***, an **obligate intracellular bacterium** — a real bacterium (its own DNA, ribosomes, cell envelope), not a virus, and one that cannot live or divide outside a host cell at all. Its entire biology is organised around a **biphasic developmental cycle** with two structurally distinct forms, and rendering that cycle correctly — not a generic "germ" — is the entire point of this subject.

**A note on genus vs. species**, because it matters for accuracy: the developmental cycle described below (EB ↔ RB, the inclusion, binary fission, the unusual cell envelope) is **genus-level biology** shared by every *Chlamydia*/*Chlamydophila* species — *trachomatis*, *psittaci*, *pneumoniae*, *felis*, and others — and almost everything citable and photographable about it comes from *C. trachomatis*, by far the best-studied species (it is the world's most common bacterial STI in humans). *C. felis* is a **different species with a different disease**: it is not sexually transmitted, it does not cause human trachoma, and it is a cat pathogen whose defining clinical picture is conjunctivitis, not a genital infection. Every fact below is marked genus-level or *C. felis*-specific so the two are not conflated.

**The two forms (the core teaching point).**
- The **elementary body (EB)** is small (~0.2–0.3 µm — NCBI Bookshelf's *Medical Microbiology* gives 0.25–0.3 µm; a PLOS ONE developmental-cycle study gives ~0.3 µm), round, dense under the electron microscope, and metabolically almost dormant. Its envelope is unusually rigid for something so small: not a classical peptidoglycan wall, but a **cysteine-rich, disulfide-cross-linked outer-membrane protein complex** (the "chlamydial anomaly" — chlamydiae are Gram-negative-like, with inner and outer membranes and LPS, but genuinely lack a substantial peptidoglycan layer). That rigid envelope is what lets the EB survive outside a cell; this is the form that transmits infection and is comparatively insensitive to antibiotics because it is barely metabolising.
- The **reticulate body (RB)** is larger (~0.5–1 µm — NCBI Bookshelf gives 0.5–0.6 µm; other sources describe growth up to 1–1.5 µm before division), round, paler/more open-textured under the electron microscope (its envelope is comparatively fragile and lipid-based), and metabolically active. It **cannot infect a new cell and cannot survive outside one** — all it does is divide. It is the RB, not the EB, that antibiotics such as doxycycline actually act on.
- **Getting the size/density relationship backwards is the single most likely and most damaging rendering error**: the EB must always read as the *smaller, darker, denser* body, and the RB as the *larger, paler* body. This must be checkable in every accepted render.

**The cycle (genus-level).** An EB attaches to a susceptible host epithelial cell and is taken up into a membrane-bound compartment called the **inclusion**. Inside the inclusion, the EB converts into an RB (over roughly the first 8–20 hours); RBs then divide repeatedly by **binary fission**, filling and expanding the inclusion — an inclusion can grow large enough to visibly **push the host cell's own nucleus aside**, a genuinely observable and checkable feature. From roughly 20–48 hours the RBs begin re-condensing, asynchronously, back into EBs; the host cell eventually lyses (NCBI Bookshelf gives an overall 36–50 h cycle; veterinary sources describe *C. felis* specifically taking about 48–72 h), releasing infectious EBs that go on to infect neighbouring cells. At no point in this cycle does a free-living chlamydial cell exist and divide outside a host cell — that is biologically impossible for this organism, and the most important "do NOT draw."

**No motility structures.** Neither EB nor RB has flagella, cilia, or any other appendage — chlamydiae are completely nonmotile at every stage (NCBI Bookshelf).

**A scale note for cytology vs. ultrastructure.** On everyday diagnostic light microscopy (a Giemsa/Romanowsky-stained conjunctival scrape), the *whole inclusion* appears as a single basophilic (purple) clump roughly 3–30 µm across, typically capping or displacing the nucleus (eClinpath, Cornell). That is a different, much larger scale than the individual 0.2–1 µm EB/RB particles resolvable only by electron microscopy — useful as a composition cue (a swollen inclusion visibly deforming the cell) but not to be confused with the EB/RB sizes themselves.

***C. felis*-specific biology.** It has a near-exclusive tropism for the **conjunctival epithelium** of cats (with occasional recovery from the upper respiratory and, less often, GI/reproductive mucosa), producing the classic clinical picture of serous-to-mucopurulent, gummy ocular discharge with conjunctival hyperaemia and chemosis — most often in kittens and young cats (2 months–1 year). It is transmitted by **direct, close contact** via eye and nose secretions; it survives poorly outside a host, so environmental/fomite spread is secondary — which is exactly why it concentrates wherever cats live in groups (shelters, catteries). First-line treatment is systemic **doxycycline** (~10 mg/kg PO q24h, at least 4 weeks, Merck Veterinary Manual); a vaccine exists and reduces severity/shedding without fully preventing infection. **Zoonotic transmission to people is possible but rare and mild** — documented cases are follicular conjunctivitis in people with close cat contact, sometimes immunocompromised (PMC, 2024) — and, unlike *C. trachomatis* in humans, this is never a sexually transmitted picture in either species.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **A free-living bacterium dividing OUTSIDE a host cell** — chlamydiae cannot do this at any stage; every dividing RB must be inside the inclusion, inside a cell.
- **Flagella, cilia, or any whip-like/hair-like appendage** on either form — chlamydiae are completely nonmotile.
- **A virus capsid or icosahedral/geometric-faceted shell** — this is a bacterium, not a virus; do not give the EB a geometric viral look.
- **A thick, classically Gram-positive-style peptidoglycan wall** on either form — the real envelope lacks a substantial peptidoglycan layer; render it as a distinct but comparatively thin, taut boundary, especially on the EB.
- **The RB drawn smaller or denser than the EB, or the two forms the same size** — the checkable core of this subject: EB = smaller & denser/darker; RB = larger & paler.
- **EBs or RBs floating loose in the host-cell cytoplasm outside the inclusion membrane** — both live inside the inclusion once the cycle has begun.
- **A cat, a person, or an eye** in any of the four science-style renders (sem, textbook, 3d, watercolor) — the subject here is the cellular/ultrastructural cycle, not the whole animal or a clinical eye photo; a cat character belongs only on the coloring page, shown well and being cared for.
- **Blood, gore, pus, or a distressed/sick-looking animal** — a teaching subject for a children's atlas; the coloring page is warm and reassuring, never showing the infection itself.
- **The human-STI framing (genital tropism, sexual transmission)** anywhere in the *C. felis* text — that belongs to *C. trachomatis* in humans and must not bleed into the cat pathogen or the rare human zoonotic cases (which are ocular, from close animal contact, never sexual).

**Kids (GiantMicrobes-style).**

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 Chlamydia felis is an obligate intracellular bacterium — it cannot replicate, or even survive for long, outside a living cell, which shapes everything about how it spreads and how it's treated. It settles almost exclusively in the conjunctiva, the thin membrane lining a cat's eyelids, producing the watery-then-gummy discharge and reddened, swollen eyes typical of feline chlamydial conjunctivitis, most often in kittens and young cats. Because the bacterium survives poorly outside a host, it needs close, direct contact to pass between cats — via eye and nose secretions — which is why the places that see it most are shelters, catteries and other settings where cats live in groups rather than the occasional single-cat household. The practical picture is reassuring: a several-week course of an oral antibiotic (doxycycline is standard) clears an active infection well, and a vaccine is available that, while not fully preventive, reduces how severely a cat is affected and how much it sheds the bacterium to others. Transmission to people does happen on rare occasions — almost always through close contact with an infected cat's eye discharge — and when it does, it typically causes a mild, self-limiting conjunctivitis, not a serious illness.  
🇩🇪 Chlamydia felis ist ein obligat intrazelluläres Bakterium — es kann sich außerhalb einer lebenden Zelle weder vermehren noch lange überleben, und das prägt alles daran, wie es sich verbreitet und wie es behandelt wird. Es setzt sich fast ausschließlich in der Bindehaut fest, der dünnen Membran, die die Augenlider einer Katze auskleidet, und verursacht den zunächst wässrigen, später verklebten Ausfluss sowie gerötete, geschwollene Augen, die für die feline Chlamydien-Konjunktivitis typisch sind — am häufigsten bei Kätzchen und jungen Katzen. Weil das Bakterium außerhalb eines Wirts nur schlecht überlebt, braucht es engen, direkten Kontakt, um von Katze zu Katze überzugehen — über Augen- und Nasensekret —, weshalb es vor allem dort auftritt, wo viele Katzen zusammenleben: in Tierheimen, Zuchten und ähnlichen Gruppenhaltungen, seltener im Ein-Katzen-Haushalt. Das praktische Bild ist beruhigend: Eine mehrwöchige Antibiotika-Kur zum Einnehmen (Doxycyclin ist Standard) heilt eine aktive Infektion gut aus, und es gibt eine Impfung, die zwar nicht vollständig vorbeugt, aber den Krankheitsverlauf abmildert und die Ausscheidung des Erregers verringert. Eine Übertragung auf Menschen kommt gelegentlich vor — fast immer durch engen Kontakt mit dem Augensekret einer infizierten Katze — und verläuft dann meist als leichte, von selbst abklingende Bindehautentzündung, nicht als ernsthafte Erkrankung.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 Chlamydia felis is a Gram-negative-like, obligate intracellular bacterium of the family Chlamydiaceae, sharing the genus-wide biphasic developmental cycle: an infectious, metabolically quiescent elementary body (EB, ~0.2-0.3 µm), stabilised by a cysteine-rich, disulfide-cross-linked outer-membrane protein complex rather than a canonical peptidoglycan sacculus, attaches to and is endocytosed by a susceptible epithelial cell; within a membrane-bound inclusion the EB differentiates into a larger, metabolically active reticulate body (RB, ~0.5-1 µm) that replicates by binary fission, before asynchronous redifferentiation back to EBs and release by host-cell lysis, completing the cycle in roughly 48-72 hours for this species. Neither developmental form is motile. C. felis shows marked tropism for conjunctival (and, secondarily, upper respiratory) epithelium in Felis catus, producing follicular conjunctivitis with serous-to-mucopurulent discharge, chemosis and hyperaemia, predominantly in cats aged 2 months to 1 year; transmission is via direct contact with ocular/nasal secretions, reflecting the organism's poor environmental survival, which concentrates prevalence in multi-cat settings such as shelters and catteries. First-line therapy is systemic doxycycline (~10 mg/kg PO q24h for a minimum of 4 weeks), targeting the metabolically active, replicating RB stage rather than the comparatively antibiotic-refractory EB; a commercially available vaccine attenuates clinical severity and shedding without conferring sterilising immunity. Zoonotic transmission is documented but rare, typically presenting as mild, self-limiting follicular conjunctivitis in individuals with close feline contact, occasionally in immunocompromised hosts. C. felis is taxonomically and clinically distinct from C. trachomatis, the sexually transmitted, genital- and ocular (trachoma)-tropic human pathogen; the two species share cell-biological mechanism, not epidemiology or disease presentation.  
🇩🇪 Chlamydia felis ist ein Gram-negativ-ähnliches, obligat intrazelluläres Bakterium der Familie Chlamydiaceae und teilt den gattungsweiten biphasischen Entwicklungszyklus: Ein infektiöses, metabolisch nahezu inaktives Elementarkörperchen (EB, ca. 0,2-0,3 µm), dessen Stabilität nicht auf einem klassischen Peptidoglykan-Sacculus, sondern auf einem cysteinreichen, durch Disulfidbrücken vernetzten Außenmembran-Proteinkomplex beruht, heftet sich an eine empfängliche Epithelzelle an und wird endozytiert; innerhalb einer membranumschlossenen Einschlusskörperchen (Inclusion) differenziert sich das EB zu einem größeren, metabolisch aktiven Retikularkörperchen (RB, ca. 0,5-1 µm), das sich durch Zweiteilung vermehrt, bevor es sich asynchron wieder zu EB umwandelt und durch Lyse der Wirtszelle freigesetzt wird — bei dieser Spezies dauert der gesamte Zyklus etwa 48-72 Stunden. Keine der beiden Entwicklungsformen ist beweglich. C. felis zeigt einen ausgeprägten Tropismus für das Bindehautepithel (und sekundär das obere respiratorische Epithel) von Felis catus und verursacht eine follikuläre Konjunktivitis mit zunächst serösem, später mukopurulentem Ausfluss, Chemosis und Hyperämie, vor allem bei Katzen zwischen 2 Monaten und 1 Jahr; die Übertragung erfolgt über direkten Kontakt mit Augen- und Nasensekret, was das schlechte Überleben des Erregers in der Umwelt widerspiegelt und die Prävalenz in Mehrkatzenhaltungen wie Tierheimen und Zuchten konzentriert. Mittel der ersten Wahl ist systemisches Doxycyclin (ca. 10 mg/kg p.o. alle 24 Stunden über mindestens 4 Wochen), das die metabolisch aktive, sich teilende RB-Stufe angreift und nicht das vergleichsweise antibiotikaresistente EB; eine verfügbare Impfung mildert den klinischen Verlauf und die Erregerausscheidung, ohne eine sterile Immunität zu erzeugen. Eine zoonotische Übertragung ist dokumentiert, aber selten und verläuft meist als milde, selbstlimitierende follikuläre Konjunktivitis bei Personen mit engem Katzenkontakt, gelegentlich bei immungeschwächten Personen. C. felis ist taxonomisch und klinisch klar von C. trachomatis abzugrenzen, dem sexuell übertragenen, genital- und okulär- (Trachom-)tropischen Humanpathogen; beide Spezies teilen den zellbiologischen Mechanismus, nicht aber Epidemiologie oder Krankheitsbild.
