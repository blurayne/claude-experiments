## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The subject is an **adult *Dirofilaria immitis*** (heartworm), a mosquito-borne **filarial nematode** — a roundworm (phylum Nematoda), an *animal*, not a bacterium, virus or flatworm. It is the largest subject in the atlas by a wide margin: an adult female reaches **25-30 cm** (CDC DPDx gives 230-310 mm), the male a smaller **15-18 cm** (CDC DPDx gives 120-190 mm). The teaching point is precisely the confusion the common image invites: nematodes have a **smooth, continuous, unsegmented cuticle** — there is nothing like an earthworm's external rings, and nothing like a tapeworm's proglottids, suckers or scolex. Getting this wrong (drawing visible cross-ribs/rungs on the body) was the dominant failure mode during rendering and took two extra attempts to eliminate (see §5).

**Body plan.** The cuticle is collagenous and smooth, with only fine longitudinal (lengthwise) striations — never crosswise rings. *D. immitis* additionally has prominent muscular lateral cords that make the cuticle bulge subtly inward over them (a "bat-wing" cross-section), a fine diagnostic detail, not a segmentation pattern. The **anterior end** is blunt/tapered with a small, simple, **unarmed mouth** opening straight into a muscular (filariform) oesophagus — no jaws, teeth, hooks or suckers of any kind (those belong to tapeworms/flukes/hookworms). The worm is off-white to cream and translucent — the real colour of the living animal, not a false-colour convention.

**Sexual dimorphism.** The clearest sex marker is the **posterior end**: the female's tail is straight and simple; the male's tail is characteristically **coiled into a tight spiral**, used to grip the female during mating (with copulatory spicules extended from the coiled end). Females are also markedly longer and, being gravid, show a paired coiled reproductive tract (ovaries/uteri) filling much of the internal volume — a strong textbook cutaway element.

**Location and life cycle.** Adults live coiled together, loosely tangled, in the **pulmonary arteries and, in heavier infections, the right ventricle** of the heart — never the left heart, never a systemic artery. Transmission is exclusively via mosquitoes: a mosquito bites an infected animal and ingests circulating **microfilariae** (first-stage larvae, ~290-330 × 6-7 µm, unsheathed, tapered anterior/pointed tail); these develop through two moults to the infective third stage (L3) inside the mosquito over roughly two weeks; the next bite deposits L3 larvae, which migrate through subcutaneous and muscle tissue, moulting to L4 and then to immature adults, entering the venous circulation and reaching the pulmonary arteries, where they mature (~6-7 months post-infection) and mated females release new microfilariae, completing the cycle. Direct animal-to-animal transmission does not occur.

Sources: [CDC DPDx — Dirofilariasis](https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/dirofilariasis/index.html) (adult size, life-cycle stages, adult lifespan 5-10 years), [Stanford Parasites-in-Humans — D. immitis morphology](https://web.stanford.edu/group/parasites/ParaSites2006/Dirofilariasis/Morphology.htm) (cuticle/lateral cords, coiled male tail, spicules/gubernaculum), [ScienceDirect — D. immitis overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/immunology-and-microbiology/dirofilaria-immitis), [Western College of Veterinary Medicine — Learn About Parasites: D. immitis](https://wcvm.usask.ca/learnaboutparasites/parasites/dirofilaria-immitis.php) (microfilaria size/morphology, life cycle), [CDC PHIL #21538 — D. immitis microfilaria photomicrograph](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/phil/Details.aspx?pid=21538).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **A segmented body with visible external rings/grooves** like an earthworm (annelid) — nematode cuticle is smooth and continuous. This was the dominant failure across three render attempts (see §5) and the single most likely and most checkable error.
- **Proglottids, a scolex, or suckers/hooks on the head** — tapeworm (cestode) features that do not belong on a nematode.
- **Jaws, teeth, fangs, or a biting mouth** — the mouth is a simple, small, unarmed opening.
- **Legs, fins, or any appendages** — nematodes have none.
- **A leech-like flattened, suckered body.**
- **The worm shown in the LEFT side of the heart** or in a systemic (non-pulmonary) artery — it lives specifically in the right ventricle and pulmonary arteries.
- **A straight, uncoiled male tail** — the coiled/spiral posterior is a defining, checkable male feature.
- **Blood, gore, open-wound or graphic surgical imagery** — a veterinary teaching subject for an atlas used by children.
- **A sick-looking dog or cat as the main subject** — depict the worm/mosquito/prevention routine, not disease symptoms on the animal.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 Heartworm is transmitted exclusively by mosquitoes: a mosquito picks up microscopic first-stage larvae (microfilariae) from an infected dog's blood, the larvae mature inside the mosquito over about two weeks, and its next bite deposits infective larvae that migrate through tissue and, months later, mature into foot-long adult worms living in the right side of the heart and the pulmonary arteries. Dogs are the natural host and carry the heaviest worm burdens; cats are infected less often because they're a poor host for the parasite, but a cat that does get infected tends to have a more severe, harder-to-diagnose illness for the worm numbers involved. The practical upshot is asymmetric: monthly preventative medication is cheap, safe and essentially completely effective at stopping the larvae before they mature, while treating an already-established adult infection is a slow, multi-month protocol with real risk (dead worms breaking loose into the pulmonary circulation), so prevention is the clear standard of care wherever the parasite is present. In much of Germany and northern Europe, risk has historically been low, but rising temperatures and travel with pets to the Mediterranean have made pre-travel or seasonal prophylaxis a routine vet recommendation.  
🇩🇪 Der Herzwurm wird ausschließlich von Stechmücken übertragen: Eine Mücke nimmt mikroskopisch kleine Larven (Mikrofilarien) aus dem Blut eines infizierten Hundes auf, die Larven reifen etwa zwei Wochen lang in der Mücke heran, und beim nächsten Stich gibt sie infektionsfähige Larven weiter, die durchs Gewebe wandern und Monate später zu fast 30 Zentimeter langen erwachsenen Würmern in der rechten Herzkammer und den Lungenarterien heranwachsen. **Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 Dirofilaria immitis (Spirurida: Onchocercidae) is a mosquito-borne filarial nematode and the agent of canine (and less commonly feline) dirofilariasis. Adults show marked sexual dimorphism (females 230-310 mm, males 120-190 mm; CDC DPDx) with a smooth, unsegmented cuticle bearing prominent lateral cords, a simple unarmed anterior mouth opening into a filariform esophagus, and a characteristically coiled male posterior bearing copulatory spicules. Mated adults reside in the pulmonary arteries and, in heavier infections, the right ventricle, where females release unsheathed first-stage larvae (microfilariae, ~290-330 x 6-7 µm) into peripheral circulation. Mosquitoes (multiple Culicidae genera) ingest microfilariae during a blood meal; these develop through two molts to the infective third stage (L3) over roughly two weeks at ambient temperature, are deposited at the next bite, and migrate through subcutaneous and muscle tissue, molting to L4 and then to immature adults (L5) before entering the venous circulation and lodging in the pulmonary vasculature, reaching sexual maturity approximately 6-7 months post-infection. Dogs are a fully permissive definitive host supporting high worm burdens and patent microfilaremia; cats are comparatively resistant, typically host fewer, often single-sex (non-patent) infections, but show disproportionate pathology — heartworm-associated respiratory disease — for the worm number involved, complicating antigen-based diagnosis. Chemoprophylaxis (macrocyclic lactones, e.g. ivermectin, milbemycin) interrupts the L3/L4 migratory stages with high efficacy and low risk; adulticidal therapy (melarsomine) targets mature worms directly and carries a risk of thromboembolic pulmonary complications from dying worm fragments, which is why staged treatment protocols and strict activity restriction are used.  
🇩🇪 Dirofilaria immitis (Spirurida: Onchocercidae) ist ein durch Stechmücken übertragener Filarien-Nematode und Erreger der caninen (seltener felinen) Dirofilariose. Adulte Tiere zeigen einen deutlichen Geschlechtsdimorphismus (Weibchen 230-310 mm, Männchen 120-190 mm; CDC DPDx) mit glatter, ungegliederter Kutikula mit ausgeprägten Lateralwülsten, einem einfachen, unbewaffneten Mund am Vorderende, der in einen filariformen Ösophagus übergeht, sowie einem charakteristisch eingerollten männlichen Hinterende mit Kopulationsspikula. Begattete Adulte siedeln in den Lungenarterien und, bei stärkerem Befall, in der rechten Herzkammer, wo die Weibchen unbescheidete Larven im ersten Stadium (Mikrofilarien, ca. 290-330 x 6-7 µm) in die periphere Blutbahn abgeben. Stechmücken (mehrere Culicidae-Gattungen) nehmen die Mikrofilarien bei einer Blutmahlzeit auf; diese entwickeln sich über zwei Häutungen binnen etwa zwei Wochen bei Umgebungstemperatur zum infektionsfähigen dritten Larvenstadium (L3), werden beim nächsten Stich abgesetzt und wandern durch Unterhaut- und Muskelgewebe, häuten sich zu L4 und dann zu unreifen Adulten (L5), bevor sie in die venöse Zirkulation gelangen und sich in der Lungenstrombahn ansiedeln; die Geschlechtsreife wird etwa 6-7 Monate nach Infektion erreicht. Hunde sind ein voll permissiver Endwirt mit hoher Wurmlast und patenter Mikrofilarämie; Katzen sind vergleichsweise resistent, beherbergen meist wenige, oft eingeschlechtliche (nicht-patente) Infektionen, zeigen jedoch eine unverhältnismäßig starke Pathologie (herzwurmassoziierte Atemwegserkrankung) im Verhältnis zur Wurmzahl, was die antigenbasierte Diagnostik erschwert. Die Chemoprophylaxe (makrozyklische Laktone, z. B. Ivermectin, Milbemycin) unterbricht die wandernden L3/L4-Stadien mit hoher Wirksamkeit und geringem Risiko; die adultizide Therapie (Melarsomin) zielt direkt auf die geschlechtsreifen Würmer und birgt das Risiko thromboembolischer Lungenkomplikationen durch absterbende Wurmfragmente, weshalb gestufte Behandlungsprotokolle und strikte Bewegungseinschränkung angewendet werden.
