## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

A cancer cell is not a foreign organism but the body's own cell that has accumulated enough mutations in growth-regulating genes (oncogenes activated, tumour-suppressor genes disabled) to escape the normal controls on division, repair and programmed death — the "hallmarks of cancer" (Hanahan & Weinberg): self-sufficient growth signalling, insensitivity to anti-growth signals, apoptosis evasion, replicative immortality, sustained angiogenesis, invasion/metastasis, plus the later-added reprogrammed metabolism, genome instability, tumour-promoting inflammation and immune evasion. None of that is visible directly under a microscope; what a pathologist or cytologist actually looks for are the cytological hallmarks of malignancy, a well-established checklist used every day in histopathology and cytopathology to flag a cell as atypical or frankly malignant:

- **Nuclear enlargement and irregularity.** The nucleus is disproportionately large and its outline is irregular — notched, indented or lobulated — rather than the smooth oval/round contour of a normal nucleus.
- **Thickened, irregular nuclear membrane.** The nuclear envelope itself looks coarser and unevenly thickened, often with peripheral chromatin margination.
- **Raised nucleus-to-cytoplasm (N:C) ratio.** Normal cells keep a fairly small, consistent fraction of their cross-section as nucleus; in malignant cells the nucleus can occupy well over half the cell (ratios above ~0.5–0.7 are used as a threshold in several grading systems), leaving only a thin rim of cytoplasm.
- **Coarse, clumped ("grumous") chromatin and hyperchromasia.** Chromatin condenses into irregular clumps rather than the fine, even texture of a resting nucleus, and stains darker (hyperchromatic) because of the increased, disorganised DNA content.
- **Prominent, often multiple nucleoli.** Nucleoli enlarge and can become irregular in shape (angular, stellate) rather than the small round nucleolus of a normal cell — a sign of the cell's runaway ribosome/protein-synthesis demand.
- **Pleomorphism.** Neighbouring tumour cells vary markedly from each other in size and shape (anisocytosis/anisokaryosis) — unlike a population of normal cells of one type, which look near-identical to each other.
- **Atypical mitotic figures.** Cell division is frequent and the mitotic spindle itself is often abnormal — tripolar or multipolar spindles, rather than the clean bipolar spindle of normal mitosis — reflecting the underlying genome instability.
- **Irregular, sometimes lobed cell outline** and a disorganised cytoskeleton, in contrast to the more regular outline of the healthy cell type it arose from.
- **Ordinary organelles are still present.** Mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes, Golgi and a cytoskeleton are all still there — it is a malfunctioning human cell, not an invader, and should read as recognisably human even though its nucleus has gone wrong.

The teaching point of this set is exactly that origin: a cancer cell carries almost the same "ID badges" as a healthy cell of the same tissue, which is precisely what makes it hard for the immune system (and for treatment) to tell apart from self. It is hunted by natural killer cells and cytotoxic T cells for as long as it still displays visibly abnormal or stress-induced surface markers; evading that surveillance is one of the hallmarks tumours evolve toward. Because "cancer cell" is a generic, cross-tissue teaching figure rather than one specific cancer type, this set renders a generic, moderately atypical carcinoma-type cell (the commonest broad category) showing the checklist above, shown next to one or two normal-looking neighbouring cells so the contrast in nuclear size/shape carries the lesson.

Sources: [Pleomorphism (cytology) — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleomorphism_(cytology)), [Anaplasia — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaplasia), [Nuclear atypia — ScienceDirect Topics overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/nuclear-atypia), [Hanahan & Weinberg, "Hallmarks of Cancer" — Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallmarks_of_Cancer), [NCI Dictionary of Cancer Terms — "anaplasia"](https://www.cancer.gov/publications/dictionaries/cancer-terms/def/anaplasia).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **No bacterial cell wall, nucleoid, flagellum or capsid of any kind** — this is a human body cell, not a microbe or virus.
- **No spikes, tentacles, claws or any alien/monster appendage.**
- **No menacing face, teeth, eyes or an "evil"/aggressive appearance** of any kind.
- **No blood, gore, wounds or a sick-looking person/body context.**
- **Do not frame it as an invader arriving from outside the body** — it must read as a cell that belongs to the body and has gone wrong internally, not something that attacked from outside.
- **Not a normal-looking, small, round, uniform nucleus** — this would defeat the entire teaching point; the nucleus must visibly dominate the cell and look irregular.
- **Not multinucleated / not a giant fused cell** (that is a different cell type, e.g. an osteoclast) — a single malignant nucleus (or one caught mid atypical-mitosis) per cell.
- **No tumour mass, lump or crowd of dozens of identical cells** — one malignant cell (optionally with one or two normal neighbours for scale/shape contrast) is the teaching subject, not a tissue-level mass.
- **Do not omit ordinary organelles** — mitochondria, ER, ribosomes, cytoskeleton must still be visible where the medium/style would show them; it is a malformed human cell, not an empty or alien shape.

## 3. Audience descriptions (EN + DE)

**Kids (GiantMicrobes-style).**  
🇬🇧 Meet the Cancer cell — one of your own body's cells that has forgotten the rules! Every cell in you normally follows a simple plan: grow when you need it, stop when there's enough, and make room for its neighbours. This cell picked up some mistakes (called mutations) in the instructions it carries, and it stopped listening — it just keeps dividing and dividing, and it stopped making room. Its control room, the nucleus, has grown big and lumpy, which is one of the clues doctors look for under a microscope. The good news: your body has patrol cells that are always on the lookout for cells acting strangely, and if a cell like this is found, doctors today have many different ways to help — surgery, medicines, radiation or treatments that boost the patrol cells — so there's a real plan for dealing with it, not just one single trick.  
🇩🇪 Das ist die Krebszelle — eine Zelle aus deinem eigenen Körper, die die Regeln vergessen hat! Normalerweise hält sich jede Zelle in dir an einen einfachen Plan: wachsen, wenn es gebraucht wird, aufhören, wenn genug da ist, und den Nachbarn Platz machen. Diese Zelle hat ein paar Fehler (Mutationen genannt) in ihrer Bauanleitung gesammelt und hört nicht mehr zu — sie teilt sich einfach immer weiter und macht keinen Platz mehr. Ihre Kommandozentrale, der Zellkern, ist groß und klumpig geworden, und genau das ist einer der Hinweise, nach denen Ärztinnen und Ärzte unter dem Mikroskop suchen. Die gute Nachricht: Dein Körper hat Wächterzellen, die ständig nach seltsam handelnden Zellen Ausschau halten, und wenn so eine Zelle gefunden wird, gibt es heute viele verschiedene Wege zu helfen — Operation, Medikamente, Bestrahlung oder Behandlungen, die die Wächterzellen stärken. Es gibt also einen echten Plan dafür, nicht nur einen einzigen Trick.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 A cancer cell is not an invader — it is one of the body's own cells that has accumulated enough genetic damage to escape the normal controls on growth and division. Under a microscope, that shows up as a set of recognisable cytological changes: an enlarged, irregularly-shaped nucleus with coarse chromatin and prominent nucleoli, a nucleus that takes up an unusually large share of the cell, an irregular outline, and cell divisions that look abnormal. Because the cell still carries most of the same surface markers as its healthy neighbours, the immune system — chiefly natural killer cells and cytotoxic T cells — can only catch it while it still looks sufficiently abnormal; tumours that manage to blend in better are the hardest to clear on their own. That's why modern treatment rarely relies on one single approach: depending on the type and stage, it combines surgery, radiotherapy, drug therapy and immunotherapy, often aimed specifically at helping the immune system recognise and respond to these cells again.  
🇩🇪 Eine Krebszelle ist kein Eindringling — sie ist eine körpereigene Zelle, die genug genetische Schäden angehäuft hat, um den normalen Kontrollen für Wachstum und Teilung zu entkommen. Unter dem Mikroskop zeigt sich das an einer Reihe erkennbarer Veränderungen: ein vergrößerter, unregelmäßig geformter Zellkern mit grobem Chromatin und auffälligen Nukleolen, ein Zellkern, der einen ungewöhnlich großen Anteil der Zelle einnimmt, ein unregelmäßiger Umriss und Zellteilungen, die abnormal aussehen. Da die Zelle noch die meisten Oberflächenmerkmale ihrer gesunden Nachbarn trägt, kann das Immunsystem — vor allem natürliche Killerzellen und zytotoxische T-Zellen — sie nur erkennen, solange sie noch ausreichend auffällig wirkt; Tumoren, denen es gelingt, sich besser zu tarnen, sind am schwersten von selbst zu beseitigen. Deshalb verlässt sich die moderne Behandlung selten auf einen einzigen Ansatz: Je nach Art und Stadium kombiniert sie Operation, Bestrahlung, medikamentöse Therapie und Immuntherapie, oft gezielt darauf ausgerichtet, dem Immunsystem zu helfen, diese Zellen wieder zu erkennen und zu bekämpfen.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 A malignant cell arises from a normal somatic cell through the sequential accumulation of driver mutations that activate oncogenes and inactivate tumour-suppressor genes, producing the hallmarks of cancer (Hanahan & Weinberg): self-sufficient growth signalling, insensitivity to anti-growth signals, apoptosis evasion, replicative immortality, sustained angiogenesis, invasion/metastasis, reprogrammed energy metabolism, genome instability, tumour-promoting inflammation and immune evasion. These are reflected cytologically in the classic criteria of malignancy used diagnostically: nuclear enlargement with an irregular, often notched or thickened membrane; coarse, hyperchromatic, clumped chromatin; enlarged and sometimes multiple/angulated nucleoli; a markedly raised nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio; intragroup pleomorphism (anisocytosis/anisokaryosis) between neighbouring tumour cells; and atypical (e.g. tripolar/multipolar) mitotic figures reflecting chromosomal instability. Immune surveillance by NK cells (recognising stress ligands and reduced MHC class I) and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (recognising tumour-associated/neoantigens via MHC class I) exerts selective pressure toward immunoediting, whereby immunogenic clones are eliminated and poorly immunogenic or actively immunosuppressive clones (e.g. via PD-L1 upregulation, MHC downregulation) predominate — the rationale behind checkpoint-inhibitor immunotherapy, alongside surgery, radiotherapy and cytotoxic/targeted drug therapy as the principal treatment modalities.  
🇩🇪 Eine maligne Zelle entsteht aus einer normalen somatischen Zelle durch die sukzessive Anhäufung von Treibermutationen, die Onkogene aktivieren und Tumorsuppressorgene inaktivieren, wodurch die Hallmarks of Cancer (Hanahan & Weinberg) entstehen: autarke Wachstumssignale, Unempfindlichkeit gegenüber wachstumshemmenden Signalen, Apoptoseresistenz, replikative Unsterblichkeit, anhaltende Angiogenese, Invasion/Metastasierung, reprogrammierter Energiestoffwechsel, Genominstabilität, tumorfördernde Entzündung und Immunevasion. Zytologisch spiegeln sich diese in den klassischen diagnostischen Malignitätskriterien wider: Kernvergrößerung mit unregelmäßiger, oft eingekerbter oder verdickter Kernmembran; grobes, hyperchromatisches, verklumptes Chromatin; vergrößerte, teils multiple bzw. eckige Nukleolen; ein deutlich erhöhtes Kern-Plasma-Verhältnis; intragruppärer Pleomorphismus (Anisozytose/Anisokaryose) zwischen benachbarten Tumorzellen; sowie atypische (z. B. tripolare/multipolare) Mitosefiguren als Ausdruck chromosomaler Instabilität. Die Immunüberwachung durch NK-Zellen (Erkennung von Stressliganden und reduziertem MHC-Klasse-I) und zytotoxische CD8+-T-Zellen (Erkennung tumorassoziierter Antigene/Neoantigene über MHC-Klasse-I) übt einen Selektionsdruck im Sinne des Immunoediting aus, wodurch immunogene Klone eliminiert werden und schlecht immunogene oder aktiv immunsuppressive Klone (z. B. über PD-L1-Hochregulation, MHC-Herunterregulation) dominieren — die Grundlage der Checkpoint-Inhibitor-Immuntherapie neben Operation, Bestrahlung sowie zytotoxischer/zielgerichteter medikamentöser Therapie als den wichtigsten Behandlungsmodalitäten.
