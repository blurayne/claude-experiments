## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The neuron is the principal signalling cell of the nervous system, structurally polarised into four compartments: **dendrites**, the **soma** (cell body / perikaryon), the **axon**, and **presynaptic axon terminals**. Multiple thin, profusely branching dendrites radiate from the soma; many carry small bumps called **dendritic spines**, the postsynaptic sites of most excitatory synapses. Each dendrite receives graded postsynaptic potentials from upstream neurons, and these signals passively spread and sum together as they travel toward the soma ("integrate-and-fire"). The soma houses a large, round, euchromatic **nucleus** with a prominent central **nucleolus**, plus the cell's main biosynthetic machinery: coarse clumps of rough endoplasmic reticulum studded with free ribosomes, classically stained as basophilic **Nissl bodies** (Nissl substance) under cresyl-violet/toluidine-blue stains, together with a well-developed **Golgi apparatus** and numerous **mitochondria**. If the summed input crosses threshold, an action potential is triggered at the **axon hillock** — a cone-shaped, Nissl-free zone with the highest density of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in the cell — and then it propagates along the single **axon**. In many axons, especially in the peripheral and central white matter, the axon is wrapped in segments of **myelin** (an insulating multilayer membrane sheath laid down by oligodendrocytes in the CNS or Schwann cells in the PNS), interrupted by short bare stretches called **Nodes of Ranvier** where the axolemma is exposed and channel-dense; this arrangement lets the impulse "jump" node to node (saltatory conduction), greatly increasing speed while lowering energy cost. Neurofilaments (a neuron-specific class of intermediate filament) and microtubules make up the axonal and dendritic **cytoskeleton**, giving the elongated processes their shape and serving as tracks for fast/slow axonal transport (kinesins moving cargo outward, dyneins inward) — essential because ribosomes are largely excluded from the axon, so axonal and presynaptic proteins must be made in the soma and shipped out. At its far end the axon arborises into fine branches ending in **axon terminals (synaptic boutons)**, each packed with **synaptic vesicles** loaded with neurotransmitter; an arriving impulse triggers Ca²⁺-dependent vesicle fusion at the presynaptic membrane, releasing transmitter into the synaptic cleft onto the next cell. The whole surface is bounded by the **plasma membrane** (called the axolemma along the axon), whose voltage-gated ion channels generate and propagate the electrical signal. Neurons are never isolated: astrocytes regulate their extracellular ion/neurotransmitter environment and metabolic supply, oligodendrocytes/Schwann cells build and maintain their myelin, and they communicate with one another almost exclusively through synapses.

Sources: [Wikipedia — Neuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuron), [NCBI Bookshelf — Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th ed., "Neurons"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26877/), [StatPearls / NCBI Bookshelf — Histology, Nissl Bodies](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK555990/), [Wikipedia — Axon hillock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axon_hillock), [Wikipedia — Node of Ranvier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Node_of_Ranvier), [OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e §12.2 — Nervous Tissue](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/12-2-nervous-tissue).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids or bacterial-style flagella** — this is a human nerve cell, not a bacterium; the axon is a real, still, non-motile eukaryotic fibre, never a whip-like flagellum with an undulating beat or a 9+2 axoneme.
- **No chloroplasts or large central vacuole** — not a plant cell.
- **Not round like a red blood cell or a lymphocyte** — the defining shape is a soma with several branching dendrites plus exactly **one** long axon, never a plain sphere or disc.
- **Only ONE axon per neuron** — many dendrites is correct; never draw more than a single axon leaving the soma, and never draw the axon itself branching along most of its length (branching happens only near the terminal end).
- **Nissl bodies must stay out of the axon hillock and the axon** — the hillock and axon shaft should read visibly clearer/paler than the Nissl-packed soma; do not paint Nissl clumps continuously down the axon.
- **If myelin is shown, it must be segmented, not one unbroken tube** — periodic Nodes of Ranvier (bare gaps) between myelin internodes are required; a smooth continuous sheath with no nodes is misleading. (Unmyelinated axons are also normal — just don't draw a broken sheath as continuous.)
- **No cilia or flagella for locomotion** — mature neurons don't swim or crawl by beating appendages.
- **A single specimen** — one neuron (with, at most, its immediate synaptic partners suggested at the terminal) — not a dense tangled network of many neurons filling the frame.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 Every thought, sensation and movement you have runs through neurons — the nervous system's signalling cells, wired together into vast communication networks across the brain, spinal cord and peripheral nerves. Each neuron listens through its branching dendrites, sums up the incoming signals in its cell body, and — if the input is strong enough — fires a brief electrical impulse down its axon to the next cell in the chain. Many axons are insulated by a fatty layer called myelin, laid down by dedicated support cells (oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord, Schwann cells elsewhere), which lets signals travel dramatically faster; it's also why conditions that damage myelin, such as multiple sclerosis, can so badly disrupt nerve signalling. Unlike most cells in the body, mature neurons generally don't divide or replace themselves once lost, which is a big part of why looking after brain health — good sleep, regular exercise, cardiovascular fitness, and steering clear of excess alcohol or other neurotoxins — pays off over a lifetime. Neurons are also never really alone: astrocytes keep their chemical environment stable and supply nutrients, while oligodendrocytes maintain the myelin that keeps their signals fast.

Jedes Neuron lauscht über seine verästelten Dendriten, summiert die ankommenden Signale in seinem Zellkörper und feuert — wenn der Reiz stark genug ist — einen kurzen elektrischen Impuls über sein Axon zur nächsten Zelle in der Kette. **Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The neuron is the principal excitable cell of the nervous system, structurally polarized into dendrites, soma (perikaryon), axon and presynaptic terminals. The soma contains a large, euchromatic nucleus with a prominent nucleolus and the cell's main biosynthetic apparatus — rough endoplasmic reticulum studded with free ribosomes, classically termed Nissl substance (Nissl bodies) under basic dye staining — together with a well-developed Golgi apparatus and numerous mitochondria. Dendrites, typically studded with dendritic spines, receive the bulk of excitatory synaptic input and integrate it as graded postsynaptic potentials that summate at the soma. Action potentials are generated at the axon hillock / axon initial segment, where an especially high density of voltage-gated Na+ channels gives the lowest threshold for spike initiation, and then propagate along the single axon; in myelinated axons, saltatory conduction between Nodes of Ranvier — short bare axolemma segments rich in voltage-gated Na+ channels, flanked by myelin internodes laid down by oligodendrocytes (CNS) or Schwann cells (PNS) — greatly increases conduction velocity while reducing energy cost. Because ribosomal machinery is largely excluded from the axon and axon hillock, axonal and presynaptic proteins are synthesized in the soma and delivered by fast and slow axonal transport along microtubule tracks (kinesins anterograde, dyneins retrograde), with neurofilaments (neuron-specific intermediate filaments) providing structural support throughout. At axon terminals, synaptic vesicles loaded with neurotransmitter undergo activity-dependent, Ca2+-triggered exocytosis at active zones, with local mitochondria supplying the ATP and Ca2+ buffering needed to sustain vesicle cycling. Neuronal signalling is metabolically and structurally interdependent with glia: astrocytes regulate extracellular ion and neurotransmitter homeostasis and provide metabolic support, while oligodendrocytes generate and maintain the myelin sheath — together forming an integrated signalling network rather than an isolated cell type.  
🇩🇪 Das Neuron ist die zentrale erregbare Zelle des Nervensystems, strukturell polarisiert in Dendriten, Soma (Perikaryon), Axon und präsynaptische Endigungen. Das Soma enthält einen großen, euchromatischen Zellkern mit deutlichem Nukleolus sowie den wesentlichen Biosyntheseapparat der Zelle — raues endoplasmatisches Retikulum, besetzt mit freien Ribosomen, unter klassischer Basenfärbung als Nissl-Substanz (Nissl-Schollen) bezeichnet — zusammen mit einem gut entwickelten Golgi-Apparat und zahlreichen Mitochondrien. Dendriten, meist mit dendritischen Dornfortsätzen (Spines) besetzt, empfangen den größten Teil des exzitatorischen synaptischen Inputs und integrieren ihn als graduierte postsynaptische Potenziale, die sich im Soma summieren. Aktionspotenziale entstehen am Axonhügel bzw. am initialen Axonsegment, wo eine besonders hohe Dichte spannungsgesteuerter Na+-Kanäle die niedrigste Schwelle für die Erregungsbildung liefert, und breiten sich dann entlang des einzigen Axons aus; bei myelinisierten Axonen erhöht die saltatorische Erregungsleitung zwischen den Ranvier-Schnürringen — kurzen, kanalreichen freiliegenden Axolemm-Abschnitten, flankiert von Myelin-Internodien, die von Oligodendrozyten (ZNS) oder Schwann-Zellen (PNS) gebildet werden — die Leitungsgeschwindigkeit erheblich und senkt zugleich den Energieverbrauch. Da ribosomale Maschinerie im Axon und Axonhügel weitgehend fehlt, werden axonale und präsynaptische Proteine im Soma synthetisiert und durch schnellen und langsamen axonalen Transport entlang von Mikrotubuli-Bahnen (Kinesine anterograd, Dyneine retrograd) verteilt, während Neurofilamente (neuronenspezifische Intermediärfilamente) durchgehend die Struktur stützen. An den Axonterminalen durchlaufen mit Neurotransmitter beladene synaptische Vesikel eine aktivitätsabhängige, Ca2+-getriggerte Exozytose an aktiven Zonen, wobei lokale Mitochondrien das für den anhaltenden Vesikelzyklus nötige ATP und die Ca2+-Pufferung liefern. Die neuronale Signalübertragung ist metabolisch und strukturell eng mit Gliazellen verzahnt: Astrozyten regulieren die extrazelluläre Ionen- und Transmitterhomöostase und liefern metabolische Unterstützung, während Oligodendrozyten die Myelinscheide bilden und erhalten — zusammen entsteht so ein integriertes Signalnetzwerk statt eines isolierten Zelltyps.
