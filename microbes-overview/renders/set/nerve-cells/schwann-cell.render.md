## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The Schwann cell (neurolemmocyte) is the principal glial cell of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) — the cranial and spinal nerves, ganglia and their roots — and is the direct anatomical counterpart of the CNS oligodendrocyte, but built on the opposite plan. A single myelinating Schwann cell associates with, and myelinates, exactly ONE segment (internode) of exactly ONE axon: it aligns along the axon, then its plasma membrane wraps around the axon many times (up to ~100 revolutions), spiralling concentrically like a rolled-up sheet of paper. Each successive wrap fuses cytoplasmic membrane faces together into the "major dense line" and extracellular faces into the "intraperiodic line," compacting into multilamellar myelin. Because the entire cell body itself becomes the wrap, the Schwann cell's own flattened, elongated, crescent- or comma-shaped nucleus (with condensed peripheral heterochromatin) is pushed to the outside and lies pressed directly against/within the outermost turn of the myelin it made — never at a distance, unlike the oligodendrocyte, whose cell body stays remote from most of the myelin it maintains via long processes. A thin, cytoplasm-filled outer collar just under the plasma membrane, called the neurilemma (sheath of Schwann), runs the length of the internode and houses the organelles: rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi supporting the huge lipid/protein synthesis needed for myelin (proteins P0, myelin basic protein, PMP22), plus mitochondria for the ATP this demands. Diagonal, cytoplasm-filled clefts called Schmidt-Lanterman incisures periodically interrupt the compact myelin, providing a spiral cytoplasmic channel for metabolite/organelle traffic across the thick wrap — a feature distinctive of PNS myelin. Uniquely for the PNS (and absent from CNS oligodendrocyte myelin), each Schwann cell + axon unit is enclosed by its own continuous basal lamina (external lamina), a thin sheet of extracellular-matrix proteins (laminin, collagen IV) that the Schwann cell itself secretes; this basal lamina is what forms the regeneration-guiding endoneurial tube after injury. Adjacent Schwann cells' internodes do not touch fully: a short bare gap of exposed axonal membrane between them, the node of Ranvier, is where voltage-gated sodium channels cluster and the nerve impulse "jumps" from node to node (saltatory conduction) — internodes average roughly 1 mm long (shorter on thinner axons, longer on thicker ones), so about 1000 Schwann cells in series myelinate a 1-metre-long axon. Not every axon is myelinated: small-caliber axons (e.g. many pain/temperature C-fibres, postganglionic autonomic fibres) are instead handled by non-myelinating Schwann cells, which simply engulf several such axons together into individual pockets of their surface membrane without wrapping, forming a Remak bundle — a variant worth noting but not the illustration's primary subject, since the poster text specifically describes the classic myelinating type. After nerve injury, Schwann cells drive peripheral regeneration: distal to the injury the myelin and axon fragment (Wallerian degeneration), and the Schwann cells dedifferentiate into a proliferative "repair" phenotype (driven by the transcription factor c-Jun) that phagocytoses myelin debris (myelinophagy) alongside recruited macrophages, then lines up end-to-end inside the persisting basal-lamina tube to form a guidance track called a band of Büngner, along which the regenerating axon tip advances at roughly 1 mm/day until it reaches its target — the cellular basis of the much better regenerative capacity of the PNS compared with the CNS.

Sources: [Wikipedia — Schwann cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwann_cell), [StatPearls — Histology, Schwann Cells (NCBI Bookshelf, NBK544316)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK544316/), [Kenhub — Schwann cells](https://www.kenhub.com/en/library/anatomy/schwann-cells), [Basic Neurochemistry — The Myelin Sheath (NCBI Bookshelf, NBK27954)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK27954/), [Jessen, Mirsky & Lloyd 2015, Schwann Cells: Development and Role in Nerve Repair, Cold Spring Harb Perspect Biol (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4632663/).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids or bacterial flagella** — this is a eukaryotic human glial cell, not a prokaryote.
- **No chloroplasts or large central vacuole** — not a plant cell.
- **Do NOT wrap several different axons with one cell.** That is the oligodendrocyte's (CNS) job. A Schwann cell myelinates exactly ONE segment of exactly ONE axon — show a single wrap, not several radiating targets.
- **Do NOT draw the nucleus far away from the myelin, oligodendrocyte-style.** The defining feature of a Schwann cell is the opposite: because the whole cell body forms the wrap, its flattened nucleus sits directly against/within the outer myelin layer it built — never at the end of a long separate process.
- **Do NOT omit the basal lamina.** A continuous external lamina around the whole Schwann-cell + axon unit is a PNS-specific hallmark (CNS oligodendrocyte myelin has none) — leaving it out misrepresents the defining PNS structure.
- **No "fried-egg" halo artifact** — a pale perinuclear halo appears in some fixed H&E preparations as a processing artifact, not a real structure; illustration styles should show ordinary continuous cytoplasm.
- **Not round like a lymphocyte or red blood cell** — along the internode the cell/wrap is a smooth elongated cylinder around the axon, not a sphere or disc.
- **No cilia or flagella for locomotion** — Schwann cells (and their precursors) migrate by crawling along axons during development, not by beating appendages.
- A single specimen (one Schwann cell wrapping its one internode, with a short stretch of bare axon/adjacent node visible at each end for context), not a dense confluent nerve cross-section with many overlapping fibres.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The Schwann cell is the peripheral nervous system's dedicated myelin-maker — every nerve running from your spinal cord to your skin and muscles depends on a chain of them. Unlike the brain's oligodendrocytes, which each insulate several axon segments at once, a Schwann cell commits to exactly one segment of one axon, spiralling its own membrane around it dozens of times to build the fatty myelin sheath that lets nerve signals travel fast instead of trickling along. That single-minded relationship also makes Schwann cells remarkably good at nerve repair: after an injury they clear away debris, revert to a proliferative state and line up end to end inside the original connective-tissue tube to steer the regrowing axon back toward its target — the main reason a crushed or cut peripheral nerve (say, in a finger) can recover function over weeks to months, something the central nervous system is far worse at.  
🇩🇪 Die Schwann-Zelle ist die Myelin-Herstellerin des peripheren Nervensystems — jeder Nerv, der vom Rückenmark bis zur Haut und den Muskeln zieht, ist auf eine ganze Kette von ihnen angewiesen. Anders als die Oligodendrozyten im Gehirn, die jeweils mehrere Axonabschnitte gleichzeitig isolieren, widmet sich eine Schwann-Zelle genau einem einzigen Abschnitt eines einzigen Axons und wickelt ihre eigene Membran dutzendfach darum, um die fetthaltige Myelinscheide aufzubauen, die Nervensignale schnell statt nur langsam kriechend weiterleitet. Diese enge Eins-zu-eins-Beziehung macht Schwann-Zellen auch bemerkenswert gut in der Nervenreparatur: Nach einer Verletzung räumen sie Trümmer weg, kehren in einen teilungsfähigen Zustand zurück und reihen sich in der ursprünglichen Bindegewebsröhre aneinander, um das nachwachsende Axon zurück zu seinem Ziel zu lenken — der Hauptgrund, warum sich ein gequetschter oder durchtrennter peripherer Nerv (etwa in einem Finger) über Wochen bis Monate wieder erholen kann, was dem zentralen Nervensystem viel schlechter gelingt.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The Schwann cell (neurolemmocyte) is the myelinating and non-myelinating glial cell of the peripheral nervous system, deriving from the neural crest via the Schwann cell precursor lineage. A myelinating Schwann cell establishes a strict 1:1 relationship with a single axonal internode, spiralling its plasma membrane concentrically (up to ~100 wraps) to compact multilamellar myelin (major dense and intraperiodic lines) around one segment (~1 mm on average) of one axon, leaving its own flattened nucleus and residual cytoplasm (neurilemma) at the outer surface of the sheath it forms; this contrasts with the oligodendrocyte, whose cell body remains remote from the multiple internodes it myelinates in the CNS. Distinctive PNS-specific features include Schmidt-Lanterman incisures (cytoplasm-retaining channels through the compact myelin) and a continuous, Schwann-cell-secreted basal (external) lamina enclosing each Schwann cell-axon unit, absent from CNS myelin. Non-myelinating Schwann cells instead ensheath multiple small-calibre axons (largely C-fibres and postganglionic autonomic fibres) without wrapping, forming Remak bundles. Following axonal injury, Schwann cells distal to the lesion undergo a striking phenotypic switch, dedifferentiating (via upregulation of the transcription factor c-Jun) into a proliferative repair phenotype that clears myelin/axon debris by myelinophagy (in concert with recruited macrophages) and aligns within the persisting endoneurial basal-lamina tube to form bands of Büngner, a physical and trophic substrate that directs the regenerating axon at roughly 1 mm/day — the principal cellular basis for the far greater regenerative capacity of the peripheral relative to the central nervous system.  
🇩🇪 Die Schwann-Zelle (Neurolemmozyt) ist die myelinisierende und nicht-myelinisierende Gliazelle des peripheren Nervensystems und entstammt der Neuralleiste über die Schwann-Zell-Vorläuferlinie. Eine myelinisierende Schwann-Zelle geht eine strikte 1:1-Beziehung mit einem einzigen axonalen Internodium ein: Sie wickelt ihre Plasmamembran konzentrisch (bis zu ca. 100 Umdrehungen) um einen Abschnitt (im Mittel ca. 1 mm) eines einzigen Axons und verdichtet sie zu multilamellärem Myelin (Major-Dense- und Intraperiod-Linien), während ihr eigener flacher Zellkern und das restliche Zytoplasma (Neurilemm) an der Außenseite der von ihr gebildeten Scheide verbleiben — im Unterschied zum Oligodendrozyten, dessen Zellkörper von den mehreren Internodien, die er im ZNS myelinisiert, entfernt bleibt. Charakteristische PNS-spezifische Merkmale sind die Schmidt-Lanterman-Einkerbungen (zytoplasmahaltige Kanäle durch das kompakte Myelin) und eine durchgehende, von der Schwann-Zelle selbst sezernierte Basal- (äußere) Lamina, die jede Schwann-Zell-Axon-Einheit umschließt und im ZNS-Myelin fehlt. Nicht-myelinisierende Schwann-Zellen umhüllen stattdessen mehrere dünnkalibrige Axone (überwiegend C-Fasern und postganglionäre autonome Fasern), ohne sie zu umwickeln, und bilden Remak-Bündel. Nach einer Axonverletzung durchlaufen die distal der Läsion gelegenen Schwann-Zellen einen bemerkenswerten Phänotypwechsel: Sie dedifferenzieren (durch Hochregulierung des Transkriptionsfaktors c-Jun) zu einem proliferativen Reparaturphänotyp, der Myelin- und Axontrümmer per Myelinophagie (zusammen mit rekrutierten Makrophagen) beseitigt und sich innerhalb der erhalten gebliebenen endoneuralen Basallamina-Röhre zu Büngner-Bändern aneinanderreiht — einem physischen und trophischen Leitgerüst, das das regenerierende Axon mit etwa 1 mm pro Tag lenkt und die Hauptursache für die im Vergleich zum zentralen Nervensystem deutlich bessere Regenerationsfähigkeit des peripheren Nervensystems ist.
