## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The oligodendrocyte is the myelin-forming glial cell of the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) — the CNS counterpart of the Schwann cell in the peripheral nervous system, but built on a very different plan. Its cell body (perikaryon) is small, with a compact round-to-oval nucleus showing dense, coarsely clumped heterochromatin and only a thin rim of cytoplasm; in routine paraffin (H&E) histology this scant cytoplasm shrinks away from the nucleus during processing, giving the classic "fried-egg" look of a dark nucleus sitting in a clear halo — an artifact of fixation, not a real structure to render in an illustration. From the cell body, several (typically a handful up to a few dozen, non-branching or only sparsely branching) cytoplasmic processes extend outward, each reaching a different nearby axon. At its tip, each process flattens into a broad membrane sheet that wraps around the axon many times in a tight spiral, extruding its cytoplasm outward and fusing the wraps into compact multilamellar myelin: cytoplasmic faces of the membrane appose to form the "major dense line" and extracellular faces appose to form the "intraperiodic line," repeating with ~12 nm periodicity. Each such wrapped segment is called an internode (roughly 150–1000 µm long depending on axon size and CNS region); internodes are separated by short bare gaps of axon membrane called the nodes of Ranvier, where voltage-gated sodium channels cluster and the nerve impulse "jumps" from node to node (saltatory conduction), the reason myelination speeds up signal transmission so much. Crucially, one oligodendrocyte myelinates several axons simultaneously — on average roughly 20–40 internodes (reports range from about 1 to 50 depending on the CNS region and axon calibre) — so its cell body and nucleus sit at a distance from most of the myelin it maintains, connected only by the long thin processes; this is the single biggest anatomical difference from a Schwann cell, which stays attached to and myelinates only one axonal segment at a time. Internally, the oligodendrocyte cell body has abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum and a well-developed Golgi apparatus, reflecting the huge amount of membrane lipid and myelin protein (e.g. myelin basic protein, proteolipid protein) it must synthesize and traffic out along its processes, plus numerous mitochondria supplying the ATP for this synthesis and for its second, less obvious job: oligodendrocytes shuttle lactate to the axons they ensheath via the monocarboxylate transporter MCT1, directly fuelling axonal metabolism independent of myelination itself — which is the biological basis of the "partly fuels axons" role in the poster text.

Sources: [Wikipedia — Oligodendrocyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligodendrocyte), [Kenhub — Oligodendrocytes: location and function](https://www.kenhub.com/en/library/physiology/oligodendrocytes), [Baumann & Pham-Dinh 2001, Biology of Oligodendrocyte and Myelin in the Mammalian Central Nervous System, Physiol Rev (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11274346/), [Heterogeneity and regulation of oligodendrocyte morphology (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9644283/), [Lee et al. 2012, Oligodendroglia metabolically support axons and contribute to neurodegeneration, Nature (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22801498/), [Mechanisms Governing Oligodendrocyte Viability in Multiple Sclerosis and Its Animal Models (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10814231/).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids or bacterial flagella** — this is a eukaryotic human glial cell, not a prokaryote.
- **No chloroplasts or large central vacuole** — not a plant cell.
- **Do NOT draw the nucleus flush against the myelin it forms, Schwann-cell style.** The defining feature of an oligodendrocyte is that its cell body/nucleus sits at a distance, connected to each myelin sheath only by a long thin process — never draw the nucleus embedded directly inside the wrapped myelin.
- **Do NOT wrap only a single axon.** One oligodendrocyte reaches out to and myelinates SEVERAL different axons at once (typically shown as 3–6 in an illustration) via separate processes — a single-axon Schwann-cell-like picture is wrong for this cell.
- **Not round like a lymphocyte or red blood cell** — the cell body is small and irregular/stellate with multiple radiating processes, not a smooth sphere or biconcave disc.
- **No cilia or flagella for locomotion** — mature oligodendrocytes are stationary; they don't swim or crawl by beating appendages.
- **No "fried-egg" halo artifact in the illustration styles** — that pale halo around the nucleus in H&E slides is a fixation/processing artifact of the real micrograph, not a real cellular structure; textbook/3d/watercolor renders should show ordinary continuous cytoplasm, not a halo.
- A single specimen (with its several myelinated axon segments), not a dense confluent field of overlapping cells.

As a bonus, it even shares little snacks of energy with the wires it wraps, keeping them well fed while it works.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The oligodendrocyte is the central nervous system's myelin-maker, quietly wrapping nerve fibres in brain and spinal cord with insulating layers of membrane. Unlike its cousin in the rest of the body (the Schwann cell, which handles just one nerve fibre), a single oligodendrocyte reaches out with several long arms and myelinates multiple axons at once, which is what lets electrical signals leap rapidly from gap to gap instead of crawling along the whole fibre. It also does double duty as a quiet energy supplier, passing lactate fuel to the very axons it wraps. Because this myelin is so essential, it is exactly what comes under attack in multiple sclerosis, where the immune system mistakenly targets it and disrupts the insulation that keeps nerve signals fast and reliable.  
🇩🇪 Der Oligodendrozyt ist der Myelin-Hersteller des zentralen Nervensystems und wickelt still Nervenfasern in Gehirn und Rückenmark in isolierende Membranschichten ein. Anders als sein Verwandter im restlichen Körper, die Schwann-Zelle, die sich nur um eine einzige Nervenfaser kümmert, streckt ein einzelner Oligodendrozyt mehrere lange Arme aus und umhüllt gleichzeitig mehrere Axone mit Myelin — genau das lässt elektrische Signale schnell von Lücke zu Lücke springen, statt sich mühsam die ganze Faser entlangzuschleppen. Nebenbei übernimmt er auch noch eine leise Versorgerrolle und gibt den Axonen, die er umwickelt, Energie in Form von Laktat weiter. Weil dieses Myelin so wichtig ist, wird es bei Multipler Sklerose genau zur Zielscheibe, wenn das Immunsystem es fälschlicherweise angreift und die Isolierung stört, die Nervensignale schnell und zuverlässig hält.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The oligodendrocyte is the myelinating glial cell of the central nervous system, distinguished from the Schwann cell of the peripheral nervous system by its capacity to extend multiple non-branching processes and myelinate several axonal internodes simultaneously (on average roughly 20-40, depending on CNS region and axon calibre), leaving the cell body and nucleus physically distant from most of the myelin it maintains. Each process terminus flattens and spirally wraps an axon segment, compacting into multilamellar myelin (major dense and intraperiodic lines, ~12 nm periodicity) that is interrupted between internodes by nodes of Ranvier, where clustered voltage-gated Na+ channels enable saltatory conduction. Abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum and a well-developed Golgi apparatus support the high biosynthetic demand for myelin lipids and proteins (e.g. MBP, PLP), while numerous mitochondria supply ATP and, via the monocarboxylate transporter MCT1, export lactate that metabolically sustains the ensheathed axon independent of myelination itself. Oligodendrocytes and their myelin are the principal targets of autoimmune attack in multiple sclerosis, where T-cell-, antibody- and complement-mediated mechanisms (frequently directed against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein, MOG) drive demyelination, oxidative and excitotoxic oligodendrocyte injury, and secondary axonal degeneration.  
🇩🇪 Der Oligodendrozyt ist die myelinbildende Gliazelle des zentralen Nervensystems und unterscheidet sich von der Schwann-Zelle des peripheren Nervensystems dadurch, dass er mehrere, kaum verzweigte Fortsätze ausbildet und gleichzeitig mehrere axonale Internodien myelinisiert (im Mittel etwa 20-40, abhängig von der ZNS-Region und dem Axondurchmesser), wodurch Zellkörper und Zellkern räumlich weit entfernt vom Großteil des von ihm unterhaltenen Myelins liegen. Jeder Fortsatzendpunkt flacht ab und umwickelt spiralförmig ein Axonsegment, wobei er sich zu multilamellärem Myelin verdichtet (Major-Dense- und Intraperiod-Linien, ca. 12 nm Periodizität), das zwischen den Internodien durch Ranvier-Schnürringe unterbrochen wird, an denen sich spannungsgesteuerte Na+-Kanäle konzentrieren und die saltatorische Erregungsleitung ermöglichen. Reichlich raues endoplasmatisches Retikulum und ein gut entwickelter Golgi-Apparat decken den hohen Biosynthesebedarf an Myelinlipiden und -proteinen (z. B. MBP, PLP), während zahlreiche Mitochondrien ATP liefern und über den Monocarboxylat-Transporter MCT1 Laktat exportieren, das die umwickelten Axone unabhängig von der Myelinisierung selbst metabolisch versorgt. Oligodendrozyten und ihr Myelin sind die Hauptziele des Autoimmunangriffs bei Multipler Sklerose, bei der T-Zell-, Antikörper- und Komplement-vermittelte Mechanismen (häufig gegen das Myelin-Oligodendrozyten-Glykoprotein, MOG, gerichtet) Demyelinisierung, oxidative und exzitotoxische Oligodendrozytenschädigung sowie sekundäre axonale Degeneration antreiben.
