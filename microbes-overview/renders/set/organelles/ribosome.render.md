## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The ribosome (*ribosoma*, German *Ribosom*) is not an organelle in the membrane-bound sense at all — it is a **ribonucleoprotein molecular machine**, found unmodified in its basic two-subunit architecture across all three domains of life (bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes). At roughly **25–30 nm** across and **~4,200 kDa** for a eukaryotic (mammalian) 80S ribosome — matching this atlas's own scale entry exactly — it is one of the smallest objects catalogued here, and genuinely below the resolving power of conventional light microscopy and most transmission electron microscopy. A single rapidly growing mammalian cell contains on the order of **10 million** of them.

**The single most important thing to get right is that the ribosome has NO membrane of any kind.** Unlike every other entry in this organelle set, it is a naked assembly of RNA and protein, about two-thirds RNA and one-third protein by mass. The folded ribosomal RNA (rRNA) forms the compact structural core of each subunit and does the actual chemistry (peptide-bond catalysis); ribosomal proteins stud its surface and fill its crevices, but it is the RNA — not the protein — that gives the particle its shape.

The particle is built from **two UNEQUAL subunits**, not two matching halves. In eukaryotes these are the **large (60S) subunit** — built from the 28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNAs plus roughly 45 proteins — and the **small (40S) subunit** — built from the 18S rRNA plus roughly 30 proteins — which together form the complete **80S ribosome** (bacteria use an analogous but distinct 50S + 30S = 70S assembly). The numbers are sedimentation coefficients, not literal size ratios, but the large subunit genuinely is the visibly bulkier of the two lobes in every solved structure, and it sits with the smaller subunit capping one face of it — like a smaller bonnet perched on a larger base — never as symmetric mirror-image halves.

**Messenger RNA (mRNA) threads through a groove** at the interface between the two subunits, running mostly along the small subunit, which provides the decoding machinery that checks each codon against an incoming tRNA anticodon. Crucially, this strand is **single-stranded RNA, never a double helix** — the ribosome never touches DNA directly. Straddling that same groove, three adjacent pockets step transfer RNAs through in sequence: the **A (aminoacyl) site**, where each new charged tRNA first arrives and is checked; the **P (peptidyl) site**, holding the tRNA carrying the growing chain while the next peptide bond forms; and the **E (exit) site**, holding the spent, now-empty tRNA immediately before its release.

The **growing polypeptide chain** — a chain of linked amino acids, chemically nothing like the mRNA thread it is built from — emerges from an **exit tunnel** that runs through the body of the **large subunit only**, from the internal peptidyl transferase centre to the particle's far surface, roughly **80–100 Å (8–10 nm) long** with an average bore of about 15 Å and a slight bend partway along. This tunnel opens on the opposite face of the particle from where the mRNA runs — the two strands (nucleic acid in, protein out) never merge or emerge from the same side.

Two more things this reference insists on: the surface of a real ribosome is **knobbly and irregular everywhere** — rRNA helices and protein loops poke out unevenly — never a smooth sphere or tidy geometric solid; and a strong compositional option, where the style allows it, is a **polysome**: several ribosomes strung along one shared mRNA thread like beads, each towing its own partially built protein, which shows what the machine is actually *for* rather than only what one looks like in isolation.

Elongation proceeds at roughly **2–20 amino acids added per second** (slower in eukaryotes, faster in bacteria), matching this atlas's own description of the ribosome. That speed difference is also the reason many antibiotics work at all: because bacterial ribosomes are built differently from human ones at the molecular level (different rRNA sequences and extra/different proteins), drugs such as macrolides, aminoglycosides and tetracyclines can jam a bacterium's ribosome — often by blocking its exit tunnel or A site — while leaving the human 80S ribosome untouched.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **No membrane, envelope or capsule of any kind.** Unlike every other organelle in this set, the ribosome is a naked assembly of RNA and protein — this is the single most important thing to get right and the most common error to avoid.
- **No two equal-sized, mirror-image halves.** The large subunit must read as visibly bulkier than the small subunit, sitting asymmetrically (small capping large), never as a symmetric clamshell.
- **No DNA double helix** threading through the groove — the strand running through the ribosome is single-stranded mRNA.
- **No smooth, featureless sphere or tidy geometric solid.** The real surface is knobbly and irregular everywhere.
- **No nucleus, mitochondrial cristae, Golgi stacks** or other organelles' features nearby, as if part of the ribosome itself.
- **No exit tunnel or emerging chain on the small subunit**, and never emerging from the same side as the mRNA groove — the exit tunnel runs through the large subunit only, opposite the mRNA.
- **No merging of the mRNA strand and the polypeptide chain into one continuous strand** — they are chemically different molecules.
- **No anthropomorphising** in the scientific plates (no face, eyes or limbs — that belongs only on the colouring page), and **no baked-in text, letters, numbers, labels, scale bars, arrows or watermarks**.

## 2. Real microscopy reference (own set `reference-microscopy`)
**Honest difficulty, stated plainly:** at 25 nm, a ribosome sits well below the resolution of conventional transmission electron microscopy, let alone light microscopy — no ordinary "photograph" can resolve one. The genuinely informative real-world reference available at this scale is a **structural density model** (X-ray crystallography / cryo-EM), not a micrograph. This log says so explicitly rather than blurring the distinction.

Picture a little bead-threading machine made of two mismatched parts, a bigger base with a smaller cap sitting on top. **Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The ribosome is the smallest thing in this atlas by a wide margin — about 25 nanometres, a scale where ordinary microscopes stop being useful and only structural techniques like X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy can actually resolve its shape. What they reveal is not a tidy sphere but two lopsided, irregular lobes of RNA and protein clamped together: a larger subunit and a smaller one, with no membrane around either. Between them runs a groove carrying a strand of messenger RNA, and three adjacent pockets step transfer RNAs through in sequence — one arriving with a new amino acid, one holding the chain under construction, one releasing the spent carrier — while the finished protein chain threads out through a tunnel in the larger subunit alone. It is worth noticing that the ribosome itself is built mostly of RNA, not protein; the RNA folds into the working machinery, and protein just studs its surface, which is part of why many biologists think ribosomes are living relics of a very early stage in the history of life, before proteins took over most cellular jobs. The practical payoff of understanding this structure is considerable: because bacterial ribosomes differ from human ones in exactly the finer molecular details — different RNA sequences, different accessory proteins — a whole class of antibiotics works by jamming a bacterium's ribosome specifically, its exit tunnel or its decoding site, while your own ribosomes keep working undisturbed. It is also rarely a lone operator: several ribosomes commonly ride the same mRNA strand at once, each partway through building its own copy of the same protein, a working formation called a polysome.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The ribosome is a ~2.5 MDa (bacterial 70S) to ~4.2 MDa (eukaryotic 80S) ribonucleoprotein particle, roughly two-thirds RNA and one-third protein by mass, assembled from two unequal subunits whose rRNA core provides both structural scaffold and catalytic function. The eukaryotic large (60S) subunit comprises the 28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNAs with approximately 45 proteins and houses the peptidyl transferase centre and the ~80-100 Angstrom polypeptide exit tunnel; the small (40S) subunit comprises the 18S rRNA with approximately 30 proteins and provides the mRNA-binding channel and decoding centre responsible for codon-anticodon fidelity checking. Messenger RNA threads through a groove at the subunit interface; three adjacent tRNA-binding sites -- A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl) and E (exit) -- mediate the elongation cycle, in which an aminoacyl-tRNA delivered by an elongation factor (EF-Tu/eEF-1alpha-GTP) enters the A site, peptide-bond formation is catalysed by the rRNA-based peptidyl transferase centre in the large subunit, and translocation (driven by EF-G/eEF-2-GTP) advances the ribosome one codon along the mRNA, shifting tRNAs P-to-E and A-to-P. Elongation proceeds at approximately 2-20 amino acids per second depending on organism and conditions. Because bacterial (70S: 50S+30S) and eukaryotic cytoplasmic (80S: 60S+40S) ribosomes differ in rRNA sequence and protein complement at functionally critical sites -- notably the exit tunnel and decoding centre -- numerous antibiotic classes (macrolides, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, oxazolidinones, among others) achieve selective toxicity by targeting bacteria-specific structural features while sparing the host's cytoplasmic ribosomes (mitochondrial ribosomes, being bacterially derived, remain a partial exception and a source of some antibiotic side effects). Multiple ribosomes commonly occupy a single mRNA simultaneously, each at a different stage of translation, forming a polysome (polyribosome) that maximises translational throughput per transcript.  
🇩🇪 Das Ribosom ist ein ribonukleoproteines Partikel von etwa 2,5 MDa (bakterielles 70S) bis 4,2 MDa (eukaryotisches 80S), zu rund zwei Dritteln aus RNA und einem Drittel aus Protein bestehend, zusammengesetzt aus zwei ungleichen Untereinheiten, deren rRNA-Kern sowohl das strukturelle Gerüst als auch die katalytische Funktion stellt. Die eukaryotische große (60S) Untereinheit umfasst die 28S-, 5.8S- und 5S-rRNA mit etwa 45 Proteinen und beherbergt das Peptidyltransferase-Zentrum sowie den etwa 80-100 Angström langen Polypeptid-Austrittstunnel; die kleine (40S) Untereinheit umfasst die 18S-rRNA mit etwa 30 Proteinen und stellt den mRNA-Bindungskanal sowie das Dekodierzentrum, das die Codon-Anticodon-Genauigkeit prüft. Boten-RNA verläuft durch eine Rinne an der Grenzfläche der Untereinheiten; drei benachbarte tRNA-Bindungsstellen -- A (Aminoacyl), P (Peptidyl) und E (Exit) -- vermitteln den Elongationszyklus, in dem eine von einem Elongationsfaktor (EF-Tu/eEF-1alpha-GTP) angelieferte Aminoacyl-tRNA in die A-Stelle eintritt, die Peptidbindungsbildung durch das rRNA-basierte Peptidyltransferase-Zentrum der großen Untereinheit katalysiert wird und die Translokation (angetrieben durch EF-G/eEF-2-GTP) das Ribosom um ein Codon entlang der mRNA weiterbewegt, wobei tRNAs von P nach E und von A nach P verschoben werden. Die Elongation verläuft je nach Organismus und Bedingungen mit etwa 2-20 Aminosäuren pro Sekunde. Da sich bakterielle (70S: 50S+30S) und eukaryotische zytoplasmatische (80S: 60S+40S) Ribosomen in rRNA-Sequenz und Proteinausstattung an funktionell entscheidenden Stellen unterscheiden -- insbesondere am Austrittstunnel und am Dekodierzentrum --, erreichen zahlreiche Antibiotikaklassen (Makrolide, Aminoglykoside, Tetracykline, Oxazolidinone u.a.) selektive Toxizität, indem sie bakterienspezifische Strukturmerkmale angreifen und die zytoplasmatischen Ribosomen des Wirts verschonen (mitochondriale Ribosomen bilden als bakteriell abstammende Strukturen eine teilweise Ausnahme und sind Quelle mancher Antibiotika-Nebenwirkungen). Häufig besetzen mehrere Ribosomen gleichzeitig eine einzelne mRNA, jedes in einem anderen Translationsstadium, und bilden so ein Polysom, das den Translationsdurchsatz pro Transkript maximiert.
