## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The cytoskeleton (Latin *cytoskeletum*, German *Zytoskelett*) is not a single membrane-bound organelle but a whole **system of three distinct protein fibre types** threading through the cytoplasm of every eukaryotic cell: **microfilaments** (actin), **intermediate filaments**, and **microtubules**. Together they give the cell its shape, let it crawl and change shape, hold organelles in place, and act as the tracks that motor proteins walk cargo along. The single most important teaching fact — and the one this poster is built around — is that the three fibre types are not just "more of the same" at different scales: they are built from completely different proteins, play different mechanical roles, and above all have unmistakably different, easily-checkable diameters, from thinnest to thickest: **actin (~7 nm) < intermediate filaments (~10 nm) < microtubules (~25 nm)**. Making that size ordering visually obvious at a glance is the entire point of this picture.

**Microfilaments (actin)**, the thinnest of the three, are helical polymers of two thin protofilament strands of the globular protein G-actin twisted around each other like a two-stranded beaded rope (assembled, the polymer is called F-actin). They are polarised — a fast-growing "plus" end and a slower "minus" end — and are concentrated in a dense meshwork just under the plasma membrane called the **cell cortex**, and inside surface protrusions such as filopodia and lamellipodia that let a cell crawl. Continuous growth at the plus end and loss at the minus end ("treadmilling"), often paired with the motor protein myosin, powers cell crawling, surface shape changes, and the contractile ring that pinches a dividing cell in two.

**Intermediate filaments**, of medium thickness (~10 nm, literally intermediate between the other two), are the most rope-like in appearance: bundled, staggered ropes of coiled, elongated fibrous-protein subunits (keratins, vimentin, desmin, neurofilament proteins and others, depending on cell type) with **no inherent polarity** — no plus or minus end, and no attached motor proteins. Mechanically they are the toughest and most tension-resistant of the three fibre types, anchoring at cell-cell and cell-matrix junctions to spread mechanical stress across a tissue.

**Microtubules**, the thickest of the three (~25 nm), are the only ones that are **hollow** — a tube wall built from 13 parallel protofilaments of alpha/beta-tubulin dimers around a visible central bore roughly 15 nm across, giving the surface a finely ridged, brick-like lattice rather than a smooth rod. Most microtubules radiate outward through the cytoplasm from the **centrosome** (the microtubule-organizing centre, MTOC, typically sitting next to the nucleus), minus ends anchored there, plus ends reaching toward the cell periphery, in a characteristic star/aster pattern. Like actin, microtubules are constantly built and torn down — "dynamic instability," rapid stochastic switching between growth and sudden shrinkage. During mitosis the centrosome duplicates and organises the mitotic spindle, whose microtubules attach to and physically pull the duplicated chromosomes apart — the direct, concrete answer to how a "railway" also handles cell division.

Riding those microtubule tracks are **motor proteins**: kinesins (which generally walk toward the microtubule plus end, outward) and dyneins (which generally walk toward the minus end, inward toward the centrosome). Both have a compact two-headed, two-legged shape that takes alternating hand-over-hand steps along the tube's surface, with a tail domain hauling cargo — typically a membrane vesicle — behind it. That two-legged walker striding along a hollow tube, carrying a vesicle, is the concrete picture behind "transport rail."

None of the three fibre systems is a fixed, permanent scaffold like a skeleton of bones. All three are constantly remodelled — actin treadmills, microtubules undergo dynamic instability, and even the sturdier intermediate filaments exchange subunits — on timescales of seconds to minutes, which is what lets a cell rebuild its shape, crawl, divide and rebuild its transport network from one moment to the next. The intracellular pathogen *Listeria monocytogenes* hijacks exactly this dynamism: its surface protein ActA, displayed at one bacterial pole, activates the host cell's Arp2/3 actin-nucleating complex (plus profilin and Ena/VASP proteins), triggering explosive, localised actin polymerisation that builds a branched "comet tail" behind the bacterium. Continuous polymerisation at the bacterium-tail interface pushes the bacterium through the cytoplasm and out into thin, actin-filled protrusions into neighbouring cells — letting it spread from cell to cell without ever leaving the cytosol or meeting an antibody.

None of the three fibre types has any colour in life. The classic real-world image of the cytoskeleton is a fluorescence light micrograph in which each fibre type carries a different coloured fluorescent antibody stain and the nucleus is counterstained separately — the colours seen are stains, not a natural property of the structures, and all colour in the illustrated plates below is likewise a chosen teaching convention.

Sources: [Cooper, *The Cell: A Molecular Approach* — "The Actin Cytoskeleton" (NCBI Bookshelf NBK9908)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9908/), ["Actin, Myosin, and Cell Movement" (NBK9961)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9961/), ["Intermediate Filaments" (NBK9834)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9834/), ["Microtubules" (NBK9932)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9932/), ["Microtubule Motors and Movements" (NBK9833)](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9833/), [Alberts et al., *Molecular Biology of the Cell* — "Molecular Motors" (NBK26888)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26888/), [Kühn et al., "The actin comet guides the way" — Cellular Microbiology 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cmi.13190), [Wikipedia — Cytoskeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytoskeleton).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **No solid microtubules.** They are HOLLOW tubes with a visible open bore/lumen at any cut end or tip, and a finely ridged 13-protofilament lattice on the surface — a solid rod is the single most damaging error for this subject.
- **No equal-thickness fibres.** The whole teaching point is thin-actin < medium-intermediate-filament < thick-microtubule; if the three look interchangeable in thickness the picture has failed at its one job.
- **No rigid fixed skeleton.** The cytoskeleton is constantly assembled and disassembled (actin treadmilling, microtubule dynamic instability) — convey active, dynamic construction, not a static cage of bones.
- **No DNA/chromosomes as cytoskeletal structures.** Microtubules pull chromosomes apart during division, but chromosomes/DNA are not cytoskeletal fibres and must not be drawn as such.
- **No bacterial flagellum.** A rotary corkscrew propeller is a bacterial motility structure (flagellin), structurally and functionally unrelated to any of the three fibre types here.
- **No smooth single-strand actin.** Actin filaments are a two-stranded twisted/beaded rope — distinct from the braided-rope look of intermediate filaments and the ridged hollow-tube look of microtubules.
- **No polarity arrow on intermediate filaments** — unlike actin and microtubules, they have no plus/minus end.
- **No anthropomorphising** in the scientific plates (no face, eyes or limbs there — that belongs only on the separate colouring page), and **no baked-in text, letters, numbers, labels, scale bars, arrows or watermarks**.

**Kids (GiantMicrobes-style).**  
🇬🇧 Meet the cytoskeleton — not one thing, but a whole toolbox of ropes and rails stretched through every cell, doing three jobs at once: scaffolding, muscle, and railway track. The thinnest ropes are microfilaments, made of a protein called actin, and they sit just under the cell's skin, letting the cell push out little feet and crawl along like it's tip-toeing. The medium ropes are intermediate filaments, tougher and more stretch-proof, holding the cell together at its seams so it doesn't tear when it gets tugged. And the thickest ropes are microtubules — and here's the neat part, they're not solid at all, they're hollow tubes, like drinking straws, radiating out from a little hub near the middle of the cell. Tiny two-legged robots called motor proteins march along those tubes like they're walking a tightrope, carrying parcels (little bubbles called vesicles) exactly where the cell needs them. And none of it stays built for long — the cell is constantly taking ropes apart and rebuilding them somewhere else, like scaffolding on a building that never stops being rearranged. There's even a sneaky microbe, Listeria, that has figured out how to steal a cell's own actin rope-making kit and build itself a little tail to shove its way from one cell into the next!

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The cytoskeleton is not one organelle but a whole cytoplasmic system built from three structurally distinct fibre types, and the easiest way to tell them apart is simply by thickness. Microfilaments, made of the protein actin, are the thinnest at about 7 nanometres — two thin strands twisted round each other like a beaded rope, concentrated in a dense mesh just under the plasma membrane where they shape the cell surface and, together with the motor protein myosin, let a cell crawl or divide in two. Intermediate filaments sit in the middle at about 10 nanometres, built from a whole family of tough, rope-like proteins (keratins in skin cells, vimentin in connective tissue, neurofilament proteins in neurons) that give a cell its tensile strength and anchor it to its neighbours. And microtubules are the thickest at about 25 nanometres — and, unlike the other two, genuinely hollow: a tube built from 13 parallel strands of tubulin protein around an open central bore, radiating out from a small hub near the nucleus called the centrosome. Microtubules double as the cell's transport rails: motor proteins called kinesin and dynein walk along them hand-over-hand, hauling vesicles and organelles to wherever they're needed, and during cell division it's microtubules of the mitotic spindle that physically pull the duplicated chromosomes apart. None of this is fixed in place — all three fibre systems are constantly being built up and dismantled again, which is what lets a cell reshape itself from one moment to the next. It's worth knowing that this dynamism has a dark side: the food-borne bacterium Listeria monocytogenes hijacks the actin-building machinery directly, growing itself a propulsive actin tail that shoves it from one host cell into the next without ever having to leave the safety of the cytoplasm.  
🇩🇪 Das Zytoskelett ist kein einzelnes Organell, sondern ein ganzes System aus drei strukturell unterschiedlichen Fasertypen im Zellplasma, die sich am einfachsten an ihrer Dicke unterscheiden lassen. Mikrofilamente aus dem Eiweiß Aktin sind mit rund 7 Nanometern die dünnsten — zwei dünne, umeinander gewundene Stränge wie ein Perlenseil, konzentriert in einem dichten Geflecht direkt unter der Plasmamembran, wo sie die Zelloberfläche formen und der Zelle zusammen mit dem Motorprotein Myosin das Kriechen oder die Teilung in zwei Hälften ermöglichen. Intermediärfilamente liegen mit rund 10 Nanometern in der Mitte, aufgebaut aus einer ganzen Familie zäher, seilartiger Proteine (Keratine in Hautzellen, Vimentin in Bindegewebe, Neurofilament-Proteine in Nervenzellen), die der Zelle Zugfestigkeit verleihen und sie an ihren Nachbarn verankern. Und Mikrotubuli sind mit rund 25 Nanometern die dicksten — und anders als die beiden anderen tatsächlich hohl: eine Röhre aus 13 parallelen Strängen des Proteins Tubulin um einen offenen zentralen Hohlraum, die von einem kleinen Knotenpunkt nahe dem Zellkern, dem Zentrosom, nach außen strahlt. Mikrotubuli dienen zugleich als Transportschienen der Zelle: Motorproteine namens Kinesin und Dynein laufen Schritt für Schritt an ihnen entlang und schleppen Vesikel und Organellen dorthin, wo sie gebraucht werden, und bei der Zellteilung sind es Mikrotubuli der Teilungsspindel, die die verdoppelten Chromosomen tatsächlich auseinanderziehen. Nichts davon ist fest verbaut — alle drei Fasersysteme werden ständig auf- und wieder abgebaut, wodurch sich eine Zelle von einem Moment zum nächsten neu formen kann. Diese Beweglichkeit hat auch eine Schattenseite: Das lebensmittelübertragene Bakterium Listeria monocytogenes kapert direkt die Aktin-Baumaschinerie der Zelle und lässt sich einen antreibenden Aktin-Schweif wachsen, der es von einer Wirtszelle in die nächste schiebt, ohne je die schützende Umgebung des Zytoplasmas verlassen zu müssen.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The eukaryotic cytoskeleton comprises three structurally and biochemically distinct polymer systems, most reliably distinguished by diameter: microfilaments (F-actin, ~7 nm), intermediate filaments (~10 nm), and microtubules (~25 nm). Microfilaments are helical polymers of two protofilaments of globular G-actin, intrinsically polarized (barbed/plus and pointed/minus ends), concentrated in the cortical actin network beneath the plasma membrane and in dynamic protrusions (lamellipodia, filopodia); actin dynamics (treadmilling), typically coupled to myosin motors, drive cell crawling, cortical tension and cytokinetic ring constriction. Intermediate filaments are assembled from a large, tissue-specific family of elongated coiled-coil proteins (type I/II keratins, type III vimentin/desmin/GFAP, type IV neurofilament proteins, type V lamins) that dimerize, associate into staggered antiparallel tetramers, and bundle into apolar ropes lacking associated motor proteins; they provide the principal tension-bearing, mechanically resilient element of the cytoskeleton, anchoring at desmosomes and hemidesmosomes. Microtubules are hollow, polarized cylinders of 13 protofilaments of alpha/beta-tubulin heterodimers surrounding a ~15 nm lumen, nucleated with minus ends anchored at the centrosome (the principal microtubule-organizing centre, MTOC) and plus ends extending toward the cell cortex; they undergo dynamic instability (stochastic switching between growth and catastrophic depolymerization via GTP-cap loss) and serve both as the mitotic/meiotic spindle apparatus, whose kinetochore-attached microtubules segregate chromosomes, and as tracks for the kinesin (largely plus-end-directed) and dynein (minus-end-directed) motor protein superfamilies, which translocate vesicular and organellar cargo via ATP-dependent hand-over-hand stepping. All three systems are maintained far from mechanical/thermodynamic equilibrium through continuous subunit turnover, enabling rapid, regulated remodelling of cell shape, motility and division. This dynamism is directly exploited by the intracellular pathogen Listeria monocytogenes: the bacterial surface protein ActA activates the host Arp2/3 complex (with profilin and Ena/VASP cofactors) to nucleate branched actin polymerization at one bacterial pole, generating a propulsive actin comet tail that drives intracytoplasmic motility and formation of membrane protrusions mediating cell-to-cell spread without extracellular exposure.  
🇩🇪 Das eukaryotische Zytoskelett besteht aus drei strukturell und biochemisch unterschiedlichen Polymersystemen, die sich am zuverlässigsten anhand ihres Durchmessers unterscheiden lassen: Mikrofilamente (F-Aktin, ~7 nm), Intermediärfilamente (~10 nm) und Mikrotubuli (~25 nm). Mikrofilamente sind helikale Polymere aus zwei Protofilamenten des globulären G-Aktins, intrinsisch polarisiert (barbed/Plus- und pointed/Minus-Ende), konzentriert im kortikalen Aktinnetzwerk unter der Plasmamembran und in dynamischen Ausstülpungen (Lamellipodien, Filopodien); Aktin-Dynamik (Treadmilling), meist gekoppelt an Myosin-Motoren, treibt Zellkriechen, kortikale Spannung und die Kontraktion des Teilungsrings an. Intermediärfilamente werden aus einer großen, gewebespezifischen Familie langgestreckter Coiled-Coil-Proteine aufgebaut (Typ-I/II-Keratine, Typ-III-Vimentin/Desmin/GFAP, Typ-IV-Neurofilament-Proteine, Typ-V-Lamine), die dimerisieren, sich zu versetzten antiparallelen Tetrameren zusammenlagern und zu unpolaren Seilen ohne assoziierte Motorproteine bündeln; sie stellen das wichtigste zugtragende, mechanisch belastbare Element des Zytoskeletts dar und verankern an Desmosomen und Hemidesmosomen. Mikrotubuli sind hohle, polarisierte Zylinder aus 13 Protofilamenten von Alpha/Beta-Tubulin-Heterodimeren um ein rund 15 nm weites Lumen, mit Minus-Enden verankert am Zentrosom (dem wichtigsten Mikrotubuli-Organisationszentrum, MTOC) und Plus-Enden zum Zellkortex hin ausgerichtet; sie durchlaufen dynamische Instabilität (stochastischer Wechsel zwischen Wachstum und katastrophalem Abbau nach Verlust der GTP-Kappe) und dienen sowohl als mitotischer/meiotischer Spindelapparat, dessen kinetochorgebundene Mikrotubuli die Chromosomen trennen, als auch als Schienen für die Motorprotein-Superfamilien Kinesin (überwiegend plus-end-gerichtet) und Dynein (minus-end-gerichtet), die vesikuläre und organelläre Fracht durch ATP-abhängiges Schritt-für-Schritt-Laufen transportieren. Alle drei Systeme werden durch fortlaufenden Untereinheiten-Umsatz fernab vom mechanischen/thermodynamischen Gleichgewicht gehalten, was eine schnelle, regulierte Umgestaltung von Zellform, Beweglichkeit und Teilung ermöglicht. Diese Dynamik wird vom intrazellulären Erreger Listeria monocytogenes direkt ausgenutzt: Das bakterielle Oberflächenprotein ActA aktiviert den Wirts-Arp2/3-Komplex (mit den Kofaktoren Profilin und Ena/VASP) und nukleiert an einem Bakterienpol verzweigte Aktinpolymerisation, wodurch ein antreibender Aktin-Kometenschweif entsteht, der die intrazytoplasmatische Beweglichkeit und die Bildung von Membranausstülpungen für die Zell-zu-Zell-Ausbreitung ohne extrazelluläre Exposition ermöglicht.
