## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The lysosome (Latin *lysosoma*, German *Lysosom*) is an **organelle, not a cell**: a roughly spherical, membrane-bound sac found in essentially every animal cell, filled with a cocktail of digestive enzymes that work only in its acidic interior. It is the cell's recycling and demolition site, breaking down material taken in from outside (via phagocytosis or endocytosis) and the cell's own worn-out parts (via autophagy) into reusable building blocks. It was discovered by Christian de Duve in the 1950s (Nobel Prize 1974) after he noticed that isolated lysosomal enzymes were "latent" — inactive until the surrounding membrane was disrupted, the first clue that the membrane itself was doing protective work, not just holding a bag of enzymes.

Anatomically the single most important fact — and the one this organelle is most often drawn wrong — is that the lysosome has **exactly ONE limiting membrane**. This is a real, deliberate contrast with the nucleus and the mitochondrion elsewhere in this atlas, both of which are double-membraned. Inside that single membrane sits the **lumen**, held strongly **acidic at about pH 4.5–5**, roughly a hundredfold more acidic than the near-neutral cytosol outside. That acidity is actively built and maintained by **V-ATPase proton pumps** studding the membrane — stalked, lollipop-shaped complexes that use ATP to pump H+ into the lumen, broadly similar in silhouette to the mitochondrion's ATP synthase but running in the opposite direction (spending ATP to pump protons in, rather than letting protons flow out to make ATP) and not producing ATP itself.

The lumen holds more than 50–60 distinct **acid hydrolases** — proteases, nucleases, glycosidases, lipases, phospholipases, phosphatases and sulfatases — all of them enzymes that work well only at the lysosome's own acidic pH and fall off sharply at the cytosol's neutral pH. That dependency is a built-in safety catch: even if a lysosome ruptured, the enzymes that leaked out would be essentially inactive in the neutral cytoplasm around them, so the digestive machinery cannot easily turn on the rest of the cell. A second, often-omitted layer of protection guards the lysosome from digesting *itself*: the inside face of the limiting membrane carries a dense, heavily sugar-coated lining of glycosylated membrane proteins (chiefly LAMP-1 and LAMP-2) called the **lysosomal glycocalyx**, which physically shields the membrane's own lipids from the surrounding acid hydrolases.

A real lysosome interior is **heterogeneous and granular**, never a smooth empty bubble — a mix of free hydrolase granules and partly digested material at various stages of breakdown, which is exactly how lysosomes read in TEM: irregular, moderately-to-highly electron-dense, textured bodies. The strongest teaching composition for this organelle — and the one used across the styles below — shows a lysosome **fusing with a phagosome** that contains an engulfed bacterium. This is not incidental: in a macrophage or neutrophil, the phagosome formed around a swallowed bacterium is, by itself, just an inert membrane bag holding a live microbe. Only once it fuses with one or more lysosomes — becoming a **phagolysosome** — does it turn acidic and receive the acid hydrolases that actually kill and digest the bacterium. The lysosome's acidic lumen is what finishes the job the immune system started.

The lysosome is **colourless in life**; TEM is greyscale. All colour in these plates is a teaching convention, chosen here to make the single-membrane / acidic-granular-lumen / V-ATPase / protective-glycocalyx relationship legible at a glance, and to make the phagosome-fusion story read clearly as two compartments merging into one.

Sources: [Cooper, *The Cell: A Molecular Approach* — "Lysosomes" (NCBI Bookshelf NBK9953)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9953/), [Alberts et al., *Molecular Biology of the Cell* — "Transport from the Trans Golgi Network to Lysosomes" (NCBI Bookshelf NBK26844)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26844/), ["The Acid Gate in the Lysosome" (Autophagy / Taylor & Francis)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15548627.2022.2125629), ["Lysosomal membrane glycoproteins bind cholesterol and contribute to lysosomal cholesterol export" (eLife)](https://elifesciences.org/articles/21635), ["Crystal structure of the conserved domain of the DC lysosomal associated membrane protein" (BMC Biology / PMC3409847)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3409847/), ["Phagolysosome" overview (ScienceDirect Topics)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/phagolysosome), [Wikipedia — Lysosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysosome).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **No double membrane.** A lysosome has exactly ONE limiting membrane — that is the whole anatomical point of this poster. Two concentric membranes with a gap is the nucleus or the mitochondrion, not a lysosome.
- **No cristae.** Deep membrane folds belong to the mitochondrion's inner membrane; the lysosome's limiting membrane is essentially smooth apart from the small stalked V-ATPase pumps studding it.
- **No ribosomes studding the membrane.** A ribosome-studded membrane is rough endoplasmic reticulum, a different organelle.
- **No stack of flattened cisternae.** That is the Golgi apparatus, a separate subject in this atlas — the lysosome is a single rounded vesicle, not a stack of discs.
- **No nucleus inside it.** No nuclear envelope, nuclear pores, or chromatin — the lysosome is not a miniature cell.
- **No literal scissors/tools for the enzymes.** Acid hydrolases are molecules; draw them as small granules, never as scissors, knives or other tool shapes.
- **No uniformly empty interior.** A real lysosome, especially one actively digesting, is heterogeneous and granular inside — a smooth empty bubble is not realistic.
- **No anthropomorphising** in the scientific plates (no face, eyes or limbs there — that belongs only on the colouring page), and **no baked-in text, letters, numbers, labels, scale bars, arrows or watermarks**.

**Kids (GiantMicrobes-style).**  
🇬🇧 Meet the lysosome — the recycling room inside almost every one of your cells. It's a small round bubble with just one wall around it, and inside, everything is kept sour, almost like a weak vinegar, because the special tools inside — little cutting-and-splitting machines called enzymes — only work properly when it's sour like that. Bring the lysosome a worn-out part of the cell, an old floppy protein, or a leftover bit of food, and it takes the thing apart piece by piece so the pieces can be reused to build something new. Nothing gets wasted. There's an even cooler trick: the lysosome pumps that sourness in itself, using tiny pumps built right into its wall, and it lines the inside of that wall with a sugary coat so its own tools never chew through the wall by accident. Here's the precise, satisfying part of the story: when a cell like a macrophage swallows a germ, it first wraps the germ in its own little bubble — but that bubble alone doesn't finish the job. It's only once a lysosome joins up with it, pouring in its sour digesting tools, that the germ actually gets broken down for good. The lysosome is the room where the job gets properly finished.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The lysosome is the cell's demolition and recycling site: a single-membrane-bound sac, typically a few hundred nanometres to just over a micron across, holding more than fifty distinct digestive enzymes that only function at the strongly acidic pH the organelle maintains inside itself - roughly a hundredfold more acidic than the cytosol surrounding it. That acidity is not passive; it is actively pumped in by V-ATPase proton pumps embedded in the membrane, which spend ATP to move H+ ions into the lumen against a steep gradient. The arrangement doubles as a safety mechanism: an enzyme that leaked out through a damaged membrane would find itself in the near-neutral cytoplasm, where it is essentially inactive. As a second line of defence, the inside face of the lysosome's own membrane is coated in a dense layer of heavily sugar-decorated proteins - a detail most illustrations skip - that physically shields the membrane's lipids from the acid enzymes surrounding them.

Lysosomes break down material from two directions at once: things the cell has taken in from outside, and the cell's own worn-out components. Old or damaged organelles, misfolded proteins, and surplus material get funnelled in via autophagy, an internal quality-control and recycling loop that becomes especially important during fasting, stress or ageing, when cells lean on it to reclaim raw materials. From outside, material arrives via endocytosis and, in specialised cells like macrophages and neutrophils, via phagocytosis: whole particles, including bacteria, engulfed into a membrane-bound phagosome.

A freshly formed phagosome, on its own, is not lethal to what it contains - it is simply a sealed bag. It becomes dangerous only once it fuses with one or more lysosomes to form a phagolysosome, at which point the compartment acidifies and receives the full battery of hydrolytic enzymes. That fusion step is, quite literally, where a swallowed bacterium in a macrophage or neutrophil is actually destroyed; everything upstream of it - recognition, engulfment - only sets the stage.  
🇩🇪 Das Lysosom ist die Abriss- und Recyclingstation der Zelle: ein von einer einzigen Membran umschlossenes Bläschen, meist einige hundert Nanometer bis knapp über einen Mikrometer groß, mit mehr als fünfzig verschiedenen Verdauungsenzymen, die nur bei dem stark sauren pH-Wert funktionieren, den das Organell in seinem Inneren selbst aufrechterhält - etwa hundertmal saurer als das umgebende Zytosol. Diese Säure entsteht nicht von selbst; sie wird aktiv von V-ATPase-Protonenpumpen in die Membran gepumpt, die ATP verbrauchen, um H⁺-Ionen gegen ein steiles Gefälle ins Innere zu befördern. Diese Anordnung dient gleichzeitig als Sicherheitsmechanismus: Ein Enzym, das durch eine beschädigte Membran nach außen gelangt, findet sich im nahezu neutralen Zytoplasma wieder, wo es praktisch unwirksam ist. Als zweite Verteidigungslinie ist die Innenseite der lysosomalen Membran selbst mit einer dichten Schicht stark zuckerbehängter Proteine ausgekleidet - ein Detail, das die meisten Darstellungen weglassen -, die die Membranlipide physisch vor den umgebenden sauren Enzymen abschirmt.

Lysosomen bauen Material aus zwei Richtungen gleichzeitig ab: Dinge, die die Zelle von außen aufgenommen hat, und die eigenen abgenutzten Bestandteile der Zelle. Alte oder beschädigte Organellen, falsch gefaltete Proteine und überschüssiges Material werden über Autophagie eingeschleust, einen internen Qualitätskontroll- und Recyclingkreislauf, der besonders beim Fasten, unter Stress oder im Alter wichtig wird, wenn Zellen darauf angewiesen sind, Rohstoffe zurückzugewinnen. Von außen kommt Material über Endozytose und, in spezialisierten Zellen wie Makrophagen und Neutrophilen, über Phagozytose: ganze Partikel, einschließlich Bakterien, werden in ein membranumschlossenes Phagosom aufgenommen.

Ein frisch gebildetes Phagosom ist für sich genommen nicht tödlich für das, was es enthält - es ist schlicht eine verschlossene Hülle. Gefährlich wird es erst, wenn es mit einem oder mehreren Lysosomen zu einem Phagolysosom verschmilzt; erst dann säuert sich das Kompartiment an und erhält die volle Ladung hydrolytischer Enzyme. Genau dieser Verschmelzungsschritt ist es, an dem ein verschlucktes Bakterium in einem Makrophagen oder Neutrophilen tatsächlich zerstört wird; alles, was vorher passiert - Erkennung, Aufnahme -, bereitet nur die Bühne.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The lysosome is a single-membrane-bound organelle of the endomembrane system, built by vesicular trafficking from the trans-Golgi network rather than possessing its own genome. It maintains a luminal pH of approximately 4.5-5.0 against a roughly hundredfold gradient with the cytosol (~pH 7.2), a difference actively generated and sustained by vacuolar-type H+-ATPase (V-ATPase) complexes in the limiting membrane: a cytosolic, ATP-hydrolysing V1 sector coupled via a central rotary stalk to a membrane-embedded V0 proton-translocating sector, mechanistically analogous in overall architecture (though functionally inverse) to the FoF1-ATP synthase of mitochondria. The lumen contains upwards of 50-60 distinct acid hydrolases - proteases (cathepsins), nucleases, glycosidases, lipases, phospholipases, phosphatases and sulfatases - synthesised in the rough ER, tagged with mannose-6-phosphate in the cis-Golgi, recognised by the mannose-6-phosphate receptor in the trans-Golgi network, and delivered by vesicular transport. Their strict pH dependence provides a built-in fail-safe: hydrolases that escape a compromised lysosomal membrane are essentially inactive at cytosolic pH. A second, structurally distinct protective layer - the lysosomal glycocalyx, formed chiefly by the type-I transmembrane glycoproteins LAMP-1 and LAMP-2, whose luminal domains carry dense poly-N-acetyllactosamine glycosylation - coats the inner leaflet of the limiting membrane and sterically shields it from the surrounding hydrolases; enzymatic removal of these glycans accelerates LAMP protein turnover, consistent with a protective role.

Lysosomal substrates arrive by two convergent routes. The endocytic/phagocytic route delivers extracellular and cell-surface material via early and late endosomes, or via phagosomes formed during phagocytosis of particulate material including microorganisms; the autophagic route delivers cytosolic material - damaged organelles, protein aggregates, macromolecular complexes - via macroautophagy (double-membrane autophagosomes), microautophagy (direct membrane invagination) or chaperone-mediated autophagy (LAMP-2A-dependent translocation of substrate proteins). In professional phagocytes, phagosome maturation proceeds through sequential fusion with early endosomes, late endosomes and finally lysosomes to form the phagolysosome, a process that both delivers the hydrolase complement and drives luminal acidification (to pH <=5.0) via V-ATPase recruitment; this acidification is required for optimal hydrolase activity and for denaturation of microbial proteins, making phagolysosome formation - not phagocytic engulfment per se - the decisive step in intracellular bacterial killing. Several pathogens (e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Legionella pneumophila) subvert this pathway by arresting phagosome maturation or blocking lysosomal fusion, underscoring how much of a phagocyte's bactericidal capacity depends specifically on this fusion event rather than on engulfment alone.  
🇩🇪 Das Lysosom ist ein von einer einzigen Membran umschlossenes Organell des Endomembransystems, das durch vesikulären Transport vom trans-Golgi-Netzwerk aus aufgebaut wird und kein eigenes Genom besitzt. Es hält einen luminalen pH-Wert von etwa 4,5-5,0 gegen ein etwa hundertfaches Gefälle zum Zytosol (~pH 7,2) aufrecht - ein Unterschied, der aktiv durch vakuoläre H⁺-ATPase-Komplexe (V-ATPase) in der begrenzenden Membran erzeugt und aufrechterhalten wird: ein zytosolischer, ATP-hydrolysierender V1-Sektor, gekoppelt über einen zentralen rotierenden Stiel an einen membranständigen, protonentranslozierenden V0-Sektor - in der Gesamtarchitektur mechanistisch verwandt mit (wenn auch funktional entgegengesetzt zu) der FoF1-ATP-Synthase der Mitochondrien. Das Lumen enthält über 50-60 verschiedene saure Hydrolasen - Proteasen (Kathepsine), Nukleasen, Glykosidasen, Lipasen, Phospholipasen, Phosphatasen und Sulfatasen -, die im rauen ER synthetisiert, im cis-Golgi mit Mannose-6-phosphat markiert, vom Mannose-6-phosphat-Rezeptor im trans-Golgi-Netzwerk erkannt und per Vesikeltransport ausgeliefert werden. Ihre strikte pH-Abhängigkeit liefert eine eingebaute Sicherung: Hydrolasen, die durch eine beschädigte lysosomale Membran entkommen, sind beim zytosolischen pH-Wert praktisch inaktiv. Eine zweite, strukturell eigenständige Schutzschicht - die lysosomale Glykokalyx, vor allem gebildet von den Typ-I-Transmembran-Glykoproteinen LAMP-1 und LAMP-2, deren luminale Domänen dicht mit Poly-N-Acetyllactosamin glykosyliert sind - überzieht das innere Blatt der begrenzenden Membran und schirmt es sterisch vor den umgebenden Hydrolasen ab; die enzymatische Entfernung dieser Glykane beschleunigt den Abbau der LAMP-Proteine, was zu einer schützenden Rolle passt.

Lysosomale Substrate erreichen das Organell über zwei zusammenlaufende Wege. Der endozytotische/phagozytotische Weg liefert extrazelluläres Material und Material von der Zelloberfläche über frühe und späte Endosomen oder über Phagosomen, die bei der Phagozytose partikulären Materials einschließlich Mikroorganismen entstehen; der autophagische Weg liefert zytosolisches Material - beschädigte Organellen, Proteinaggregate, makromolekulare Komplexe - über Makroautophagie (doppelmembranige Autophagosomen), Mikroautophagie (direkte Membraneinstülpung) oder chaperonvermittelte Autophagie (LAMP-2A-abhängige Translokation von Substratproteinen). In professionellen Phagozyten durchläuft das Phagosom eine Reifung durch aufeinanderfolgende Fusion mit frühen Endosomen, späten Endosomen und schließlich Lysosomen zum Phagolysosom - ein Prozess, der sowohl das Hydrolase-Set liefert als auch die luminale Ansäuerung (bis pH <=5,0) über die Rekrutierung von V-ATPase vorantreibt; diese Ansäuerung ist für die optimale Hydrolase-Aktivität und die Denaturierung mikrobieller Proteine notwendig, weshalb die Phagolysosomenbildung - nicht die phagozytotische Aufnahme an sich - der entscheidende Schritt bei der intrazellulären Abtötung von Bakterien ist. Mehrere Erreger (z. B. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Legionella pneumophila) unterlaufen diesen Weg, indem sie die Phagosomenreifung stoppen oder die lysosomale Fusion blockieren - ein Beleg dafür, wie stark die bakterizide Kapazität eines Phagozyten spezifisch von diesem Fusionsschritt abhängt und nicht allein von der Aufnahme.
