## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The nucleus (*nucleus*, German *Zellkern*) is an **organelle, not a cell** — the largest organelle in most eukaryotic cells (roughly **6 µm** across in a typical human cell, ~10% of cell volume) and, together with mitochondria, one of only two membrane-bound compartments with their own genome-adjacent identity. Bacteria and archaea have no true nucleus at all; their DNA sits unbound in the cytoplasm as a nucleoid.

**The single most important thing to get right is that the nuclear envelope is a DOUBLE membrane, not one.** It consists of two separate, concentric lipid bilayers — an **outer nuclear membrane (ONM)** facing the cytoplasm and an **inner nuclear membrane (INM)** facing the nucleoplasm — separated by a narrow **perinuclear space** (~20–50 nm). The two membranes are chemically distinct (different resident proteins) but physically continuous with each other at the rim of every nuclear pore, where they fuse into a single curved pore membrane. Crucially, the **outer membrane is directly continuous with the rough endoplasmic reticulum**: it is studded with ribosomes exactly like the ER, and the perinuclear space is continuous with the ER lumen. A nucleus should therefore never be drawn as a sealed bag floating alone in the cytoplasm — a patch of ribosome-studded ER belongs merging into its surface.

Piercing both membranes wherever they fuse are the **nuclear pore complexes (NPCs)**: roughly octagonal (eight-fold symmetric) protein rings, among the largest macromolecular machines in the cell (100–120 nm outer diameter, tens of megadaltons, built from ~30 different nucleoporins present in multiples of eight). A typical vertebrate nucleus carries on the order of **2,000–4,000 of them — genuinely thousands** — scattered fairly evenly over the whole envelope, forming the sole regulated gateway for traffic between nucleus and cytoplasm: mRNA and tRNA and ribosomal subunits go out, histones and polymerases and gene-regulatory proteins come in.

Just inside the inner membrane lies the **nuclear lamina**, a thin meshwork of lamin intermediate filaments that mechanically supports the envelope, anchors the pore complexes, and tethers chromatin. Inside, the genome fills the nucleoplasm as **chromatin** in two visually and functionally distinct states: dense, transcriptionally silent **heterochromatin** clumps against the lamina at the nuclear periphery (and rims the nucleolus), reading dark/electron-dense in TEM because of its packing density; loose, transcriptionally active **euchromatin** disperses through the nuclear interior and around the pores, reading pale and open. One or more rounded, **membrane-less nucleoli** sit in the nucleoplasm — denser and more uniform than the surrounding chromatin, organised internally into a fibrillar centre, dense fibrillar component and granular component — where ribosomal RNA genes are transcribed and ribosome subunits are assembled before export through the pores.

In thin-section **TEM** the envelope reads as a smooth, gently curved outline (the two membranes often merge visually into one contour at moderate magnification; only at high magnification do they resolve into two separate lines with a pale gap between), interrupted by pore profiles, enclosing a mottled interior of dark heterochromatin against paler euchromatin, with one or more denser rounded nucleolar patches, and — where the section catches it — a seam of ribosome-studded rough ER running up to and merging with the outer membrane.

The nucleus is **colourless in life** and TEM is greyscale, so all colour in these plates is teaching convention. The palette used consistently across all four styles here: two related blues for the outer/inner envelope membranes so the double structure reads clearly, warm amber for the pore rings, deep dusty purple for peripheral heterochromatin against lighter lavender euchromatin, warm terracotta-orange for the nucleolus, and the same cool grey-blue used for the Golgi apparatus plate in this set for the continuing rough ER.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **No single-membrane envelope.** The nuclear envelope is a **double** membrane (inner + outer, with a perinuclear space between) all the way around — this is the single most important thing to get right and the most common error to avoid.
- **No membrane around the nucleolus.** It has none; it is a denser, more uniform condensation inside the nucleoplasm, not a walled compartment.
- **No condensed, X-shaped (metaphase) chromosomes inside an intact nucleus.** That form exists only during mitosis, after the envelope has broken down. Interphase chromatin is a diffuse fibrous/granular mass — dense heterochromatin at the rim, loose euchromatin centrally — never distinct paired-chromatid X shapes.
- **Not a bacterial nucleoid.** No naked DNA loop in a cell with a wall, capsule, flagella or pili — the nucleus is a membrane-bound eukaryotic organelle; prokaryotes have no true nucleus.
- **No plant cell wall or chloroplast** around it — this is a generic eukaryotic organelle plate.
- **Not a sealed, isolated bag.** The outer membrane is continuous with the rough ER; at least one patch of ribosome-studded ER should merge into the envelope.
- **No pores drawn as simple round holes with no ring structure**, and not just a handful of them — there are thousands, each an octagonal protein ring, evenly scattered over the whole surface.
- **No anthropomorphising** in the scientific plates (no face, eyes or limbs — that belongs only on the colouring page), and **no baked-in text, letters, numbers, labels, scale bars, arrows or watermarks**.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The nucleus is the one organelle almost every cell in your body has exactly one of, and it is easy to underestimate just how carefully engineered its boundary is. It isn't a single bag — it's a double membrane, two separate lipid layers with a slim fluid-filled gap between them, and that outer layer flows directly into the endoplasmic reticulum, the cell's protein-folding factory. Piercing both layers wherever they meet are thousands of nuclear pore complexes, among the most intricate protein machines your cells build: eight-sided rings that let RNA and ribosome parts out and reel proteins that belong inside back in, while keeping the DNA itself firmly inside. That control matters because the genome doesn't get read wholesale. Only the stretches of DNA a cell actually needs get copied into RNA at any given moment — which is the entire reason a nerve cell and a skin cell, carrying identical DNA, end up doing completely different jobs. Look closely and you'll see the genome isn't uniformly packed either: tightly wound, switched-off regions hug the inner wall, while loosely open, active regions sit toward the centre where the reading machinery can reach them. And in a corner of that same room a dense little body called the nucleolus works constantly, producing the ribosomal machinery every cell needs to translate those copied instructions into actual proteins. A few cells break the one-nucleus rule for good reason: mature red blood cells eject theirs entirely to make room for oxygen-carrying haemoglobin, while skeletal muscle fibres fuse so many cells together during development that a single fibre can carry hundreds of nuclei.  
🇩🇪 Der Zellkern ist das eine Organell, von dem fast jede Zelle in deinem Körper genau eines besitzt, und man unterschätzt leicht, wie sorgfältig konstruiert seine Grenze eigentlich ist. Es ist kein einfacher Sack — es ist eine Doppelmembran, zwei getrennte Lipidschichten mit einem schmalen, flüssigkeitsgefüllten Spalt dazwischen, und die äußere Schicht geht direkt in das endoplasmatische Retikulum über, die Faltungsfabrik der Zelle für Proteine. Beide Schichten werden dort, wo sie sich treffen, von Tausenden Kernporenkomplexen durchbrochen, einigen der kompliziertesten Proteinmaschinen, die deine Zellen bauen: achteckige Ringe, die RNA und Ribosomenbausteine hinauslassen und Proteine, die ins Innere gehören, wieder hereinholen — während die DNA selbst fest im Inneren bleibt. Diese Kontrolle ist wichtig, denn das Erbgut wird nicht am Stück abgelesen. Nur die Abschnitte der DNA, die eine Zelle gerade tatsächlich braucht, werden zu einem gegebenen Zeitpunkt in RNA kopiert — genau deshalb erledigen eine Nervenzelle und eine Hautzelle, obwohl sie identische DNA tragen, am Ende völlig unterschiedliche Aufgaben. Schaut man genauer hin, ist das Erbgut auch nicht gleichmäßig verpackt: eng gewundene, stillgelegte Abschnitte liegen dicht an der Innenwand, während locker geöffnete, aktive Abschnitte eher in der Mitte liegen, dort, wo die Ablesemaschinerie hinkommt. Ein paar Zellen brechen die Ein-Kern-Regel aus gutem Grund: reife rote Blutkörperchen werfen ihren Kern vollständig ab, um Platz für sauerstofftragendes Hämoglobin zu schaffen, während Skelettmuskelfasern während der Entwicklung so viele Zellen miteinander verschmelzen, dass eine einzelne Faser Hunderte von Zellkernen tragen kann.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The nucleus is bounded by a double membrane, the nuclear envelope, comprising an outer nuclear membrane (ONM, continuous with and functionally equivalent to rough ER, ribosome-studded) and an inner nuclear membrane (INM, lined on its nucleoplasmic face by the lamin-based nuclear lamina), separated by a ~20–50 nm perinuclear space that is topologically continuous with the ER lumen. ONM and INM are chemically distinct but fuse at every nuclear pore complex (NPC), an ~100–120 nm, eight-fold symmetric assembly of ~30 nucleoporins (mass on the order of tens of MDa) that mediates all regulated nucleocytoplasmic exchange: passive diffusion of small solutes, facilitated diffusion of ions and small proteins, and signal- and carrier-mediated transport (importins/exportins, RanGTP gradient) of large cargo including mRNP export and ribosomal-subunit export. A typical vertebrate somatic nucleus carries on the order of 2,000–4,000 NPCs. Chromatin occupies the nucleoplasm in two principal states distinguishable by packing density and transcriptional competence: heterochromatin, condensed and largely silent, is preferentially tethered to the nuclear lamina (lamina-associated domains, LADs) and rims the nucleolus; euchromatin, decondensed and transcriptionally permissive, occupies the interior and the perinuclear-pore-proximal zone, facilitating export of nascent transcripts. The nucleolus is a non-membrane-bound, phase-separated nuclear body organised into fibrillar centre, dense fibrillar component and granular component, assembled around nucleolar organiser regions (rDNA repeats); it is the site of RNA polymerase I-driven pre-rRNA transcription, rRNA processing, and pre-ribosomal subunit assembly, with additional roles in stress signalling and non-ribosomal RNP maturation. Selective, compartmentalised transcription — only the loci a given cell type requires are engaged at any time — together with regulated nuclear export underlies cell-type-specific gene expression from an invariant genome.  
🇩🇪 Der Zellkern ist von einer Doppelmembran umgeben, der Kernhülle, bestehend aus einer äußeren Kernmembran (ONM, kontinuierlich mit dem rauen ER und funktionell diesem gleichzusetzen, ribosomenbesetzt) und einer inneren Kernmembran (INM, an ihrer nukleoplasmatischen Seite von der Lamin-basierten Kernlamina ausgekleidet), getrennt durch einen etwa 20–50 nm breiten perinukleären Raum, der topologisch mit dem Lumen des ER durchgängig ist. ONM und INM sind chemisch unterschiedlich, verschmelzen aber an jedem Kernporenkomplex (NPC), einer rund 100–120 nm großen, achtzählig symmetrischen Baugruppe aus etwa 30 Nukleoporinen (Masse im Bereich mehrerer zehn MDa), die den gesamten regulierten Austausch zwischen Kern und Zytoplasma vermittelt: passive Diffusion kleiner gelöster Stoffe, erleichterte Diffusion von Ionen und kleinen Proteinen sowie signal- und trägervermittelten Transport (Importine/Exportine, RanGTP-Gradient) größerer Fracht, einschließlich des Exports von mRNP-Partikeln und ribosomalen Untereinheiten. Ein typischer somatischer Wirbeltierkern trägt in der Größenordnung von 2.000–4.000 NPCs. Chromatin nimmt das Nukleoplasma in zwei Hauptzuständen ein, die sich durch Packungsdichte und Transkriptionskompetenz unterscheiden: Heterochromatin, kondensiert und größtenteils stillgelegt, ist bevorzugt an die Kernlamina gebunden (laminaassoziierte Domänen, LADs) und säumt den Nukleolus; Euchromatin, dekondensiert und transkriptionell zugänglich, besetzt das Innere und die kernporennahe Zone, was den Export naszierender Transkripte erleichtert. Der Nukleolus ist ein nicht membranumhülltes, phasensepariertes Kernkörperchen, gegliedert in Fibrillarzentrum, dichte fibrilläre Komponente und granuläre Komponente, organisiert um die nukleolären Organisatorregionen (rDNA-Wiederholungen); er ist der Ort der durch RNA-Polymerase I angetriebenen prä-rRNA-Transkription, der rRNA-Prozessierung und der Vormontage ribosomaler Untereinheiten, mit zusätzlichen Funktionen in der Stresssignalgebung und der Reifung nicht-ribosomaler RNPs. Selektive, kompartimentierte Transkription — zu jedem Zeitpunkt sind nur die Genorte aktiv, die der jeweilige Zelltyp benötigt — begründet zusammen mit regulierbarem Kernexport die zelltypspezifische Genexpression aus einem invarianten Genom.
