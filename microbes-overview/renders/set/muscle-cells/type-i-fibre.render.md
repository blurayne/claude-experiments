# Type I fibre (slow-twitch) — render log

**Set:** `muscle-cells` · **Microbe key:** `type-i-fibre`
**Short description:** A slow-twitch, oxidative skeletal muscle fibre built for endurance: a single unbranched, cylindrical cell running centimetres long (~40–60 µm across, up to tens of centimetres long — the largest human cell), with hundreds of nuclei flattened at the PERIPHERY just under the sarcolemma, regular cross-striations (A/I bands, Z-disc to Z-disc sarcomeres), and dense packing of mitochondria and myoglobin that give it a deep red colour and an essentially inexhaustible aerobic ATP supply. Fed by a rich surrounding capillary bed and driven by a small, easily-excited motor neuron, it is recruited first for almost any movement and dominates postural muscle such as the soleus.

## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

A **skeletal muscle fibre** is a single, giant multinucleated cell formed during development by the fusion of many myoblasts end to end into one continuous tube — which is why it can run the **entire length of a muscle**, from a few millimetres in a small muscle to tens of centimetres in a long one (e.g. the sartorius); it is **unbranched and cylindrical**, roughly **40–60 µm in diameter**, and by length it is the largest cell in the human body. **Type I** ("slow-twitch", "red", oxidative) is one of the physiologically distinct fibre types found within this single cell category — distinguished not by a different cell plan but by which myosin heavy-chain isoform it expresses (MyHC I) and by how it is built to generate ATP.

The defining, most checkable structural fact is **nuclear position**: because the fibre is built by fusing many individual myoblasts, it keeps **hundreds of nuclei**, one contributed by roughly each founder cell, and every one of them stays **flattened against the inside of the sarcolemma at the fibre's PERIPHERY**, immediately under the plasma membrane. A nucleus sitting in the middle of the fibre is never correct for a normal mature skeletal fibre — that is either the signature of a *different* cell type (a cardiomyocyte, whose one or two nuclei sit centrally) or, in skeletal muscle specifically, a red flag for a **regenerating** fibre (a fibre repaired after injury transiently carries a central nucleus before the nucleus migrates back out to the periphery as the fibre matures).

The cytoplasm (sarcoplasm) is packed with **myofibrils**, bundles of the contractile rod built from serially repeated **sarcomeres**, each running **Z-disc to Z-disc**. Actin (thin) and myosin (thick) filaments interdigitate and produce the fibre's cross-striation: the darker **A band** (the full length of the myosin filaments, with a paler H-zone and central M-line inside it) alternates with the lighter **I band** (thin actin filaments only, on either side of the Z-disc where they anchor). Excitation reaches the interior through **T-tubules**, deep tubular invaginations of the sarcolemma; in *skeletal* muscle (unlike cardiac) these sit at the **A–I junction**, two per sarcomere, and each T-tubule is flanked by two terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum to form a **triad** (cardiac muscle instead has one T-tubule per sarcomere at the Z-disc, forming a dyad). Depolarisation is communicated directly, mechanically, to the ryanodine receptor via the T-tubule's DHP receptor (voltage sensor), releasing Ca²⁺ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to trigger contraction — skeletal muscle contraction does not require an external Ca²⁺ influx the way cardiac muscle does.

What makes this fibre **Type I** rather than Type II is its metabolic build. It is dense with **mitochondria**, both in a subsarcolemmal rim just under the membrane and packed in interfibrillar rows between the myofibrils — markedly more than in a Type IIx fibre — and it is rich in **myoglobin**, the oxygen-binding, oxygen-storing pigment that (together with the mitochondrial density) gives the fibre its characteristic **deep red colour** — the reason Type I muscle is called "red muscle" and Type IIx "white muscle". It is served by a noticeably **denser surrounding capillary network** than a glycolytic fibre, matching its high, sustained oxygen demand, and it burns fat and glucose oxidatively for a slow-onset but essentially inexhaustible ATP supply, resisting fatigue far longer than a Type II fibre can. Its myosin ATPase is the "slow" isoform (histochemically it stains dark at acid pH, pale at alkaline pH — the reverse of Type II — which is the classical basis of ATPase fibre-typing). It has a comparatively **small motor unit**: a small, easily-excited alpha motor neuron innervates relatively few Type I fibres, and by Henneman's size principle these small motor units are recruited **first**, for almost any voluntary contraction — postural holding, walking, a light grip — before larger, fast motor units are added as force demand rises. Type I fibres dominate postural, endurance-oriented muscle such as the soleus; the balance between fibre types in a given muscle is set partly by genetics and can be shifted somewhat by endurance training.

Scattered between the sarcolemma and its surrounding basal lamina sit **satellite cells** — small, quiescent, mononuclear muscle stem cells. A mature fibre's own many nuclei are post-mitotic and cannot divide to repair damage; instead, satellite cells activate on injury, proliferate, and fuse into (or alongside) the existing fibre to repair or regenerate it, which is also how the peripheral-vs-central nuclear position becomes a maturity marker (see above).

**What NOT to draw / do NOT confuse this with:** this is skeletal muscle, not cardiac. It must never show intercalated discs, branching, or a small number of central nuclei — those are cardiomyocyte features (see the `cardiomyocyte` entry in the `heart-cells` set), and a central nucleus is the single most common way this subject gets drawn wrong. It is also not smooth muscle (no striations there at all). Within the fast/slow spectrum, this is the fibre's *oxidative, red* end — it must not be drawn pale/white or mitochondria-sparse, which is the visual signature of its glycolytic sibling, the Type IIx fibre (rendered separately in this same set), nor a short stubby cell that fits entirely inside the frame — a real fibre is centimetres long and must run out of frame at both ends.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **NO central nucleus.** This is the single most common and most checkable error — a nucleus (or few nuclei) in the MIDDLE of the fibre reads as cardiac muscle or a regenerating fibre, never a mature Type I fibre. Nuclei must be numerous and ALL pressed against the periphery, just under the sarcolemma.
- **NOT branched, and NO intercalated discs.** Those are cardiomyocyte features. This fibre is a single straight, unbranched cylinder.
- **NOT a short, stubby cell that fits entirely inside the frame.** A real fibre is centimetres long; compose it running diagonally past both edges of the frame so its unbroken, unbranched length reads clearly, not as a capsule floating in empty space.
- **NOT pale, white, or mitochondria-sparse.** That is the glycolytic Type IIx fibre (a sibling subject in this same set). This fibre must read as visibly DEEP RED with densely packed mitochondria; if it looks anywhere close to white/pale pink and sparse inside, it has drifted into the wrong fibre type.
- **NO smooth, unstriated stretches.** Cross-striations (A/I bands, Z-discs) must be regular and visible along the whole visible length, in register across neighbouring myofibrils.
- **NO sparse or absent capillary network** — Type I fibres are unusually well perfused; show a visibly denser capillary presence around the fibre than a token vessel or two.
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids, flagella or cilia** — this is an animal cell, not a bacterium or protist.
- **T-tubules, if shown, sit at the A–I junction (two per sarcomere)**, not at the Z-disc — a Z-disc T-tubule is the cardiac arrangement.
- One clearly dominant fibre as the subject; neighbouring fibres may appear cropped at the frame edge for context but must not turn the image into an unreadable tissue block.

**Honest caveats.**
1. This is a **section (a teased histological preparation), not a single free-standing isolated cell** — several fibres are visible side by side, though the teasing technique means each one is close to individually resolvable, unlike a dense tissue block.
2. **Plain H&E does not distinguish fibre type.** Nothing in this image identifies any specific fibre as Type I versus Type II — that distinction requires a histochemical stain (myosin ATPase at defined pH, producing the classic light/dark "checkerboard" pattern across a cross-section) or immunohistochemistry against a specific myosin heavy-chain isoform. A search for a freely-licensed (PD/CC0/CC BY/CC BY-SA, non-pathological) fibre-type-stained cross-section mosaic did not turn up a usable candidate: the two ATPase-stained Commons candidates found (`ATPase_targetoid_fibers_neurogenic_muscle_atrophy.jpg`, `Fasertypendysproportion.jpg`) are both **pathological** specimens (neurogenic atrophy and congenital fibre-type disproportion respectively) and would misrepresent normal Type I fibre morphology if used as the reference; two PMC figures with a clean checkerboard pattern (PMC2278719, PMC4541118) carry non-CC copyright/unclear licensing. This image is therefore used to verify the two structural facts that DO carry over to every skeletal fibre regardless of type — striation and peripheral nuclei — while the type-specific colour and mitochondrial density are sourced from the literature (§1) rather than from this micrograph.
