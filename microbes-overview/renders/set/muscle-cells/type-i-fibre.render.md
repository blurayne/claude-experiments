# Type I fibre (slow-twitch) — render log

**Set:** `muscle-cells` · **Microbe key:** `type-i-fibre`
**Short description:** A slow-twitch, oxidative skeletal muscle fibre built for endurance: a single unbranched, cylindrical cell running centimetres long (~40–60 µm across, up to tens of centimetres long — the largest human cell), with hundreds of nuclei flattened at the PERIPHERY just under the sarcolemma, regular cross-striations (A/I bands, Z-disc to Z-disc sarcomeres), and dense packing of mitochondria and myoglobin that give it a deep red colour and an essentially inexhaustible aerobic ATP supply. ## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

A **skeletal muscle fibre** is a single, giant multinucleated cell formed during development by the fusion of many myoblasts end to end into one continuous tube — which is why it can run the **entire length of a muscle**, from a few millimetres in a small muscle to tens of centimetres in a long one (e.g. the sartorius); it is **unbranched and cylindrical**, roughly **40–60 µm in diameter**, and by length it is the largest cell in the human body. **Type I** ("slow-twitch", "red", oxidative) is one of the physiologically distinct fibre types found within this single cell category — distinguished not by a different cell plan but by which myosin heavy-chain isoform it expresses (MyHC I) and by how it is built to generate ATP.

The defining, most checkable structural fact is **nuclear position**: because the fibre is built by fusing many individual myoblasts, it keeps **hundreds of nuclei**, one contributed by roughly each founder cell, and every one of them stays **flattened against the inside of the sarcolemma at the fibre's PERIPHERY**, immediately under the plasma membrane. A nucleus sitting in the middle of the fibre is never correct for a normal mature skeletal fibre — that is either the signature of a *different* cell type (a cardiomyocyte, whose one or two nuclei sit centrally) or, in skeletal muscle specifically, a red flag for a **regenerating** fibre (a fibre repaired after injury transiently carries a central nucleus before the nucleus migrates back out to the periphery as the fibre matures).

The cytoplasm (sarcoplasm) is packed with **myofibrils**, bundles of the contractile rod built from serially repeated **sarcomeres**, each running **Z-disc to Z-disc**. Actin (thin) and myosin (thick) filaments interdigitate and produce the fibre's cross-striation: the darker **A band** (the full length of the myosin filaments, with a paler H-zone and central M-line inside it) alternates with the lighter **I band** (thin actin filaments only, on either side of the Z-disc where they anchor). Excitation reaches the interior through **T-tubules**, deep tubular invaginations of the sarcolemma; in *skeletal* muscle (unlike cardiac) these sit at the **A–I junction**, two per sarcomere, and each T-tubule is flanked by two terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum to form a **triad** (cardiac muscle instead has one T-tubule per sarcomere at the Z-disc, forming a dyad). Depolarisation is communicated directly, mechanically, to the ryanodine receptor via the T-tubule's DHP receptor (voltage sensor), releasing Ca²⁺ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to trigger contraction — skeletal muscle contraction does not require an external Ca²⁺ influx the way cardiac muscle does.

What makes this fibre **Type I** rather than Type II is its metabolic build. It is dense with **mitochondria**, both in a subsarcolemmal rim just under the membrane and packed in interfibrillar rows between the myofibrils — markedly more than in a Type IIx fibre — and it is rich in **myoglobin**, the oxygen-binding, oxygen-storing pigment that (together with the mitochondrial density) gives the fibre its characteristic **deep red colour** — the reason Type I muscle is called "red muscle" and Type IIx "white muscle". It is served by a noticeably **denser surrounding capillary network** than a glycolytic fibre, matching its high, sustained oxygen demand, and it burns fat and glucose oxidatively for a slow-onset but essentially inexhaustible ATP supply, resisting fatigue far longer than a Type II fibre can. Its myosin ATPase is the "slow" isoform (histochemically it stains dark at acid pH, pale at alkaline pH — the reverse of Type II — which is the classical basis of ATPase fibre-typing). It has a comparatively **small motor unit**: a small, easily-excited alpha motor neuron innervates relatively few Type I fibres, and by Henneman's size principle these small motor units are recruited **first**, for almost any voluntary contraction — postural holding, walking, a light grip — before larger, fast motor units are added as force demand rises. Type I fibres dominate postural, endurance-oriented muscle such as the soleus; the balance between fibre types in a given muscle is set partly by genetics and can be shifted somewhat by endurance training.

Scattered between the sarcolemma and its surrounding basal lamina sit **satellite cells** — small, quiescent, mononuclear muscle stem cells. A mature fibre's own many nuclei are post-mitotic and cannot divide to repair damage; instead, satellite cells activate on injury, proliferate, and fuse into (or alongside) the existing fibre to repair or regenerate it, which is also how the peripheral-vs-central nuclear position becomes a maturity marker (see above).

**What NOT to draw / do NOT confuse this with:** this is skeletal muscle, not cardiac. It must never show intercalated discs, branching, or a small number of central nuclei — those are cardiomyocyte features (see the `cardiomyocyte` entry in the `heart-cells` set), and a central nucleus is the single most common way this subject gets drawn wrong. It is also not smooth muscle (no striations there at all). Within the fast/slow spectrum, this is the fibre's *oxidative, red* end — it must not be drawn pale/white or mitochondria-sparse, which is the visual signature of its glycolytic sibling, the Type IIx fibre (rendered separately in this same set), nor a short stubby cell that fits entirely inside the frame — a real fibre is centimetres long and must run out of frame at both ends.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **NO central nucleus.** This is the single most common and most checkable error — a nucleus (or few nuclei) in the MIDDLE of the fibre reads as cardiac muscle or a regenerating fibre, never a mature Type I fibre. Nuclei must be numerous and ALL pressed against the periphery, just under the sarcolemma.
- **NOT branched, and NO intercalated discs.** Those are cardiomyocyte features. This fibre is a single straight, unbranched cylinder.
- **NOT a short, stubby cell that fits entirely inside the frame.** A real fibre is centimetres long; compose it running diagonally past both edges of the frame so its unbroken, unbranched length reads clearly, not as a capsule floating in empty space.
- **NOT pale, white, or mitochondria-sparse.** That is the glycolytic Type IIx fibre (a sibling subject in this same set). This fibre must read as visibly DEEP RED with densely packed mitochondria; if it looks anywhere close to white/pale pink and sparse inside, it has drifted into the wrong fibre type.
- **NO smooth, unstriated stretches.** Cross-striations (A/I bands, Z-discs) must be regular and visible along the whole visible length, in register across neighbouring myofibrils.
- **NO sparse or absent capillary network** — Type I fibres are unusually well perfused; show a visibly denser capillary presence around the fibre than a token vessel or two.
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids, flagella or cilia** — this is an animal cell, not a bacterium or protist.
- **T-tubules, if shown, sit at the A–I junction (two per sarcomere)**, not at the Z-disc — a Z-disc T-tubule is the cardiac arrangement.
- One clearly dominant fibre as the subject; neighbouring fibres may appear cropped at the frame edge for context but must not turn the image into an unreadable tissue block.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The Type I muscle fibre is the endurance specialist of skeletal muscle, and its whole design serves one goal: staying active for a long time without running out of fuel. Structurally it is like any skeletal fibre — a single, unbranched cell stretched the length of a muscle, carrying hundreds of nuclei all displaced to the periphery, just under its outer membrane, rather than clustered anywhere in the middle. What sets Type I apart is what fills the space that frees up: an unusually high density of mitochondria, packed both in a rim beneath the membrane and in rows between the contractile filaments, plus abundant myoglobin, the oxygen-storing pigment that gives the fibre its deep red colour and lends its old name, 'red muscle'. A denser capillary network than in faster fibre types keeps that oxygen supply flowing continuously. The trade-off for all that stamina is force and speed: Type I fibres contract slowly and generate relatively little force per fibre compared with their fast, glycolytic Type II relatives, but they resist fatigue for far longer. Because Type I fibres sit on small motor units served by small, easily excitable motor neurons, the nervous system recruits them first for almost any contraction — standing, walking, holding posture — only calling in the more powerful but quickly-tiring Type II fibres once demand rises. Muscles built around sustained, low-intensity work, such as the soleus in the calf, are dominated by Type I fibres; muscles built for sudden, forceful movement lean fast-twitch instead. The ratio between fibre types in a given muscle is set largely by genetics — part of why some people are natural distance runners and others natural sprinters — but endurance training can push some intermediate fast fibres toward a more oxidative profile, closing part of that gap.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The Type I (slow-twitch, oxidative) skeletal muscle fibre is defined by expression of the slow myosin heavy chain isoform (MyHC I / MYH7) and by a metabolic and structural profile optimised for sustained, fatigue-resistant force production at low contraction velocity. Like all skeletal myofibres it is a single post-mitotic, multinucleated cell (a syncytium formed by embryonic myoblast fusion), unbranched and cylindrical, ~40-60 um in diameter and up to tens of centimetres long, with myonuclei displaced to the subsarcolemmal periphery throughout its length — a central nucleus in mature skeletal muscle instead marks a regenerating fibre, whose nucleus migrates peripherally as it matures. Type I fibres carry a markedly higher mitochondrial volume density than Type II fibres, arranged in subsarcolemmal and interfibrillar populations, together with elevated myoglobin content, which together produce the fibre's characteristic red colour and its high oxidative capacity; a correspondingly denser surrounding capillary network supports the associated oxygen flux. ATP is supplied predominantly via oxidative phosphorylation of fatty acids and glucose, giving slow cross-bridge cycling (low myosin ATPase activity, classically demonstrated histochemically by dark staining at acid pH and light staining at alkaline pH — the inverse of Type II) but very high fatigue resistance. Type I fibres are innervated by small-diameter, low-threshold alpha motor neurons forming small motor units; per Henneman's size principle these are recruited first as contractile demand increases, before larger, fast motor units are engaged. Fibre-type proportion varies by muscle (postural/endurance muscles such as soleus are Type I-dominant) and by individual, with a substantial genetic component; chronic endurance training can shift the intermediate Type IIx/IIa population toward a more oxidative phenotype, though interconversion between Type I and Type II is limited in adult human muscle under physiological training stimuli. Quiescent satellite cells, mononuclear muscle stem cells resident between the sarcolemma and basal lamina, provide the capacity for fibre repair and regeneration that the post-mitotic myonuclei themselves cannot.  
🇩🇪 Die Typ-I-Skelettmuskelfaser (langsam zuckend, oxidativ) ist definiert durch die Expression der langsamen Myosin-Schwerketten-Isoform (MyHC I / MYH7) und durch ein metabolisches und strukturelles Profil, das auf anhaltende, ermüdungsresistente Kraftentwicklung bei niedriger Kontraktionsgeschwindigkeit ausgelegt ist. Wie jede Skelettmuskelfaser ist sie eine einzelne postmitotische, vielkernige Zelle (ein durch Fusion embryonaler Myoblasten entstandenes Synzytium), unverzweigt und zylindrisch, etwa 40-60 µm im Durchmesser und bis zu mehreren Dutzend Zentimetern lang, mit Myonuklei, die über die gesamte Länge subsarkolemmal an den Rand verdrängt sind — ein zentraler Zellkern markiert in reifer Skelettmuskulatur dagegen eine regenerierende Faser, deren Kern erst im Zuge der Reifung an die Peripherie wandert. Typ-I-Fasern weisen eine deutlich höhere mitochondriale Volumendichte auf als Typ-II-Fasern, angeordnet in subsarkolemmalen und interfibrillären Populationen, zusammen mit erhöhtem Myoglobingehalt, was gemeinsam die charakteristische rote Farbe der Faser und ihre hohe oxidative Kapazität erzeugt; ein entsprechend dichteres umgebendes Kapillarnetz unterstützt den zugehörigen Sauerstofffluss. ATP wird überwiegend durch oxidative Phosphorylierung von Fettsäuren und Glukose bereitgestellt, was einen langsamen Querbrücken-Zyklus ergibt (niedrige Myosin-ATPase-Aktivität, klassischerweise histochemisch durch dunkle Anfärbung bei saurem und helle Anfärbung bei alkalischem pH-Wert nachgewiesen — umgekehrt zu Typ II) bei gleichzeitig sehr hoher Ermüdungsresistenz. Typ-I-Fasern werden von dünnen, niedrigschwelligen Alpha-Motoneuronen innerviert, die kleine motorische Einheiten bilden; nach dem Größenprinzip (Henneman) werden diese bei steigendem Kraftbedarf zuerst rekrutiert, bevor größere, schnelle motorische Einheiten hinzugeschaltet werden. Der Fasertypanteil variiert je nach Muskel (Haltungs- und Ausdauermuskeln wie der Soleus sind Typ-I-dominiert) und je nach Person, mit einer erheblichen genetischen Komponente; chronisches Ausdauertraining kann die intermediäre Typ-IIx/IIa-Population in Richtung eines oxidativeren Phänotyps verschieben, wobei die Umwandlung zwischen Typ I und Typ II in erwachsener menschlicher Muskulatur unter physiologischen Trainingsreizen begrenzt bleibt. Ruhende Satellitenzellen, einkernige Muskelstammzellen zwischen Sarkolemm und Basallamina, stellen die Fähigkeit zur Faserreparatur und -regeneration bereit, die die postmitotischen Myonuklei selbst nicht besitzen.
