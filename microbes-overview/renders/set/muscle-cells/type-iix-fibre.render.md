## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The **Type IIx fibre** is the fastest and most purely glycolytic of the three physiological types of human skeletal muscle fibre (Type I, Type IIa, Type IIx). Like every skeletal muscle fibre it is a **single, unbranched, cylindrical multinucleated cell** — a syncytium formed during development by the fusion of many myoblasts — running the **full length of the muscle or of a long fascicle within it**: centimetres long in an adult limb muscle, far longer than it is wide, so in any single frame it must run off the edges rather than sit as a short self-contained sausage. Of the three human fibre types it has the **largest diameter**, roughly 70–90 µm in a trained adult (Type I and IIa are narrower), which is one of the reasons it develops the most force per fibre.

Its defining visible feature, shared with every skeletal fibre and the single most important thing to get right in a render, is regular **transverse cross-striation**: alternating dark **A bands** (the full length of the myosin thick filaments) and light **I bands** (thin actin filaments only, bisected by the dense **Z-disc**), repeating along the whole fibre as a stack of **sarcomeres**, each running Z-disc to Z-disc (~2–2.5 µm at rest). The banding of neighbouring myofibrils lines up in register across the fibre, which is what gives the whole cell its striped look under the light microscope and is the feature every "do NOT draw" item below is protecting.

The second defining, and most commonly bungled, feature is nuclear position. A skeletal fibre carries **many nuclei — dozens to hundreds along its length** — and every one of them sits **flattened just beneath the sarcolemma, at the cell's periphery**, oriented with their long axis parallel to the fibre. A **single central nucleus is the single most reliable "wrong answer" signature**: it means the artist has drawn a cardiomyocyte (which is mononucleate with one central nucleus) or a regenerating/immature fibre, not a mature skeletal fibre. Count the nuclei on every render and confirm each one hugs the rim, none sit mid-cross-section.

What sets Type IIx apart from its two siblings is entirely metabolic and colour-related, not structural. It is built for a short, powerful burst rather than endurance: **sparse mitochondria** (visibly fewer and smaller than in Type I or IIa, clustered thinly, not filling the interfibrillar space), **low myoglobin**, giving the fibre a **pale pink-cream colour** rather than the deep red of Type I — pale, not colourless or grey, since it is still living pink-cream tissue, just the lightest of the three types — and a **thin surrounding capillary network**, sparser than the dense capillary bed around an oxidative Type I fibre, matching its low oxygen demand. In its place the fibre stores **abundant glycogen granules** scattered through the sarcoplasm between the myofibrils, its main fuel reserve for rapid anaerobic glycolysis. It is driven by the largest, fastest motor neurons in the pool, recruited last and only for maximal effort (heavy lifting, jumping, sprinting), and fatigues within seconds under sustained load — the trade for its top speed and peak force.

**The naming point.** Older ATPase histochemistry, working from three pH-dependent staining reactions, called the fastest human fibres "Type IIb" by analogy with rodent muscle. Molecularly this was a misnomer: the fastest fibre in adult human limb muscle expresses **myosin heavy chain IIx (MYH1)**, not **MYH4**, which is the gene that actually encodes true IIb myosin. True IIb/MYH4 fibres are real and common in mice and rats, but they are essentially absent from healthy adult human limb muscle (Smerdu et al. 1994, *Am J Physiol* 267(6 Pt 1):C1723-8, first showed by in-situ hybridisation and immunohistochemistry that the fibres histochemically typed as "IIB" in human muscle express MYH1/IIx mRNA and protein, not MYH4/IIb; Schiaffino & Reggiani 2011, *Physiol Rev* 91(4):1447-1531, is the standard review confirming the myosin heavy chain isoform map across species and formally retiring "human IIb" as a species-mismatched label). So: **this fibre should never be labelled "Type IIb"** as if that were merely an older synonym for the same human cell — IIb is a distinct, real fibre type, just not a human one. In plain words: the fast fibre used to get a name borrowed from mouse muscle before anyone had checked which actual motor protein it makes; once someone checked, it turned out to make the "x" version, not the mouse "b" version, so the "b" label was retired for humans (some clinical histochemistry labs still report "IIB" out of habit, meaning IIx).

Sources: [Smerdu et al. 1994, *Am J Physiol* 267(6 Pt 1):C1723-8 — "Type IIx myosin heavy chain transcripts are expressed in Type IIb fibers of human skeletal muscle"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7545970/), [Schiaffino & Reggiani 2011, *Physiol Rev* 91(4):1447-1531 — "Fiber Types in Mammalian Skeletal Muscles"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8262285/) (open review, DOI 10.1152/physrev.00031.2010), [Wikipedia — Myosin heavy chain / skeletal muscle fiber types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle_contraction), [OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e — 10.5 Types of Muscle Fibers](https://open.oregonstate.education/anatomy2e/chapter/types-muscle-fibers/), [Pette & Staron 2000, *Microsc Res Tech* 50(6):500-9 — "Myosin isoforms, muscle fiber types, and transitions"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10998639/).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **NO single central nucleus.** That is the cardiomyocyte signature. This fibre has MANY nuclei, and every one sits flattened at the periphery, against the sarcolemma — never floating mid-cross-section.
- **Not branched, not Y-shaped, no intercalated discs.** Those are cardiac muscle features. This is a plain, unbranched cylinder running out of frame at both ends.
- **Not a short stubby sausage that fits entirely inside the frame.** A muscle fibre is centimetres long; compose it running diagonally off two opposite edges so its true proportions (far longer than wide) read even though the whole length cannot fit.
- **No dense red colour and no packed rows of large mitochondria.** That profile belongs to Type I (or to cardiac muscle). Keep mitochondria visibly sparse and small, and keep the fibre pale pink-cream, not deep red.
- **Not colourless, grey, or monochrome.** "Pale" means pale pink-cream living tissue, not a desaturated or greyscale render — the palette must still read as biological, just lighter than a Type I fibre.
- **No smooth, unstriated surface anywhere along the fibre.** Cross-striations (A/I banding) must be continuous and in register the whole visible length; a smooth patch reads as smooth muscle, which is wrong here.
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids, flagella or cilia** — this is an animal cell, not a bacterium or a protist.
- **No motor end plate drawn as a large obvious structure dominating the composition** — a neuromuscular junction may appear small and subordinate at most one end, but the fibre itself is the subject.
- **Never label anything "Type IIb"** as though it were simply this fibre's other name. Human IIx and rodent IIb are molecularly distinct (MYH1 vs MYH4); IIb is essentially absent from adult human limb muscle. If a legacy "IIb" name needs to appear at all (it does not need to, for this teaching set), it must be flagged as a historical human-muscle misnomer for IIx, not presented as an equivalent synonym.
- One clearly dominant fibre as the subject of the composition; at most a couple of neighbouring fibres, subordinate and cropped by the frame, so the frame does not turn into an unreadable cross-section thicket.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The Type IIx fibre is the fastest, most purely glycolytic of the three physiologically distinct human skeletal muscle fibre types, characterised by expression of myosin heavy chain IIx (MYH1). It has the largest cross-sectional diameter of the three (commonly ~70–90 µm in trained adult limb muscle), the fewest and smallest mitochondria, the lowest myoglobin content — hence its pale colouration relative to Type I and IIa — and the sparsest capillary density, consistent with a design optimised for peak force and contraction velocity rather than sustained aerobic output. Metabolically it relies almost exclusively on anaerobic glycolysis, supported by substantial intramyocellular glycogen stores, generating the highest force per fibre and fastest shortening velocity of the three types at the cost of rapid fatigue (typically within seconds to tens of seconds under continuous load). It is innervated by the largest-diameter, fastest-conducting alpha motor neurons and, per the size principle of motor unit recruitment, is activated last, only once force demand exceeds what Type I and IIa motor units can supply — i.e., near-maximal voluntary contractions, plyometric and ballistic movements. Its proportion within a given human limb muscle is comparatively low relative to Type I and IIa and is not fixed: chronic endurance training shifts a subset of Type IIx fibres toward the more oxidative Type IIa phenotype (partial fast-to-fast transition along the IIx→IIa→I continuum), while resistance training emphasising peak power, or disuse/deconditioning, can shift the balance back toward IIx. A significant nomenclatural point: earlier ATPase histochemistry classified the fastest human fibres as "Type IIB" by direct analogy with the three fibre types characterised in rodent muscle (I, IIA, IIB). This was shown to be a misassignment: in situ hybridisation and immunohistochemistry (Smerdu et al. 1994) demonstrated that fibres histochemically typed as IIB in adult human limb muscle express MYH1 (IIx) transcript and protein, not MYH4 (true IIb). Schiaffino & Reggiani's 2011 review formalised the correction: true MYH4/IIb-expressing fibres are a genuine, distinct fibre type present in rodent and some other mammalian muscle, but are essentially absent from healthy adult human limb muscle, so "Type IIb" is not a valid synonym for the human fast fibre and should not be applied to it, though the older usage persists in some clinical histochemistry reporting.  
🇩🇪 Die Typ-IIx-Faser ist die schnellste und am stärksten glykolytische der drei physiologisch unterscheidbaren menschlichen Skelettmuskelfasertypen, gekennzeichnet durch die Expression der schweren Myosinkette IIx (MYH1). Sie besitzt den größten Querschnittsdurchmesser der drei Typen (üblicherweise ~70–90 µm in trainierter erwachsener Extremitätenmuskulatur), die wenigsten und kleinsten Mitochondrien, den niedrigsten Myoglobingehalt — daher ihre blasse Farbe im Vergleich zu Typ I und IIa — und die geringste Kapillardichte, passend zu einem auf maximale Kraft und Kontraktionsgeschwindigkeit statt auf anhaltende aerobe Leistung optimierten Design. Metabolisch stützt sie sich fast ausschließlich auf anaerobe Glykolyse, unterstützt durch beträchtliche intramyozelluläre Glykogenspeicher, und erzeugt von den drei Typen die höchste Kraft pro Faser und die schnellste Verkürzungsgeschwindigkeit — auf Kosten rascher Ermüdung (typischerweise innerhalb von Sekunden bis wenigen Zehnersekunden bei anhaltender Belastung). Innerviert wird sie von den im Durchmesser größten, am schnellsten leitenden Alpha-Motoneuronen und wird nach dem Größenprinzip der motorischen Rekrutierung als Letzte aktiviert — erst wenn der Kraftbedarf übersteigt, was Typ-I- und Typ-IIa-motorische Einheiten liefern können, also bei nahezu maximalen Willkürkontraktionen sowie plyometrischen und ballistischen Bewegungen. Ihr Anteil in einem gegebenen menschlichen Extremitätenmuskel ist im Vergleich zu Typ I und IIa vergleichsweise gering und nicht festgelegt: chronisches Ausdauertraining verschiebt einen Teil der Typ-IIx-Fasern in Richtung des oxidativeren Typ-IIa-Phänotyps (partielle Fast-zu-fast-Transition entlang des Kontinuums IIx→IIa→I), während auf Spitzenkraft ausgerichtetes Krafttraining oder Inaktivität/Dekonditionierung die Balance wieder in Richtung IIx verschieben kann. Ein bedeutsamer nomenklatorischer Punkt: Frühere ATPase-Histochemie klassifizierte die schnellsten menschlichen Fasern in direkter Analogie zu den drei bei Nagetieren charakterisierten Fasertypen (I, IIA, IIB) als "Typ IIB". Dies erwies sich als Fehlzuordnung: In-situ-Hybridisierung und Immunhistochemie (Smerdu et al. 1994) zeigten, dass die histochemisch als IIB typisierten Fasern in erwachsener menschlicher Extremitätenmuskulatur MYH1(IIx)-Transkript und -Protein exprimieren, nicht MYH4 (echtes IIb). Der Übersichtsartikel von Schiaffino und Reggiani (2011) formalisierte die Korrektur: Echte MYH4/IIb-exprimierende Fasern sind ein eigenständiger, real existierender Fasertyp bei Nagetieren und einigen anderen Säugetieren, in gesunder erwachsener menschlicher Extremitätenmuskulatur jedoch praktisch nicht vorhanden — "Typ IIb" ist daher kein gültiges Synonym für die schnelle menschliche Faser und sollte nicht auf sie angewendet werden, auch wenn sich der ältere Sprachgebrauch in manchen klinisch-histochemischen Befunden hält.
