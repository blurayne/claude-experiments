## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

**This is an umbrella entry, and that is the single most important thing about it.** "Type II fibre" is not a third, separate kind of muscle cell alongside Type IIa and Type IIx — it is the **family name** for the fast-twitch branch of human skeletal muscle, and its two specific members, [Type IIa](type-iia-fibre.render.md) and [Type IIx](type-iix-fibre.render.md), already have their own fully-rendered entries in this same set. Rendering "Type II fibre" as one more single fibre, indistinguishable from either sibling, would just produce a seventh near-duplicate image and teach nothing the other two don't already teach better. So every render in this entry is a **group composition**: two adjacent fibres, bundled together, one carrying the IIa profile and one carrying the IIx profile, so a single frame shows both the shared fast-twitch design and the concrete difference between its two members. See `type-ii-fibre.research.json`'s `composition_decision` field for the reasoning recorded before any prompt was written.

**What the family shares.** Like every skeletal muscle fibre (Type I included), a Type II fibre is a **single, unbranched, cylindrical, multinucleated cell** — a syncytium formed by the fusion of many myoblasts during development — running the full length of the muscle or a long fascicle within it: centimetres long, far longer than wide, so any single frame must show it running off the edges rather than sitting as a short self-contained sausage. Its defining visible feature, shared with every skeletal fibre, is regular **transverse cross-striation**: alternating dark **A bands** (myosin thick filaments) and light **I bands** (thin actin filaments, bisected by the dense **Z-disc**), repeating along the whole fibre as a stack of sarcomeres, in register across neighbouring myofibrils. Every one of its **many nuclei** sits flattened just beneath the sarcolemma at the periphery — never one central nucleus, which is the cardiomyocyte's signature, not skeletal muscle's. Metabolically, both members of the Type II family lean on **glycolysis** — breaking down glucose without needing oxygen — for a fast but short-lived burst of ATP, in contrast to Type I's oxidative, endurance-oriented design. Both are innervated by comparatively large, fast motor neurons and, by the **size principle** of motor unit recruitment, are switched on only once demand exceeds what Type I fibres can supply: a sprint, a jump, a heavy lift, never quiet standing. Both fatigue within seconds to a couple of minutes, because a glycolytic fuel supply is limited compared with an oxidative fibre's.

**What separates the two members — the thing this render has to make visible.** Type IIa is the "middle ground": fast like IIx, but with enough mitochondria and myoglobin to also burn fuel oxidatively, giving it noticeably better fatigue resistance than its purely glycolytic sibling. It carries a **moderate density of mitochondria**, a **moderate surrounding capillary network**, and a **pale rose-pink** colour — between Type I's deep red and Type IIx's near-white cream. Type IIx is the fast, purely glycolytic extreme: the **largest-diameter** fibre of the three human types, with the **fewest and smallest mitochondria**, the **sparsest capillary supply**, abundant **glycogen granules** as its main fuel reserve, and a **pale pink-cream** colour because it carries the least myoglobin of the three. Training shifts the balance between them — sustained endurance training tends to convert some IIx fibres toward the more fatigue-resistant IIa profile, and disuse tends to push the balance back the other way — which is itself a fact about the family, not about either fibre alone, and part of why showing them together earns its place.

**The naming point** (inherited from the IIx entry, restated here because it is the family's, not just IIx's). Older ATPase histochemistry called the fastest human fibres "Type IIb" by analogy with rodent muscle, which has three distinct fast fibre types (IIA, IIX, IIB). Molecularly this was a misnomer for humans: the fastest fibre in adult human limb muscle expresses **myosin heavy chain IIx (MYH1)**, not **MYH4**, the gene that actually encodes true IIb myosin. True IIb/MYH4 fibres are real and common in mice and rats but essentially absent from healthy adult human limb muscle (Smerdu et al. 1994; Schiaffino & Reggiani 2011). So the human Type II family has exactly two members worth rendering — IIa and IIx — and the real-microscopy reference below, which is rat tissue and does show a genuine IIB population, has to be read with that species caveat stated plainly, not glossed over.

Sources: [Smerdu et al. 1994, *Am J Physiol* 267(6 Pt 1):C1723-8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7545970/), [Schiaffino & Reggiani 2011, *Physiol Rev* 91(4):1447-1531](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8262285/), [OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 2e — 10.5 Types of Muscle Fibers](https://open.oregonstate.education/anatomy2e/chapter/types-muscle-fibers/), [Pette & Staron 2000, *Microsc Res Tech* 50(6):500-9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10998639/), [Bloemberg & Quadrilatero 2012, *PLoS ONE* 7(4):e35273](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0035273) (real-microscopy reference source).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **Do NOT render a single fibre.** That is this entry's one governing rule — a lone fibre, however well drawn, duplicates a sibling entry and fails to show the family. Every accepted render must contain two clearly distinguishable fibres bundled together.
- **The two fibres must actually look different from each other**, not just be two identical cylinders side by side: one visibly paler rose-pink with more, and slightly larger, mitochondria and a denser capillary alongside it (IIa); the other visibly paler still — near-white cream — with fewer, smaller mitochondria and a thinner capillary (IIx). If both look identical, the render has failed the entry's whole point even if each fibre individually is well drawn.
- **NO single central nucleus** on either fibre. That is the cardiomyocyte signature. Both fibres have many nuclei, every one flattened at the periphery, against the sarcolemma — never floating mid-cross-section.
- **Not branched, not Y-shaped, no intercalated discs.** Cardiac muscle features; these are plain unbranched cylinders running out of frame at both ends.
- **Not two short stubby sausages that fit entirely inside the frame.** Muscle fibres are centimetres long; compose them running diagonally off two opposite edges so their true proportions read even though the whole length cannot fit.
- **No deep true-red colour on either fibre** — deep red belongs to Type I, not shown here. Both fibres stay in the pale rose-to-cream range, with IIa distinctly less pale than IIx.
- **Not colourless, grey, or monochrome.** "Pale" means pale living tissue, not a desaturated or greyscale render.
- **No smooth, unstriated surface anywhere along either fibre.** Cross-striations (A/I banding) must be continuous and in register the whole visible length on both fibres; a smooth patch reads as smooth muscle, which is wrong here.
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids, flagella or cilia** — these are animal cells, not bacteria or protists.
- **Never label anything "Type IIb"** as though it were a synonym for either fibre shown. Human IIx and rodent IIb are molecularly distinct; IIb is essentially absent from adult human limb muscle and is not part of what this entry depicts.
- Exactly **two** fibres as the dominant subject; at most a sliver of a third, subordinate and cropped by the frame, so the composition does not turn into an unreadable cross-section thicket.

**Honest caveats, stated plainly:**
- **Species.** This is **rat** tibialis anterior, not human tissue. Rat and other rodent muscle genuinely contains a third fast fibre type, **Type IIB (MYH4)**, that is essentially absent from healthy adult human limb muscle (see §1's naming point) — the red IIB fibres in this plate are real rat fibres but do **not** correspond to anything in the human Type II family this entry depicts. They are shown in the reference because that is what the source image contains, not because IIB is part of the subject.
- **What the stain can and cannot distinguish.** This triple-antibody cocktail *can* separate I (blue) from IIA (green) from IIB (red) directly, by positive staining. It does **not** have a positive marker for IIX — those fibres are inferred by process of elimination (unstained by all three antibodies), which is a real and commonly-used approach in fibre-typing immunohistochemistry, but weaker evidence than a direct positive stain would be. A single-pH myosin-ATPase reaction (the classical alternative technique, used in some rejected candidates for the IIx sibling entry) is cruder still: it separates Type I from Type II as a whole but **cannot** separate IIa from IIx at all without a second reaction at a different pH.
- **This is a cross-section, not a longitudinal view.** It shows fibre identity and packing well but does not show — and is not being used to justify — the transverse A/I banding or peripheral-nucleus position that the renders below must get right; those checks are made against §1's text and the siblings' own already-verified longitudinal renders, not against this plate.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 "Type II fibre" isn't one cell — it's the umbrella name for the fast-twitch branch of skeletal muscle, and in a real muscle it always shows up as two related but distinct types living side by side: Type IIa and Type IIx. Both are built around anaerobic glycolysis rather than oxygen-fuelled endurance, both are driven by large, fast motor neurons that only switch on for real effort, and both fatigue within a couple of minutes at most. Where they differ is how far each one leans into pure speed. IIa keeps a moderate stock of mitochondria and a fair capillary supply, so it can back up a burst of power with some staying ability — the fibre that lets a set of heavy reps or a hard hill sprint go on for more than a couple of seconds. IIx strips that back further: minimal mitochondria, the thinnest capillary bed of the three human fibre types, and the largest diameter, built for one maximal effort and nothing after. The mix between them isn't fixed — sustained training tends to nudge some IIx fibres toward the more resilient IIa profile, and inactivity tends to drift the balance back — which is really a fact about the family as a whole, not about either fibre alone.  
🇩🇪 "Typ-II-Faser" ist keine einzelne Zelle, sondern der Sammelbegriff für den schnell zuckenden Zweig der Skelettmuskulatur, und in einem echten Muskel taucht sie immer als zwei verwandte, aber unterschiedliche Typen auf, die Seite an Seite liegen: Typ IIa und Typ IIx. Beide bauen auf anaerobe Glykolyse statt auf sauerstoffgetriebene Ausdauer, beide werden von großen, schnellen Motoneuronen angesteuert, die nur bei echtem Kraftaufwand einschalten, und beide ermüden innerhalb von höchstens ein paar Minuten. Der Unterschied liegt darin, wie weit jeder von beiden auf reine Geschwindigkeit setzt. IIa hält einen mäßigen Mitochondrienbestand und eine passable Kapillarversorgung vor, sodass sie einen Kraftschub mit etwas Durchhaltevermögen unterfüttern kann — die Faser, die einen Satz schwerer Wiederholungen oder einen harten Bergsprint länger als ein paar Sekunden durchstehen lässt. IIx streicht das weiter zusammen: minimale Mitochondrien, die dünnste Kapillarversorgung der drei menschlichen Fasertypen und den größten Durchmesser, gebaut für einen einzigen maximalen Einsatz und nichts danach. Die Mischung zwischen beiden ist nicht festgelegt — anhaltendes Training verschiebt manche IIx-Fasern tendenziell in Richtung des belastbareren IIa-Profils, Inaktivität lässt die Balance eher zurückdriften — eine Tatsache, die eigentlich die ganze Familie betrifft, nicht eine einzelne Faser.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 Type II designates the fast-twitch class of mammalian skeletal muscle fibre, distinguished from Type I by expression of fast myosin heavy chain isoforms and reliance on glycolytic rather than oxidative ATP production. In adult human limb muscle the class comprises two physiologically and molecularly distinct fibre types rather than one: Type IIa (MYH2), a fast oxidative-glycolytic fibre with moderate mitochondrial volume density, moderate capillarity and intermediate fatigue resistance, and Type IIx (MYH1), a fast glycolytic fibre with minimal mitochondrial density, the sparsest capillary supply of the three human types, the largest fibre cross-sectional area, and the fastest fatigue rate. Both are innervated by large-diameter, fast-conducting alpha motor neurons and recruited according to the size principle only once force demand exceeds Type I motor unit capacity. The IIa/IIx ratio within a muscle is plastic: chronic endurance or moderate resistance training shifts fibres along the IIx→IIa continuum toward the more oxidative phenotype, while disuse or training emphasising peak power shifts the balance back toward IIx. A rodent-derived third fast type, Type IIB (MYH4), is a genuine, distinct fibre type in mouse and rat muscle but is essentially absent from healthy adult human limb muscle; the human "Type II family" therefore has exactly two members worth distinguishing, IIa and IIx, and no valid human IIB.  
🇩🇪 Typ II bezeichnet die schnell zuckende Klasse der Skelettmuskelfasern von Säugetieren, unterschieden von Typ I durch die Expression schneller Myosin-Schwerketten-Isoformen und die Abhängigkeit von glykolytischer statt oxidativer ATP-Produktion. In der erwachsenen menschlichen Extremitätenmuskulatur umfasst diese Klasse zwei physiologisch und molekular unterscheidbare Fasertypen statt eines einzigen: Typ IIa (MYH2), eine schnelle, oxidativ-glykolytische Faser mit mäßiger mitochondrialer Volumendichte, mäßiger Kapillarisierung und mittlerer Ermüdungsresistenz, sowie Typ IIx (MYH1), eine schnelle glykolytische Faser mit minimaler Mitochondriendichte, der geringsten Kapillarversorgung der drei menschlichen Typen, der größten Faserquerschnittsfläche und der schnellsten Ermüdungsrate. Beide werden von im Durchmesser großen, schnell leitenden Alpha-Motoneuronen innerviert und nach dem Größenprinzip erst rekrutiert, wenn der Kraftbedarf die Kapazität von Typ-I-motorischen Einheiten übersteigt. Das Verhältnis von IIa zu IIx innerhalb eines Muskels ist plastisch: chronisches Ausdauer- oder moderates Krafttraining verschiebt Fasern entlang des Kontinuums IIx→IIa in Richtung des oxidativeren Phänotyps, während Inaktivität oder auf Spitzenkraft ausgerichtetes Training die Balance zurück in Richtung IIx verschiebt. Ein von Nagetieren bekannter dritter schneller Typ, Typ IIB (MYH4), ist bei Maus und Ratte ein echter, eigenständiger Fasertyp, in gesunder erwachsener menschlicher Extremitätenmuskulatur jedoch praktisch nicht vorhanden; die menschliche "Typ-II-Familie" hat daher genau zwei relevante Mitglieder, IIa und IIx, und kein gültiges menschliches IIB.
