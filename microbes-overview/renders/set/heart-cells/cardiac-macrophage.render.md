## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The **cardiac resident macrophage** is a tissue-resident mononuclear phagocyte of the myocardium, and it is not simply "a macrophage that happens to sit in the heart". The dominant steady-state population is **CCR2‑negative, MHC‑II‑low, TIMD4⁺/LYVE1⁺/FOLR2⁺ ("TLF⁺")**, seeded prenatally from **yolk‑sac erythro‑myeloid progenitors** (arriving from ~E10.5, well before definitive haematopoiesis) and from fetal liver, and it is maintained across life by **local self‑renewal** rather than by recruitment of blood monocytes — it divides roughly once a month. A separate, much smaller **CCR2⁺** subset is monocyte-derived and expands in injury; the resident CCR2⁻ cells are the ones that do the housekeeping. Resident macrophages make up roughly **6–8 % of the non‑myocyte cells** of the adult heart, and on average **about five of them envelop each cardiomyocyte**, so they are a normal, permanent part of healthy heart tissue and not a sign of inflammation.

**Gross morphology.** Unlike the round, free‑floating blood monocyte it descends from in other tissues, a cardiac resident macrophage is **elongated and spindled**, physically **wedged into the narrow interstitial cleft between neighbouring cardiomyocytes** and moulded by them: the cell body is stretched along the long axis of the muscle fibres, often with two tapering ends and slender processes that run for tens of micrometres along the sarcolemma. Its surface carries **membrane ruffles, lamellipodial veils and fine filopodia** that probe the myocyte surface and the surrounding basal lamina. The single **nucleus** is comparatively small relative to the cell, oval to **kidney‑shaped (reniform)** and eccentric, with clumped peripheral heterochromatin. The cytoplasm holds the standard professional‑phagocyte kit: numerous **lysosomes** (small, dense, enzyme-filled vesicles), one or more larger **phagosomes** containing engulfed cargo — in the heart typically apoptotic cell fragments, worn‑out cell debris and **exophers**, the mitochondria-filled packages that cardiomyocytes eject and macrophages clear — plus scattered **mitochondria**, **rough endoplasmic reticulum** and a **Golgi apparatus** for the cytokines and growth factors it secretes.

**The distinguishing feature: electrical coupling.** [Hulsmans et al., *Cell* 2017](https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0092-8674(17)30412-9) showed that in the **distal atrioventricular (AV) node** the HCN4⁺ conducting cells are densely interspersed with **elongated macrophages that express connexin 43 (Cx43, gene *GJA1*)**. Where a macrophage process presses against a myocyte, the two plasma membranes form true **gap‑junction plaques** — patches of connexin‑43 hemichannel hexamers docked end to end — so the macrophage is *electrically continuous* with the muscle cell. Patch‑clamp recordings showed the coupled macrophage has a negative resting membrane potential and **depolarises in synchrony with the cardiomyocyte**; reciprocally, the macrophage nudges the myocyte's resting potential more positive and accelerates its repolarisation. Optogenetic photostimulation of channelrhodopsin‑2‑expressing macrophages **improved AV conduction**, whereas macrophage‑specific deletion of Cx43, or macrophage ablation, **delayed AV conduction and produced progressive AV block**. In other words: the tidy‑up cell of the heart is also, in this one location, part of the wiring. This is why the render must show the **contact and the connexin‑43 plaques**, not merely a cell swallowing something.

Sources: [Hulsmans M, Clauss S, Xiao L, et al. *Macrophages Facilitate Electrical Conduction in the Heart.* Cell 2017;169(3):510–522.e20 (PMID 28431249)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28431249/) · [full text at Cell](https://www.cell.com/fulltext/S0092-8674(17)30412-9) · [Nahrendorf M. et al. — *Resident cardiac macrophages: Heterogeneity and function in health and disease*, Immunity 2022](https://www.cell.com/immunity/pdf/S1074-7613(22)00402-2.pdf) · [Li et al., *The role of cardiac resident macrophages in cardiac aging*, Aging Cell 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acel.14008) · [Liao et al., *At the heart of inflammation: unravelling cardiac resident macrophage biology*, J Cell Mol Med 2024 (PMC11369210)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11369210/) · [Lavine/Epelman — *Resident and monocyte-derived macrophages in cardiovascular disease* (PMC5777215)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777215/) · generic macrophage ultrastructure: [StatPearls — Histology, Macrophage (NCBI Bookshelf NBK540980)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK540980/).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **NOT a free-floating round blood monocyte** — no lone spherical cell drifting in plasma among red blood cells. The resident cardiac macrophage is elongated/spindled and squeezed between muscle cells.
- **NOT a giant multinucleate cell** — no foreign-body giant cell, no osteoclast-like ring of many nuclei. Exactly **one** nucleus.
- **Do NOT omit the cardiomyocyte contact.** At least one cardiomyocyte must touch the macrophage, and the connexin-43 gap-junction plaques must be visible at that contact. A macrophage alone, or a macrophage only gobbling a germ, is a FAIL — the coupling is the point.
- **No bacteria being eaten as the main event** — this cell's cargo is the body's own debris (apoptotic fragments, exophers), not an infection scene.
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids, chloroplasts or a large central vacuole** — this is a human cell, not a bacterium or a plant cell.
- **No cilia or flagella** — it crawls with actin-driven ruffles and filopodia.
- **Do not draw the intercalated disc of the myocytes as the macrophage's junction** — the macrophage's connexin-43 plaques are small patches on the *lateral* sarcolemma contact, not a full intercalated disc.
- A single macrophage, not a sheet of confluent immune cells.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 Every organ keeps its own permanent crew of macrophages, and the heart's crew turned out to have a job nobody had suspected. These cells are not recruited from the blood: most of them arrive in the embryo, from the yolk sac and the fetal liver, and then simply stay for life, dividing about once a month to keep their numbers up. They make up something like six to eight per cent of the heart's non-muscle cells, and on average around five of them wrap around each individual heart muscle cell. Squeezed into the interstitial clefts, they lose the round shape of a blood monocyte entirely and become long and spindly. Their day job is maintenance: swallowing dying cells and debris, and clearing the mitochondria-filled packages ('exophers') that heart muscle cells eject when they renew their power plants. The unexpected part came in 2017, when a Boston group found that in the atrioventricular node — the relay station between the atria and the ventricles — these elongated macrophages sit interleaved with the conducting cells and connect to them through connexin-43 gap junctions, the same protein channels heart muscle cells use to wire themselves to each other. Being electrically continuous with the muscle, the macrophage depolarises in time with every beat and, in return, nudges the muscle cell's membrane potential and speeds its recovery. Take the connexin-43 away from the macrophages, or remove the macrophages altogether, and conduction through the node slows and eventually blocks. It is a striking reminder that the heart's electrical system is not built from muscle and nerve alone, and it opens a new angle on why conduction problems become more common as the heart ages.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 Cardiac resident macrophages are tissue-resident mononuclear phagocytes of the myocardial interstitium. The dominant steady-state population is CCR2-negative and MHC-II-low, with a TIMD4+/LYVE1+/FOLR2+ ('TLF+') signature; fate-mapping shows it originates from yolk-sac erythro-myeloid progenitors seeded from about E10.5 and from fetal-liver monocytes, and is maintained by local self-renewal largely independent of monocyte input, whereas the minor CCR2+ subset derives from definitive haematopoiesis and expands in injury. Resident macrophages constitute roughly 6-8% of non-myocyte cells, with on average about five cells apposed to each cardiomyocyte. Morphologically they are elongated and spindled, conforming to the interstitial cleft rather than adopting the rounded shape of a circulating monocyte, with membrane ruffles, lamellipodial veils and filopodia, an eccentric reniform nucleus with peripheral heterochromatin, abundant lysosomes, phagosomes, mitochondria, rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi. Homeostatic functions include efferocytosis of apoptotic cells, clearance of cardiomyocyte-derived exophers containing dysfunctional mitochondria, coronary and valvular morphogenesis, and adaptive remodelling. Hulsmans et al. (Cell 2017;169:510-522) demonstrated an additional electrophysiological role: in the distal atrioventricular node, HCN4+ conducting cells are densely interspersed with connexin-43-expressing elongated macrophages that form functional gap junctions with them. Patch-clamp and optical mapping showed the coupled macrophage holds a negative resting membrane potential and depolarises in synchrony with the cardiomyocyte, while reciprocally depolarising the myocyte resting potential and, per in-silico modelling, accelerating repolarisation. Optogenetic depolarisation of ChR2-expressing macrophages improved AV conduction; conditional Gja1 (Cx43) deletion in the macrophage compartment or macrophage ablation (Cd11b-DTR) prolonged AV conduction and produced progressive AV block. Resident cardiac macrophages therefore act as accessory conducting elements as well as scavengers, and are implicated in both physiological conduction and arrhythmogenesis.
