## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The intracardiac neuron is a **peripheral autonomic ganglion neuron that lives inside the heart wall itself**. It is not a solitary cell: it sits, together with a few dozen to a few hundred of its kind, inside a small **intracardiac ganglion**, and those ganglia are strung together by fine **interganglionic nerve trunks** into the **epicardial ganglionated plexus** — the anatomical substrate of the **intrinsic cardiac nervous system**, often nicknamed the *"heart's own brain"* or *"little brain on the heart"*. In the human heart the ganglia are concentrated in **epicardial fat pads**: five atrial ganglionated subplexuses (superior and posterior right atrium, superior and posteromedial left atrium, the inferior-lateral posterior left atrium, with extensions into the interatrial septum) and five ventricular clusters sitting in the fat around the aortic root and the origins of the major coronary arteries. Individual ganglia range from a handful of neurons up to roughly **0.5 × 1 mm** in size; totals for the whole human intrinsic cardiac nervous system are commonly quoted in the region of tens of thousands of neurons (the figure of ~43,000 traces back to Armour et al. 1997 and should be treated as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a precise count).

**Morphology.** The cell is a classic **multipolar ganglion neuron**: a plump, round-to-ovoid **soma** of roughly **20–40 µm** across in human material (intracellularly labelled canine intracardiac neurons averaged about **62 × 40 µm**, so somata are frankly large by peripheral-nerve standards and vary a lot with species and chemical phenotype), from which **2–12 primary dendrites** spring. Some cells are **pseudo-unipolar** or effectively unipolar; a substantial minority have a short axon that never leaves the ganglion, or no axon that can be traced at all — those are the local-circuit cells. The dendritic arbour is typically **confined within the ganglion**, where it collects convergent input, so this neuron looks *bushy and compact*, not like a long-range projection cell. The **nucleus is large, round and pale (vesicular)**, often slightly eccentric, with a single **prominent, strongly stained nucleolus** — the single most reliable identifier of a ganglion neuron on a slide. The cytoplasm carries coarse basophilic **Nissl substance** (rough ER plus free ribosomes) that clumps peripherally and leaves a paler zone around the nucleus; human intrinsic cardiac neurons also frequently accumulate **lipofuscin-type inclusions** with age. A single **axon** leaves the soma and runs out of the ganglion into the plexus, either to the local myocardium and conduction tissue or, through an interganglionic nerve, to a *different* intracardiac ganglion.

**The satellite glial sheath is not optional.** Like every peripheral ganglion neuron, the intracardiac neuron is completely **enveloped by a thin, continuous sheath of satellite glial cells** (amphicytes) that wraps the soma and the initial segment of its processes, leaving only a ~20 nm cleft. The satellite cells buffer potassium and neurotransmitter, supply metabolic support, and actively modulate the neuron's excitability — in whole-mount preparations of the mouse sinoatrial ganglion, neurons *plus* their satellite glia form a flat sheet lying directly on the underlying atrial muscle. On a histological section the sheath shows up as a **ring of small, dark, flattened glial nuclei hugging the outline of each big pale neuron**; an illustration that leaves it out is drawing a CNS neuron, not a ganglion cell. Around the whole ganglion lies a **connective-tissue capsule**, and around that, **adipose tissue** of the epicardial fat pad with the **atrial myocardium** beneath.

**Function — why it is a brain and not a relay.** The classic textbook picture treated these cells as simple parasympathetic postganglionic relays: vagal preganglionic fibre in, cholinergic fibre out to the sinoatrial node. That is now known to be only one of several populations. The ganglia contain **afferent (sensory) neurons** responding to stretch and to local chemistry, **local circuit interneurons**, and **efferent motor neurons** — cholinergic, adrenergic and a substantial **biphenotypic** and **nitrergic (nNOS-positive)** contingent — all synaptically interconnected. Because sensory, integrating and motor elements are all present *inside the heart*, the network can close a **complete reflex loop locally**: it measures what the myocardium is actually doing beat by beat and adjusts rate, conduction and contractile force on the spot, on a timescale far shorter than a round trip to the brainstem. Denervated (transplanted) hearts still beat and still adapt, which is the clinical proof that the loop exists. Descending vagal and sympathetic traffic then acts as an *overall instruction* that the intracardiac network integrates with its own local data, rather than as a direct command line. Clinically this network matters a great deal: catheter ablation of the atrial ganglionated plexuses is used in atrial fibrillation, and intrinsic-cardiac-neuron remodelling is implicated in arrhythmia after myocardial infarction and in heart failure.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **NOT a CNS pyramidal cell.** No triangular pyramidal soma with a single thick apical dendrite rising to a tufted apex, and no cortical-layer setting. This is a peripheral autonomic ganglion neuron: a plump, round-to-ovoid soma with several roughly equivalent dendrites.
- **NO long myelinated tract.** Do not draw a long, thickly myelinated axon marching across the frame with a ladder of Nodes of Ranvier and a motor end-plate — that is the motor neuron. The axon here leaves the ganglion as a fine fibre into the local plexus and mostly stays within the heart wall.
- **NEVER omit the satellite glial sheath.** A bare soma sitting in open space is wrong: every peripheral ganglion neuron is completely wrapped by satellite glia, visible as a thin continuous envelope (and as a ring of small dark glial nuclei in histology). This is the single most important difference from the generic neuron plate.
- **Do not duplicate the generic neuron illustration.** The picture must read as *a neuron inside a ganglion inside the heart wall* — ganglion capsule, at least one neighbouring soma, an interganglionic nerve trunk, epicardial fat and heart muscle nearby — not as one isolated star-shaped nerve cell on an empty background.
- **No brain, no spinal cord, no cortical or spinal-grey-matter context** and no glia of the CNS (no astrocytes, no oligodendrocyte wrapping several axons).
- **Not a heart muscle cell.** No sarcomere cross-striations, no intercalated discs, no branched myocyte — the myocardium may appear only as *context tissue* beneath or beside the ganglion, never as the labelled subject.
- **No cell wall, nucleoid, plasmids, flagella, cilia, chloroplasts or a large central vacuole** — this is a human nerve cell, not a bacterium, protist or plant cell, and it is non-motile.
- **Only ONE axon**, and Nissl substance must stay out of the axon hillock and axon; do not paint Nissl clumps down the axon.
- **No more than one nucleus per neuron**, and exactly one prominent nucleolus inside it.
- **No decorative electricity**: no lightning bolts, sparks, glow flares, ECG traces, waveforms, starbursts or radiating rays, and no brain icon, wires or circuit-board motifs — the "heart's own brain" idea belongs in the text, not in the scientific plate.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 Most people picture the heart as an organ that takes orders — the brain sends nerves down, the heart obeys. The truth is stranger and more interesting: the heart carries a nervous system of its own, physically inside its wall, and it reacts faster than your brain can. Anatomically it is a network of small ganglia, clusters of a few dozen to a few hundred nerve cells, sitting mainly in the fat pads on the outer surface of the atria and around the roots of the great vessels, wired to each other by fine nerve trunks into what anatomists call the epicardial ganglionated plexus. Estimates of the total run into the tens of thousands of neurons. Each of those neurons is a plump, round cell body with a large pale nucleus and a single conspicuous nucleolus, dressed in a complete jacket of satellite glial cells, sending a bushy spray of dendrites to its neighbours inside the same ganglion. The old textbook view called them simple parasympathetic relay stations for the vagus nerve. They are not. The ganglia contain sensory neurons that measure stretch and local chemistry, interneurons that talk only to other cells in the network, and motor neurons — cholinergic, adrenergic and a large mixed population — that go back out to the pacemaker, the conduction system and the muscle. That means a complete reflex arc lives inside the heart: measure, decide, correct, all without leaving the organ. The signals from your brain still matter, but they arrive as a broad instruction — be ready, we are about to run — which the local network then reconciles with what the muscle is actually doing beat by beat. The clearest proof is a transplanted heart. Its connection to the brain has been cut and never fully returns, yet it still beats, still adapts its rate to exercise, and still works for decades. Clinically this little brain is now a target in its own right: ablating the atrial ganglionated plexuses is part of some atrial-fibrillation procedures, and remodelling of these neurons after a heart attack or in heart failure is a recognised contributor to dangerous rhythm disturbances.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The intracardiac (intrinsic cardiac) neuron is a peripheral autonomic ganglion neuron residing within intracardiac ganglia of the epicardial ganglionated plexus, the anatomical substrate of the intrinsic cardiac nervous system or "little brain on the heart". In the human heart, ganglia are distributed across five atrial epicardial ganglionated subplexuses (ventral right atrial, ventral left atrial, dorsal right atrial, middle dorsal and left dorsal, per Pauza's nomenclature) and several ventricular clusters located in adipose tissue around the aortic root and the origins of the major coronary arteries; individual ganglia range from a few neurons to structures of approximately 0.5 × 1 mm, with whole-heart neuron totals conventionally cited in the tens of thousands (the frequently quoted ~43,000 derives from Armour et al. 1997 and is best treated as an order-of-magnitude estimate). Morphologically the neuron is multipolar, with a round-to-ovoid soma of roughly 20–40 µm in human material (intracellularly labelled canine intracardiac neurons averaged 62 × 40 µm) bearing 2–12 primary dendrites whose arborisation is largely confined to the ganglion; pseudo-unipolar and effectively unipolar profiles occur, and a substantial subset possesses only a short intraganglionic axon or no traceable axon, consistent with local-circuit function. The nucleus is large, vesicular and euchromatic with a single prominent nucleolus, the perikaryon contains coarse Nissl substance (rough endoplasmic reticulum plus polyribosomes) sparing the axon hillock, and lipofuscin-type inclusions are frequent in adult human cells. As in all peripheral ganglia, the soma and proximal processes are completely ensheathed by satellite glial cells (amphicytes) separated from the neurolemma by a cleft on the order of 20 nm; these cells buffer extracellular K+ and neurotransmitter, provide metabolic support and modulate neuronal excitability, and in whole-mount preparations neurons together with their satellite glia form a monolayer directly apposed to the underlying atrial myocardium. Functionally the plexus is not a simple parasympathetic relay. It comprises afferent (mechanosensory and chemosensory) neurons, local circuit interneurons, and efferent postganglionic neurons of cholinergic, adrenergic, biphenotypic (ChAT/TH co-expressing) and nitrergic (nNOS-positive) phenotype, with NPY, calbindin and VIP among the co-transmitters described; soma size correlates with chemical code and location within the subplexus. Because sensory, integrative and motor elements are co-localised intracardially, complete reflex arcs are closed within the organ, permitting beat-to-beat modulation of chronotropy, dromotropy and inotropy on a latency far shorter than a spinal or medullary loop; descending vagal and sympathetic preganglionic traffic synapses onto these neurons as a higher-order set-point that the local network integrates with ongoing mechanical and chemical feedback. Persistence of rate adaptation in the cardiac-denervated transplanted heart is the standard functional demonstration. Clinically, ganglionated plexus ablation is employed adjunctively in atrial fibrillation, and post-infarction and heart-failure remodelling of intrinsic cardiac neurons contributes to arrhythmogenesis.  
🇩🇪 Das herzeigene (intrinsische kardiale) Neuron ist ein peripheres autonomes Ganglienneuron innerhalb der intrakardialen Ganglien des epikardialen Ganglienplexus, des anatomischen Substrats des intrinsischen kardialen Nervensystems oder „kleinen Gehirns des Herzens". Im menschlichen Herzen verteilen sich die Ganglien auf fünf atriale epikardiale Subplexus (ventral rechtsatrial, ventral linksatrial, dorsal rechtsatrial, mittlerer dorsaler und linker dorsaler Subplexus nach der Nomenklatur von Pauza) sowie mehrere ventrikuläre Ansammlungen im Fettgewebe um die Aortenwurzel und die Ursprünge der großen Koronararterien; einzelne Ganglien reichen von wenigen Neuronen bis zu Strukturen von etwa 0,5 × 1 mm, und die Gesamtzahl der Neurone im Herzen wird üblicherweise mit Zehntausenden angegeben (die häufig zitierten ~43.000 gehen auf Armour et al. 1997 zurück und sind als Größenordnung, nicht als exakte Zahl zu verstehen). Morphologisch ist das Neuron multipolar, mit einem rundlich-ovalen Soma von etwa 20–40 µm beim Menschen (intrazellulär markierte kanine intrakardiale Neurone maßen im Mittel 62 × 40 µm) und 2–12 Primärdendriten, deren Verzweigung weitgehend auf das Ganglion beschränkt bleibt; pseudounipolare und faktisch unipolare Profile kommen vor, und ein erheblicher Anteil besitzt nur ein kurzes intraganglionäres Axon oder gar kein verfolgbares Axon, passend zu einer Funktion als lokales Schaltneuron. Der Zellkern ist groß, bläschenförmig und euchromatisch mit einem einzigen auffälligen Nukleolus, das Perikaryon enthält grobschollige Nissl-Substanz (raues endoplasmatisches Retikulum und Polyribosomen) unter Aussparung des Axonhügels, und Lipofuszin-artige Einschlüsse sind in adulten menschlichen Zellen häufig. Wie in allen peripheren Ganglien sind Soma und proximale Fortsätze vollständig von Satellitenzellen (Amphizyten) umhüllt, getrennt durch einen Spalt in der Größenordnung von 20 nm; diese Zellen puffern extrazelluläres K+ und Neurotransmitter, leisten metabolische Unterstützung und modulieren die neuronale Erregbarkeit, und in Whole-Mount-Präparaten bilden Neurone samt ihren Satellitenzellen eine Monolage unmittelbar auf dem darunterliegenden Vorhofmyokard. Funktionell ist der Plexus keine einfache parasympathische Umschaltstation. Er umfasst afferente (mechano- und chemosensorische) Neurone, lokale Interneurone sowie efferente postganglionäre Neurone cholinergen, adrenergen, biphänotypischen (ChAT/TH-koexprimierenden) und nitrergen (nNOS-positiven) Phänotyps, mit NPY, Calbindin und VIP unter den beschriebenen Kotransmittern; die Somagröße korreliert mit chemischem Code und Lage im Subplexus.
