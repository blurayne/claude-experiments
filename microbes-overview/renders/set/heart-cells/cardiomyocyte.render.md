## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The contractile ("working") cardiomyocyte is the muscle cell of the myocardium and the cell that actually does the pumping. In the human ventricle it is a **branched, roughly cylindrical striated cell, about 100–150 µm long and 20–35 µm wide** (atrial cardiomyocytes are shorter and thinner); it makes up only about a third of the heart's cells by number but some 70–80 % of its mass. Unlike a skeletal muscle fibre — a long, unbranched, multinucleated syncytium formed by the fusion of many myoblasts, with a row of nuclei pressed flat against the sarcolemma — the cardiomyocyte is a **single cell with usually ONE nucleus, and that nucleus sits in the middle of the cell**, pushing the myofibrils aside into a spindle-shaped perinuclear clearing; a substantial minority of human cardiomyocytes are binucleate (both nuclei still central), and polyploidy is common, but a peripheral row of many nuclei is never correct here. The cell **branches**, typically into a Y at one or both ends, so that neighbouring cells interlock into a three-dimensional meshwork rather than lying as parallel straps.

Its cytoplasm (sarcoplasm) is filled with **myofibrils** built from serially repeated **sarcomeres**, each running **Z-disc to Z-disc** and roughly 1.6–2.2 µm long depending on filling. This gives the cell its cross-striation: the darker, birefringent **A band** (the full length of the myosin thick filaments, including the region where thin filaments overlap them, with the lighter H zone and its central M line inside it) alternating with the lighter **I band** (thin actin filaments only), bisected by the narrow, dense **Z-disc** where the thin filaments of adjacent sarcomeres are anchored. Contraction is sliding-filament: myosin heads walk along actin and the Z-discs are pulled together, shortening every sarcomere in series.

Excitation reaches the interior through **transverse (T-) tubules**, deep invaginations of the sarcolemma. Two details distinguish cardiac from skeletal T-tubules: cardiac T-tubules are **wider and fewer**, and they enter the cell **at the level of the Z-disc** (in skeletal muscle they sit at the A–I junction, two per sarcomere). Each cardiac T-tubule apposes a single terminal cisterna of the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** to form a **dyad** (skeletal muscle forms triads). Depolarisation opens L-type calcium channels in the T-tubule membrane, the small influx of Ca²⁺ triggers a much larger release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through ryanodine receptors (calcium-induced calcium release), and that Ca²⁺ binds troponin C to let contraction proceed — so, unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac contraction genuinely depends on extracellular calcium.

The single most recognisable, defining feature is the **intercalated disc** (*discus intercalatus*, German *Glanzstreifen*, historically "lines of Eberth"): the step-like, transverse end-to-end junction between two cardiomyocytes, which under the light microscope appears as a dark, slightly zig-zag line running right across a fibre. It is not one junction but three working together. On its **transverse** (perpendicular) segments sit the **fascia adherens**, a broad adherens-type junction into which the actin thin filaments of the terminal sarcomere insert — mechanically it is the cell's substitute Z-disc, and it is what transmits contractile force from cell to cell — and the **desmosomes** (*maculae adhaerentes*), spot welds that anchor the desmin intermediate-filament network and stop the cells being pulled apart during systole. In mammalian heart these two intermingle so thoroughly that the composite structure is often called an **area composita**. On the **longitudinal** (parallel to the fibre axis) segments sit the **gap junctions**, channel plaques built mainly of **connexin-43**, which give a low-resistance electrical path from one cytoplasm to the next; they are what allow the action potential to spread from cell to cell and make the myocardium behave as a **functional syncytium** — an electrically continuous sheet, even though it is made of separate cells. Because of them a cardiomyocyte cannot be recruited individually or graded like a motor unit: when the wave arrives, everybody contracts.

The cell's energetics match its duty cycle. **Mitochondria occupy roughly a third of the cell volume** (about 30–40 %, higher than in any other human cell type except brown adipocytes), packed in longitudinal rows between the myofibrils and in a cluster around the nucleus and under the sarcolemma, with densely stacked cristae. Cardiac muscle is almost entirely aerobic, burning mainly fatty acids with lactate, glucose and ketones as alternates; it carries myoglobin, glycogen granules and lipid droplets as local fuel stores, and it is essentially fatigue-resistant — its long absolute refractory period also makes tetanus impossible, which is exactly what a pump needs. Contraction is **involuntary and myogenic**: the beat is initiated by pacemaker cells and delivered by the conduction system, and the autonomic nervous system and hormones only modulate rate and force; there is no voluntary control and no neuromuscular junction of the skeletal kind. Adult cardiomyocytes are essentially post-mitotic — renewal runs at well under 1 % per year — which is why an infarct heals as scar rather than as new muscle.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **NOT a long, unbranched, multinucleated skeletal muscle fibre.** The cardiomyocyte is short (100–150 µm), branched, and a single cell.
- **NO peripheral row of nuclei** flattened against the membrane — that is the skeletal muscle giveaway. Draw ONE nucleus, in the MIDDLE of the cell (a second, also central, nucleus is acceptable; a row of many is not).
- **NOT a smooth-muscle cell:** no long, tapered, unstriated spindle with a single cigar-shaped central nucleus and no sarcomeres. Cardiac muscle IS striated.
- **NO neuron-like features:** no dendrites, no axon, no growth cone, no synapse, no myelin. The branches are thick, striated arms of the muscle cell itself, continuing the myofibrils, not thin signalling processes.
- **No intercalated discs along the SIDES of the cell** — they are end-to-end (and at branch junctions), always transverse to the fibre axis, never running lengthwise down a flank.
- **No neuromuscular junction / motor end plate** on the cell, and no nerve terminal delivering the command: this cell is driven electrically by its neighbours through gap junctions, not by a personal motor nerve.
- **No cell wall, no nucleoid, no plasmids, no flagella, no cilia** — this is an animal cell, not a bacterium or a protist.
- **Not a plain, unstriated blob:** the cross-striations (A/I bands with Z-discs) must be visible along every myofibril, and they must line up across neighbouring myofibrils (register), not be drawn as random scratches.
- **Sparse mitochondria are wrong** — they should be conspicuously abundant, packed in rows between the myofibrils (≈⅓ of the volume), not a token two or three.
- **T-tubules must not be drawn at the A–I junction** (that is skeletal muscle) — in cardiac muscle they line up on the Z-disc.
- One clearly dominant cardiomyocyte as the subject; neighbouring cells may be shown only where they meet it at an intercalated disc, and must not turn the image into an unreadable tissue thicket.

No adequately licensed EM plate of a cardiac intercalated disc was found on Commons, so those three sub-structures appear in the illustrated styles (which are diagrams) but must be taken on the diagram's word, not on the micrograph's. Two other candidate micrographs were rejected during the search: Commons *"Glanzstreifen.jpg"* (CC BY 2.5) carried a baked-in white magnifier inset and leader lines that survived two cleaning passes, and *"Histology of cardiac muscle.jpg"* turned out to be a hand-drawn student sketch, not a micrograph.
