## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

The neutrophil (neutrophilic granulocyte, polymorphonuclear neutrophil/PMN) is the most abundant leukocyte in human blood (roughly 40–70% of circulating white cells) and the prototypical cell of innate immunity's acute-inflammation response. It is produced by granulopoiesis in the bone marrow (on the order of 10^11 cells per day) and is a round, amoeboid cell about 10–12 µm across — somewhat larger than a red blood cell. Its defining feature is a **multilobed (polymorphic) nucleus**, typically 2–5 lobes (most often 3) joined by thin strands of nuclear chromatin, which is why the whole granulocyte lineage is named "polymorphonuclear." "Neutrophil" itself refers to a staining property: on classic Romanowsky (Wright/Giemsa) blood-smear stains the cytoplasmic granules take up neither the acidic (eosin, red) nor the basic (haematoxylin, blue) dye strongly, staining a neutral pale lilac-pink instead — unlike the coarse red-orange granules of an eosinophil or the dark purple-black granules of a basophil.

The cytoplasm is densely packed with at least three morphologically and biochemically distinct populations of membrane-bound granules, released in sequence during activation: **primary (azurophilic) granules**, the largest and most electron-dense, containing myeloperoxidase (MPO), defensins, elastase, cathepsin G and other proteases used for intracellular killing inside the phagolysosome; **secondary (specific) granules**, smaller, containing lactoferrin, lysozyme and components of the NADPH-oxidase system, mobilised early and often fused with the plasma membrane or the forming phagosome; and **tertiary (gelatinase) granules** plus secretory vesicles, which carry membrane receptors and matrix-degrading enzymes (e.g. gelatinase/MMP-9) to the cell surface, helping the cell squeeze through the vascular basement membrane during extravasation. Beneath the plasma membrane sits a dense cortical **actin cytoskeleton** that drives amoeboid crawling and the extension of a leading pseudopod toward a chemotactic gradient (bacterial peptides such as fMLP, the complement fragment C5a, and chemokines such as IL-8/CXCL8). Neutrophils are strongly glycolytic — energy comes mainly from stored cytoplasmic **glycogen** rather than oxidative phosphorylation, so, unlike a fibroblast or a secretory gland cell, they carry comparatively few, small mitochondria and only modest rough ER/Golgi (they are terminally differentiated and do relatively little new protein synthesis in circulation). On encountering a microbe the cell engulfs it into a **phagosome**, which fuses with granules to become a phagolysosome where MPO-generated hypochlorous acid, defensins and proteases kill and digest the microbe; a respiratory burst from the NADPH-oxidase complex assembled on the phagosomal/plasma membrane also generates reactive oxygen species. As a last resort a dying or strongly activated neutrophil can undergo NETosis, expelling decondensed nuclear chromatin studded with granule proteins (MPO, elastase) as a sticky extracellular web — a **neutrophil extracellular trap (NET)** — that immobilises and helps kill bacteria and fungi outside the cell. Circulating neutrophils are short-lived, surviving on the order of hours in blood and up to a few days once they have entered tissue.

Sources: [Wikipedia — Neutrophil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrophil), [OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 18.3 — Formed Elements of the Blood](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/18-3-erythrocytes), [NCBI Bookshelf — Molecular Biology of the Cell, "Neutrophils"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26928/), [Mayadas, Cullere & Lowell 2014, "The Multifaceted Functions of Neutrophils" (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4136684/), [Brinkmann et al. 2004, "Neutrophil Extracellular Traps Kill Bacteria" (Science, via PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15001782/).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **No single round/oval nucleus** — a lymphocyte-style large round nucleus filling most of the cell is wrong; the defining feature is a nucleus split into 2–5 lobes connected by thin chromatin strands.
- **No cell wall, capsule, nucleoid, plasmid or bacterial flagella** — this is a human granulocyte, not a bacterium.
- **No dense, prominent rough ER/Golgi stacks dominating the cell** — neutrophils are terminally differentiated and do little new secretory synthesis; keep ER/Golgi modest, not a plasma-cell-style secretory apparatus.
- **No large numerous mitochondria packed through the cytoplasm** — neutrophils are glycolytic and mitochondria-poor; a few small ones are correct, a mitochondria-rich cell (like a muscle or heart cell) is not.
- **No cilia or flagella for locomotion** — the cell moves by amoeboid crawling (actin-driven pseudopods), not beating appendages.
- **No spindle/elongated fibroblast shape** — the resting/crawling cell body is round to amoeboid, not a long tapering spindle.
- **Granules should read as at least two visually distinct populations** (larger/darker primary vs. smaller/paler secondary), not one uniform dot pattern.
- A single specimen, not a crowded blood-smear field with red blood cells and platelets filling the frame.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 The neutrophil is the body's rapid-response innate immune cell, and by sheer numbers it dominates the bloodstream, making up roughly half to two-thirds of all white blood cells. Within minutes of an infection or injury, neutrophils sense chemical distress signals, squeeze out of nearby blood vessels and swarm the site, where they engulf bacteria and fungi, release antimicrobial enzymes, and generate a burst of reactive molecules to destroy what they've swallowed. When overwhelmed, a neutrophil can even sacrifice itself, expelling a web of its own DNA studded with toxic proteins to trap and kill microbes outside the cell — a process called NETosis. This intensity comes at a cost: neutrophils are short-lived, surviving only hours in blood and up to a few days in tissue, so the bone marrow constantly mass-produces fresh ones. The pus of a healing wound is, largely, spent neutrophils that gave their all.  
🇩🇪 Der Neutrophile ist die schnelle Eingreiftruppe des angeborenen Immunsystems und dominiert allein durch seine schiere Zahl den Blutkreislauf — er macht etwa die Hälfte bis zwei Drittel aller weißen Blutkörperchen aus. Innerhalb von Minuten nach einer Infektion oder Verletzung registrieren Neutrophile chemische Alarmsignale, quetschen sich aus nahegelegenen Blutgefäßen und stürmen den Ort des Geschehens, wo sie Bakterien und Pilze verschlingen, antimikrobielle Enzyme freisetzen und einen Schwall reaktiver Moleküle erzeugen, um das Verschluckte zu zerstören. Wenn es zu viel wird, kann sich ein Neutrophiler sogar selbst opfern und ein Netz aus seiner eigenen DNA, gespickt mit giftigen Proteinen, ausstoßen, um Mikroben außerhalb der Zelle zu fangen und zu töten — ein Vorgang namens NETose. Diese Intensität hat ihren Preis: Neutrophile leben nur kurz, überstehen im Blut nur Stunden und im Gewebe höchstens wenige Tage, weshalb das Knochenmark ständig Nachschub produziert. **Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The neutrophil (polymorphonuclear neutrophil, PMN) is a terminally differentiated granulocyte and the most abundant circulating leukocyte, characterised by a 2-5-lobed nucleus and cytoplasm packed with primary (azurophilic), secondary (specific) and tertiary (gelatinase) granules. Guided by a chemotactic gradient of complement fragments (C5a), bacterial formyl peptides and chemokines (notably CXCL8/IL-8), neutrophils undergo selectin-mediated rolling, integrin-mediated firm adhesion and transendothelial migration to reach sites of infection or tissue damage within minutes. Effector mechanisms include phagocytosis followed by phagolysosomal killing via myeloperoxidase-generated hypochlorous acid and granule proteases, NADPH-oxidase-dependent respiratory burst producing reactive oxygen species, degranulation, and, as a terminal effector response, NETosis — the release of decondensed chromatin decorated with granule and cytoplasmic antimicrobial proteins that immobilises and neutralises extracellular pathogens. Neutrophils rely predominantly on glycolysis for ATP production, consistent with their low mitochondrial content, and have a short circulating half-life (hours) before undergoing constitutive apoptosis or migrating into tissue, necessitating continuous replenishment via bone-marrow granulopoiesis under G-CSF control.  
🇩🇪 Der Neutrophile (polymorphkerniger neutrophiler Granulozyt, PMN) ist ein endgültig differenzierter Granulozyt und der häufigste zirkulierende Leukozyt, gekennzeichnet durch einen 2-5-lappigen Zellkern und ein Zytoplasma voller primärer (azurophiler), sekundärer (spezifischer) und tertiärer (Gelatinase-)Granula. Geleitet von einem chemotaktischen Gradienten aus Komplementfragmenten (C5a), bakteriellen Formylpeptiden und Chemokinen (insbesondere CXCL8/IL-8), durchlaufen Neutrophile selektinvermitteltes Rollen, integrinvermittelte feste Adhäsion und transendotheliale Migration, um Infektions- oder Gewebeschadensorte innerhalb von Minuten zu erreichen. Zu den Effektormechanismen zählen Phagozytose mit anschließender phagolysosomaler Abtötung durch myeloperoxidase-erzeugte hypochlorige Säure und Granula-Proteasen, der NADPH-Oxidase-abhängige respiratorische Burst mit reaktiven Sauerstoffspezies, Degranulation sowie, als terminale Effektorantwort, die NETose — die Freisetzung dekondensierten Chromatins, besetzt mit Granula- und zytoplasmatischen antimikrobiellen Proteinen, das extrazelluläre Erreger immobilisiert und neutralisiert. Neutrophile decken ihren ATP-Bedarf überwiegend durch Glykolyse, was zu ihrem geringen Mitochondriengehalt passt, und haben eine kurze zirkulierende Halbwertszeit (Stunden), bevor sie eine konstitutive Apoptose durchlaufen oder ins Gewebe wandern, sodass unter Kontrolle von G-CSF ständiger Nachschub durch Granulopoese im Knochenmark nötig ist.
