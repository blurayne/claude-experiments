## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

"White blood cell" (leukocyte) is not a single cell type but the umbrella category for every nucleated cell that circulates in the blood as part of the immune system — as distinct from the anucleate red blood cells (erythrocytes) and the cell-fragment platelets that share the bloodstream with them. A healthy adult carries roughly 4,000–11,000 leukocytes per microlitre of blood (about 1% of blood volume), all descended from multipotent haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. The category splits into **granulocytes** (neutrophils ~40–70% of circulating leukocytes, eosinophils, basophils) — named for the visible cytoplasmic granules that pick up different dyes — and **agranulocytes** (lymphocytes ~20–40%, i.e. T cells, B cells and NK cells; and monocytes ~2–10%, which mature into macrophages or dendritic cells in tissue). This atlas already gives six of those specific cell types their own dedicated entries; **this entry is the group they all belong to**, so the render must show that variety directly rather than picking one representative species.

Because this is a composition about the category rather than one organism, the defining visual idea is comparative: **every leukocyte has a nucleus; red blood cells do not.** Human erythrocytes are enucleated during maturation (the nucleus is extruded before the cell enters circulation) so they can pack in more haemoglobin and deform through capillaries — a mature RBC is a biconcave disc with no nucleus, no mitochondria and no other organelles. Leukocytes, by contrast, are fully nucleated cells, and the *shape* of that nucleus is itself diagnostic of which subtype you're looking at: granulocytes (e.g. the neutrophil) have a nucleus condensed into 2–5 rounded **lobes** joined by thin chromatin strands; small lymphocytes have a single large, dense, nearly **round** nucleus that fills most of the cell, leaving only a thin rim of cytoplasm; monocytes — the largest normal blood cell, up to ~20 µm — have a large, pale, characteristically **folded/kidney-bean- or horseshoe-shaped** nucleus with abundant grey-blue cytoplasm. Granulocytes also carry cytoplasmic granules (visible as stippling on a stained smear) that lymphocytes and monocytes largely lack.

Leukocytes are not permanent residents of the bloodstream: they use it purely as transport. Guided by a multistep "leukocyte adhesion cascade" (selectin-mediated rolling along the vessel wall, chemokine-triggered activation, integrin-mediated firm adhesion, then crawling to find a permissive junction), a leukocyte squeezes between — occasionally through — the endothelial cells lining a post-capillary venule in a process called **diapedesis** (transendothelial migration), extending an amoeboid **pseudopod** into the gap before the rest of the cell body follows into the surrounding tissue. This is the key idea behind "they do not stay in the blood, they only use it as a transport route" and is worth depicting directly: a white cell mid-squeeze through a visible gap in the endothelial wall, with red cells continuing to flow past in the lumen.

Sources: [Wikipedia — White blood cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_blood_cell), [OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology 18.3 — Erythrocytes (and surrounding sections on formed elements)](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/18-3-erythrocytes), [NCBI Bookshelf — Molecular Biology of the Cell, "Leukocytes"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26928/), [Vestweber 2015 / "Novel aspects in the regulation of the leukocyte adhesion cascade" (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2722029/).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)
- **Do NOT render a single leukocyte subtype standing in for the whole category** — this entry already has dedicated siblings (helper T cell, cytotoxic T cell, B cell, natural killer cell, macrophage, neutrophil); a lone neutrophil captioned "leukocyte" duplicates one of them and misses the point of a generic/umbrella entry. Every render must show **several different white cells together**, each with a visibly different nucleus shape.
- **No nucleus in the red blood cells.** Mammalian erythrocytes are anucleate — drawing a nucleus inside any red cell is a common and glaring error and defeats the entry's core teaching point.
- **No bacterial cell wall, nucleoid, flagellum or capsid anywhere** — every cell in this composition is a human blood cell, not a microbe.
- **No identical white cells** — if every "white cell" in the group has the same round shape and the same nucleus, it stops being a demonstration of variety and just becomes an unlabelled lymphocyte crowd.
- **No dense, opaque clumping of cells that hides individual nuclei** — leave visible gaps/negative space so each nucleus shape and the anucleate red cells are individually readable.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 "White blood cell" is not a single cell type but the umbrella term covering every nucleated defence cell that travels in the blood — neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes and monocytes, together making up roughly 4,000 to 10,000 cells per microlitre. What unites them, and what sets them apart from red blood cells, is the nucleus: red blood cells lose theirs during maturation to make room for haemoglobin, while every white cell keeps its full genetic control centre, and the shape of that nucleus — tightly lobed, round and dense, or loosely kidney-shaped — is often enough on its own to identify which subtype you're looking at under a microscope. A routine "differential count" simply tallies the proportions of each subtype, and shifts in that ratio are one of the most informative signals in a standard blood test, hinting at anything from a bacterial infection to a viral one to an allergic reaction. Crucially, white blood cells treat the bloodstream as infrastructure rather than habitat: they roll along the vessel wall, get chemically flagged down near a site of trouble, and squeeze bodily through the gap between two endothelial cells — a process called diapedesis — to reach the tissue where they're actually needed.  
🇩🇪 "Weißes Blutkörperchen" ist kein einzelner Zelltyp, sondern der Oberbegriff für jede kernhaltige Abwehrzelle, die mit dem Blut unterwegs ist — Neutrophile, Eosinophile, Basophile, Lymphozyten und Monozyten, zusammen etwa 4.000 bis 10.000 Zellen pro Mikroliter. Was sie eint und zugleich von den roten Blutkörperchen unterscheidet, ist der Zellkern: Rote Blutkörperchen verlieren ihn während der Reifung, um Platz für Hämoglobin zu schaffen, während jede weiße Zelle ihre komplette genetische Kommandozentrale behält — und schon die Form dieses Kerns, ob eng gelappt, rund und dicht, oder locker nierenförmig, reicht oft aus, um den jeweiligen Subtyp unter dem Mikroskop zu bestimmen. Ein routinemäßiges "Differentialblutbild" zählt schlicht die Anteile der einzelnen Subtypen, und Verschiebungen in diesem Verhältnis gehören zu den aussagekräftigsten Signalen eines Standard-Bluttests — sie deuten auf alles von einer bakteriellen über eine virale Infektion bis hin zu einer allergischen Reaktion hin. Entscheidend ist: Weiße Blutkörperchen behandeln den Blutkreislauf als Transportweg, nicht als Lebensraum. Sie rollen an der Gefäßwand entlang, werden in der Nähe eines Krankheitsherds chemisch aufgehalten und zwängen sich regelrecht durch die Lücke zwischen zwei Endothelzellen hindurch — ein Vorgang namens Diapedese —, um das Gewebe zu erreichen, in dem sie tatsächlich gebraucht werden.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 Leukocyte is the collective term for the nucleated cellular component of blood, comprising the granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils — classified by the tinctorial properties of their specific cytoplasmic granules) and the agranulocytes (lymphocytes and monocytes), all derived from multipotent haematopoietic stem cells via myeloid or lymphoid progenitor lineages in the bone marrow. Circulating reference range is approximately 4.0-11.0 x10^9/L. Leukocytes are morphologically distinguished chiefly by nuclear architecture: granulocytes exhibit a segmented (polymorphonuclear) nucleus of 2-5 lobes connected by thin chromatin strands; small lymphocytes present a single dense, round-to-ovoid nucleus with condensed heterochromatin and minimal surrounding cytoplasm (high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio); monocytes, the largest normal leukocyte (12-20 um), display an eccentric, reniform to horseshoe-shaped nucleus with abundant grey-blue cytoplasm. Unlike leukocytes, mature circulating erythrocytes are anucleate, having extruded their nucleus during the orthochromatic-normoblast-to-reticulocyte transition, and lack mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles. Leukocytes are functionally non-resident in the vasculature: they undergo a multistep adhesion cascade (selectin-mediated tethering and rolling, chemokine-triggered integrin activation, firm adhesion via ICAM-1/VCAM-1, intraluminal crawling) culminating in diapedesis — predominantly paracellular transendothelial migration through interendothelial junctions mediated by PECAM-1, JAM family proteins and CD99 — to enter tissue at sites of infection or inflammation. Clinically, the leukocyte differential count (relative proportions of each subtype) is a routine diagnostic index, with leukocytosis, leukopenia or a shift in differential proportions indicating infection, inflammation, haematologic malignancy or immune dysfunction.  
🇩🇪 Leukozyt ist der Sammelbegriff für die kernhaltigen zellulären Bestandteile des Blutes und umfasst die Granulozyten (neutrophile, eosinophile, basophile — klassifiziert nach den Färbeeigenschaften ihrer spezifischen Zytoplasmagranula) sowie die Agranulozyten (Lymphozyten und Monozyten), die alle über myeloische oder lymphatische Vorläuferlinien aus multipotenten hämatopoetischen Stammzellen im Knochenmark hervorgehen. Der zirkulierende Referenzbereich liegt bei etwa 4,0-11,0 x10^9/L. Morphologisch unterscheiden sich Leukozyten vor allem durch ihre Kernarchitektur: Granulozyten weisen einen segmentierten (polymorphkernigen) Zellkern mit 2-5 durch dünne Chromatinstränge verbundenen Lappen auf; kleine Lymphozyten zeigen einen einzelnen, dichten, rundlich-ovalen Zellkern mit kondensiertem Heterochromatin und minimalem umgebendem Zytoplasma (hohes Kern-Plasma-Verhältnis); Monozyten, die größten normalen Leukozyten (12-20 µm), besitzen einen exzentrischen, nieren- bis hufeisenförmigen Zellkern mit reichlich graublauem Zytoplasma. Im Gegensatz zu Leukozyten sind reife zirkulierende Erythrozyten kernlos, da sie ihren Zellkern während des Übergangs vom orthochromatischen Normoblasten zum Retikulozyten ausgestoßen haben, und ihnen fehlen zudem Mitochondrien und andere membranumschlossene Organellen. Leukozyten sind funktionell keine Dauerbewohner des Gefäßsystems: Sie durchlaufen eine mehrstufige Adhäsionskaskade (selektinvermitteltes Anhaften und Rollen, chemokinausgelöste Integrinaktivierung, feste Adhäsion über ICAM-1/VCAM-1, intraluminales Kriechen), die in der Diapedese gipfelt — überwiegend parazelluläre transendotheliale Migration durch interendotheliale Verbindungen, vermittelt durch PECAM-1, JAM-Proteine und CD99 —, um Gewebe an Infektions- oder Entzündungsherden zu erreichen. Klinisch ist das Leukozyten-Differentialblutbild (relative Anteile der einzelnen Subtypen) ein Routine-Diagnoseindex; Leukozytose, Leukopenie oder eine Verschiebung der Differentialanteile weisen auf Infektion, Entzündung, hämatologische Malignität oder Immundysfunktion hin.
