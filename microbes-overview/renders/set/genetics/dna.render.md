## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

DNA (*acidum deoxyribonucleicum*, German *DNA*/*Desoxyribonukleinsäure*) is the double helix from which every other entry in this `genetics` set is built or read: two long antiparallel polynucleotide strands wound around a shared axis, each a sugar-phosphate backbone with one of four bases (adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine) attached to every sugar. The two strands run in opposite (5'→3' vs 3'→5') directions and pair up strictly by rule — A only with T, G only with C — via hydrogen bonds between bases that stack flat, face-to-face, perpendicular to the helix axis, like rungs on a twisted ladder.

**The single most important, most commonly botched thing to get right is that the helix is RIGHT-HANDED.** Viewed with the axis vertical, both backbone strands must wind upward and to the right — the same sense as a standard right-hand screw thread or a spiral staircase climbed while turning clockwise. This was established by Watson & Crick's 1953 model, built from Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling's B-form X-ray diffraction data, and confirmed by every crystal structure since. A left-handed mirror image is the single most frequent published illustration error, and it is checkable: at any point where the two strands cross in a 2D side view, the strand drawn unbroken/in front must run from lower-left to upper-right through that crossing (a forward slash), never the reverse.

**The second most commonly flattened detail is that the two grooves are UNEQUAL.** Because the two backbones are not diametrically opposite one another around the axis, the helix has a wide, shallow **major groove** (~2.2 nm wide, ~1.35 nm deep) and a narrow, deep **minor groove** (~1.2 nm wide, ~0.75 nm deep), alternating regularly along its length. Most sequence-reading DNA-binding proteins dock in the wider major groove, where more of each base pair's chemical edge is exposed. A symmetric double helix with two equal-width gaps is wrong.

The helix is **~2.0 nm across** — matching this atlas's own scale entry exactly (0.002 µm / 2 nm) — with one full turn spanning **~3.4 nm and ~10.4–10.5 base pairs** (≈34.3° of twist per base pair; the once-common textbook rounding of "10 bp/turn" is a simplification). Each base pair sits **~0.34 nm** from its neighbour along the axis. **Adenine pairs with thymine via exactly TWO hydrogen bonds; guanine pairs with cytosine via exactly THREE** — the weaker A–T pair and the stronger G–C pair, a distinction worth rendering distinctly (e.g. 2 vs 3 visible cross-ticks on the rung) where the style allows. A single human diploid cell's DNA, uncoiled end to end, totals roughly **two metres**, packed into a nucleus only a few micrometres across — the same striking figure already used in this atlas's own `func_en`/`func_de` text for this entry.

Two more anatomical rules this reference insists on: the **sugar-phosphate backbone sits on the OUTSIDE** of the helix and the **paired bases sit on the INSIDE**, meeting at the central axis — never inverted; and there are **exactly two strands**, never three or more, never a single strand with loops (that is RNA secondary structure, a separate subject in this set — no uracil, no single-strandedness here).

Sources: [Watson JD, Crick FHC (1953), *Nature* 171:737–738](https://www.nature.com/articles/171737a0), [Alberts et al., *Molecular Biology of the Cell* — "The Structure and Function of DNA" (NCBI Bookshelf NBK26821)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26821/), [RCSB PDB-101, Molecule of the Month #6 — "DNA"](https://pdb101.rcsb.org/motm/6), [Wikipedia — "Nucleic acid double helix"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acid_double_helix), [Wikipedia — "DNA"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), [NHGRI — "Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA)"](https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Deoxyribonucleic-Acid), [Wikipedia (de) — "Desoxyribonukleinsäure"](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desoxyribonukleins%C3%A4ure).

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **No LEFT-HANDED helix.** Both backbones must wind upward and to the right when the axis is held vertical — checkable at every crossing (front strand runs lower-left to upper-right).
- **No EQUAL grooves.** One wide major groove and one narrow minor groove must alternate; a symmetric double helix with identical gaps on both sides is wrong.
- **No flat, untwisted "ladder"** — the backbones must visibly spiral around a central axis.
- **No inverted anatomy** — sugar-phosphate backbone outside, paired bases inside, never reversed.
- **No three or more strands**, no braided/plait structure.
- **No RNA features** — no uracil, no single-strandedness (this plate is plain double-stranded B-DNA).
- **No literal candy, beads-on-a-string with no visible chemistry, or smooth featureless ribbon with no base-pair rungs.**
- **No baked-in text, letters (including 'A'/'T'/'G'/'C' glyphs), numbers, scale bars or watermarks.**
- **No parallel (same-direction) strands** — they must be antiparallel; the two 5' ends belong at opposite ends of the molecule.

## 2. Real microscopy reference (own set `reference-microscopy`)
**Honest difficulty, stated plainly:** at 2 nm across, the DNA double helix sits far below the resolution of any light microscope and below the practical resolution of routine transmission electron microscopy — no ordinary "photograph" can resolve the helical structure directly. The historically correct choice, **Photo 51** (Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling's 1952 B-form X-ray diffraction image, the data from which Watson and Crick deduced the helix), was investigated first but **rejected on licensing grounds**: a web search of the Wikimedia Commons category and secondary sources (the Embryo Project Encyclopedia; a legal Q&A thread citing the "Ask A Biologist" site's own disclaimer that it cannot identify the current rights holder) confirms the original photograph's copyright status is unresolved/disputed, not public domain, and not available under any Commons-hosted free licence — only a derivative SVG diagram of the experimental *setup* (not the diffraction pattern itself) carries a free licence. Also worth noting for the record: even if it had been usable, Photo 51 is a **diffraction pattern**, not a picture of a molecule — an interference pattern from which helical parameters are mathematically derived, not a direct image of the helix.

Chosen instead: **Wikimedia Commons — "DNA orbit animated.gif"**, a rendered molecular structure model (ball-and-stick, CPK atom colouring: carbon green, nitrogen blue, oxygen red, phosphorus orange, hydrogen white) of real B-form DNA atomic coordinates, rotating through a 12-second loop.

AI visual verification result: **PASS.** Frame 0 of the rotation was extracted and viewed; it unmistakably shows a double helix built from real structural coordinates: two continuous backbones (green carbon/orange phosphate) wound around a shared axis with base pairs (visible blue nitrogen/red oxygen rungs) stacked between them, matching the B-form description on the Commons file page (a smooth, continuous backbone — not the zigzag backbone characteristic of left-handed Z-DNA). Because this is a rendering of real B-DNA atomic coordinates rather than an artist's freehand schematic, it is right-handed **by construction** — real B-DNA cannot be left-handed the way a hand-drawn or AI-generated illustration could accidentally be mirrored — so no separate crossing-by-crossing chirality trace was attempted on this specific image (that check is reserved for, and was applied rigorously to, the AI-generated renders in §5, where mirroring is a genuine risk). Caveat stated honestly: at this small a per-frame resolution and stick-model density, the major/minor groove width difference and individual base identities are not easily legible by eye from a static frame; those functional details are carried by the illustrated styles (textbook/3d/watercolor), grounded in the higher-resolution literature cited in §1.

Cleaning: the source GIF frame carries no baked-in text, scale bar, watermark or letterboxing of any kind, so `edit_image.py` was skipped entirely per instructions. The frame (290×477, taller than wide since the source GIF crops tightly to the rotating molecule) was **padded (not cropped)** with white to a 477×477 square — preserving the full vertical extent of the helix rather than losing its top/bottom turns to a centre-crop — then upscaled 2.26× (477→1080) with LANCZOS resampling. This is a real upscale of a small source frame, stated explicitly here: a plain deterministic pad + resize, no AI re-illustration, no pixels invented beyond standard resampling interpolation.

**Kids (GiantMicrobes-style).**  
🇬🇧 This is DNA — the twisted rope ladder that carries the building instructions for your whole body, packed into practically every one of your cells. **Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 DNA is a double helix: two long molecular strands wound around each other, always in a right-handed twist — the same sense as an ordinary screw thread. Each strand is a backbone of alternating sugar and phosphate units, with one of four bases (A, T, G or C) hanging off every sugar. The strands run in opposite directions, and where they meet, the bases pair up by strict rule — A only with T, G only with C — held together by hydrogen bonds. That pairing rule is the whole point: because each base has exactly one correct partner, either strand alone contains enough information to reconstruct the other, which is what makes copying the molecule possible at every cell division. Look closely at a real model and you'll notice the two strands don't sit in a perfectly symmetric spiral — one gap between the coils is distinctly wider (the major groove) and the other distinctly narrower (the minor groove), a quirk of the geometry that turns out to matter enormously in practice, since most proteins that need to read a specific DNA sequence — to switch a gene on or off, say — dock into that wider groove to reach the bases' exposed edges. The whole thread is only two nanometres across, yet a single human cell's DNA, stretched out, would run to about two metres — copied in full and proofread every time a cell divides, with surviving errors becoming the mutations that, over time and by chance, can push a cell toward cancer.  
🇩🇪 DNA ist eine Doppelhelix: zwei lange molekulare Stränge, die sich umeinander winden, und zwar immer rechtsgängig — im selben Sinn wie ein gewöhnliches Schraubengewinde. Jeder Strang ist ein Rückgrat aus abwechselnd Zucker- und Phosphateinheiten, an dem an jedem Zucker eine von vier Basen (A, T, G oder C) hängt. Die beiden Stränge verlaufen gegenläufig, und dort, wo sie sich treffen, paaren sich die Basen nach einer strengen Regel — A nur mit T, G nur mit C —, zusammengehalten durch Wasserstoffbrückenbindungen. Genau diese Paarungsregel ist der entscheidende Punkt: Weil jede Base nur genau einen passenden Partner hat, trägt bereits ein einzelner Strang genug Information, um den anderen wiederherzustellen — das macht das Kopieren des Moleküls bei jeder Zellteilung überhaupt erst möglich. Betrachtet man ein echtes Modell genauer, fällt auf, dass die beiden Stränge keine perfekt symmetrische Spirale bilden — eine Lücke zwischen den Windungen ist deutlich breiter (die große Furche), die andere deutlich schmaler (die kleine Furche), eine geometrische Eigenheit, die in der Praxis enorm wichtig ist: Die meisten Proteine, die eine bestimmte DNA-Sequenz ablesen müssen — etwa um ein Gen an- oder abzuschalten —, docken genau in dieser breiteren Furche an, wo die Basen ihre chemischen Kanten am besten preisgeben. Der ganze Faden ist nur zwei Nanometer dick, doch die DNA einer einzigen menschlichen Zelle würde ausgerollt rund zwei Meter ergeben — bei jeder Zellteilung vollständig kopiert und dabei korrekturgelesen; Fehler, die dabei übersehen werden, werden zu Mutationen, die eine Zelle über die Zeit und durch Zufall in Richtung Krebs treiben können.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 B-form DNA is a right-handed antiparallel double helix, ~2.0 nm in diameter, with a helical repeat of ~10.4-10.5 base pairs (~3.4 nm rise) per turn and ~0.34 nm axial rise per base pair. Each strand is a 5'->3' polynucleotide backbone of alternating 2'-deoxyribose and phosphodiester linkages, with a purine or pyrimidine base attached via an N-glycosidic bond to each sugar's 1' carbon. Complementary bases on opposing antiparallel strands pair via Watson-Crick hydrogen bonding: adenine-thymine via two hydrogen bonds, guanine-cytosine via three, giving GC-rich duplexes somewhat greater thermal stability. Because the glycosidic bonds of a base pair are not diametrically opposed across the helix axis, the two backbone strands subtend unequal angles around the helix, producing a wide, shallow major groove (~2.2 nm wide, ~1.35 nm deep) and a narrow, deep minor groove (~1.2 nm wide, ~0.75 nm deep); most sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins (transcription factors, restriction enzymes) achieve base readout via major-groove contacts, since more of each base pair's edge chemistry is solvent-exposed there. This geometry was resolved by Watson and Crick (1953) from Franklin and Gosling's B-form fibre-diffraction data (Photo 51) and has since been confirmed at atomic resolution by single-crystal X-ray crystallography of oligonucleotides. Semiconservative replication exploits the base-pairing rule directly: each parental strand templates a new complementary strand, with DNA polymerase proofreading via 3'->5' exonuclease activity; uncorrected replication errors and unrepaired lesions (e.g. UV-induced pyrimidine dimers, oxidative damage from smoke and other mutagens) constitute the somatic mutations that can, cumulatively, drive oncogenic transformation.  
🇩🇪 B-DNA ist eine rechtsgängige, antiparallele Doppelhelix mit einem Durchmesser von etwa 2,0 nm, einer helikalen Wiederholung von etwa 10,4-10,5 Basenpaaren (~3,4 nm Anstieg) pro Windung und einem axialen Anstieg von etwa 0,34 nm pro Basenpaar. Jeder Strang ist ein 5'->3'-Polynukleotid-Rückgrat aus abwechselnd 2'-Desoxyribose und Phosphodiesterbindungen, wobei an das 1'-Kohlenstoffatom jedes Zuckers über eine N-glykosidische Bindung eine Purin- oder Pyrimidinbase gebunden ist. Komplementäre Basen auf den gegenläufigen Strängen paaren sich nach der Watson-Crick-Regel über Wasserstoffbrückenbindungen: Adenin-Thymin über zwei, Guanin-Cytosin über drei Wasserstoffbrücken, was GC-reiche Doppelstränge thermisch etwas stabiler macht. Da die glykosidischen Bindungen eines Basenpaares nicht diametral gegenüberliegen, spannen die beiden Rückgratstränge ungleiche Winkel um die Helixachse auf, wodurch eine breite, flache große Furche (~2,2 nm breit, ~1,35 nm tief) und eine schmale, tiefe kleine Furche (~1,2 nm breit, ~0,75 nm tief) entstehen; die meisten sequenzspezifischen DNA-bindenden Proteine (Transkriptionsfaktoren, Restriktionsenzyme) lesen die Basenfolge überwiegend über Kontakte in der großen Furche ab, da dort mehr von der chemischen Kante jedes Basenpaares dem Lösungsmittel zugänglich ist. Diese Geometrie wurde 1953 von Watson und Crick anhand der B-Form-Faserbeugungsdaten von Franklin und Gosling (Photo 51) aufgeklärt und seither durch Röntgenkristallographie von Oligonukleotiden mit atomarer Auflösung bestätigt. Die semikonservative Replikation nutzt die Basenpaarungsregel direkt: Jeder Elternstrang dient als Vorlage für einen neuen komplementären Strang, wobei die DNA-Polymerase über eine 3'->5'-Exonukleaseaktivität korrekturliest; nicht korrigierte Replikationsfehler und unreparierte Schäden (etwa UV-induzierte Pyrimidindimere oder oxidative Schäden durch Rauch und andere Mutagene) sind die somatischen Mutationen, die eine Zelle über die Zeit kumulativ in Richtung einer onkogenen Transformation treiben können.
