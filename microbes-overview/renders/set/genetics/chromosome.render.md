**Set:** `genetics` · **Microbe key:** `chromosome`
**Short description:** The genome in transport mode: two identical sister chromatids joined at a pinched centromere, each about 700 nm thick, built by winding a 2 nm DNA double helix around histone spools into nucleosomes, then into a 30 nm chromatin fibre, then into looped domains — roughly 10,000-fold compaction into a body about 5 µm long. ## 1. Scientific reference (the yardstick for verification)

A chromosome (*chromosoma*, German *Chromosom*) is not a cell and not an organism: it is one enormously long DNA molecule together with the proteins that package it, roughly half its mass DNA and half protein. The shape everyone pictures — the **X** — is a *condensed metaphase chromosome*: two identical **sister chromatids** lying alongside one another, joined at a pinched waist called the **centromere**, with all four arm tips capped by **telomeres**. A mid-sized human chromosome in this state runs about **5 µm** end to end, matching this atlas's scale entry exactly, and each individual chromatid is about **700 nm** across, so the two-chromatid body reads roughly 1.4 µm wide at the arms. Its DNA content is about **0.3 pg**, again consistent with the atlas figure: a diploid human cell after replication carries ~13.2 pg of DNA spread across 46 chromosomes, i.e. ~0.29 pg each.

**The single most important thing to get right — and the thing this subject is most often drawn wrong — is that the X is a temporary transport form.** It exists only from prophase through metaphase and into early anaphase. For the overwhelming majority of a cell's life the very same DNA lies decondensed as **chromatin**, a diffuse tangle filling the nucleus, with no X anywhere in sight. This atlas's own entry text says so explicitly ("Most of the time, though, the genome lies unwound as chromatin; the X shape exists only during division"), so no render may contradict it. There is a **second timing rule** that is just as often missed: the X only exists *after* replication. In G1, before the DNA has been copied, a chromosome is a **single chromatid** — one rod, one thread, not an X. An X is always a picture of a chromosome that has already been duplicated and is waiting to be pulled apart. And a third: in a human cell at metaphase the **nuclear envelope has broken down** (open mitosis), so metaphase chromosomes are not sitting inside an intact nucleus at all. Drawing 46 tidy Xs inside a round nucleus gets all three rules wrong at once.

**The packing hierarchy is the teaching spine**, and showing it is the honest way to make the "only sometimes an X" point *visible*, because it shows the X being built out of something that normally is not an X. Smallest to largest: the naked **DNA double helix**, 2 nm across and right-handed; **nucleosomes**, in which 147 bp of DNA is wound about **1.65 turns** around a **histone octamer** (two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4), strung together by short stretches of linker DNA with one histone H1 apiece to give the 11 nm **"beads on a string"** fibre; those packed into the **~30 nm chromatin fibre**; that thrown into **looped domains** anchored on a non-histone protein **scaffold**, with condensin driving the mitotic compaction and cohesin gluing the two sisters together; and finally the fully condensed **metaphase chromatid**, ~700 nm across. End to end this is a compaction of roughly **10,000-fold**, against about 1,000-fold in interphase.

**Two chirality facts sit at different scales and must not be conflated.** The DNA **double helix** itself is **right-handed** (B-form) and must always be drawn so — with the axis vertical, the strand drawn unbroken and in front at each crossing runs from lower-left to upper-right, like a forward slash. Separately, the way DNA **wraps around a histone octamer** is a **left-handed superhelical** coil. A left-handed wrap around a nucleosome bead is correct; a left-handed double helix never is. This matters practically here: on the sibling **DNA** subject in this same set, `gemini-2.5-flash-image` rendered a *left-handed* helix on 3 of its 4 styles and had to be explicitly corrected, so every chromosome prompt that shows a helix segment carries the forward-slash test and every render is checked against it.

The **centromere** is a genuine **constriction** — visibly narrower than the arms — not a bead or clasp stuck between four loose rods; each chromatid runs continuously through it from one arm to the other. It is not necessarily at the middle, and its position defines the classic shapes: **metacentric** (near the middle), **submetacentric** (clearly off-centre) and **acrocentric** (near one end — human chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22). In humans the underlying DNA is alpha-satellite repeat sequence, but the centromere is specified **epigenetically**, by nucleosomes carrying the histone H3 variant **CENP-A**, rather than by sequence alone. On the centromere assembles the **kinetochore**, a large multiprotein plate — **one per chromatid**, so a metaphase chromosome carries **two**, sitting back-to-back on opposite faces of the waist. That opposition is the entire point: it makes each chromatid face a different spindle pole, so the microtubules that attach pull the two copies in opposite directions. The arms either side of the centromere are the short **p arm** (from French *petit*) and the long **q arm** — the standard account of that name being simply that q is the letter after p; the frequently-repeated derivation from French *queue* is folklore rather than established etymology.

**Telomeres** cap **all four** arm ends — a common illustration error is capping only two. In humans they are tandem repeats of **TTAGGG**, folded into a protective structure that stops the cell's DNA-repair machinery mistaking a natural chromosome end for a broken strand and fusing it to a neighbour. Because DNA polymerase cannot fully copy the very end of a linear molecule (the **end-replication problem**), telomeres shorten a little at every division; telomerase rebuilds them in germline and stem cells, and its reactivation is one of the hallmarks of cancer — which is why this entry sits immediately before the cancer set.

Humans have **46 chromosomes, 23 pairs**, one of each pair from each parent. Sorting a stained metaphase spread into those numbered pairs gives a **karyotype** — the real-microscopy view used in §2.

**One honest caveat.** The ~30 nm fibre is the classic textbook rung and is well characterised for chromatin *in vitro* and in isolated preparations, but whether a regular 30 nm fibre actually exists inside living interphase nuclei has been genuinely contested in the modern literature, with a good deal of evidence favouring a more irregular, heterogeneous arrangement. It is kept in the hierarchy here because it is the level every reader will meet in a textbook and it makes the ladder legible — but it is not presented as settled.

**Colour is convention, and unusually pointedly so for this subject.** A chromosome has no colour in life. The *chromo-* in its name comes from the fact that these bodies took up **dye** — they were named for how brightly they stained — and the familiar banded karyotype is Giemsa stain, not pigment. Every colour in these renders is a teaching choice, kept consistent across all four styles.

### Do NOT draw (scientifically misleading)

- **No 46 Xs inside an intact nucleus.** At metaphase — the only time the X exists — the nuclear envelope has **broken down**. An intact nucleus full of Xs is wrong twice over.
- **No X inside a normal interphase nucleus.** In interphase the same DNA is decondensed chromatin. This is *the* widespread misconception about chromosomes and the entry text explicitly contradicts it.
- **No unreplicated chromosome drawn as an X.** Before S phase a chromosome is **one** chromatid — a single rod. The X appears only after duplication.
- **No three or more chromatids, and no mismatched halves.** Exactly **two**, and they are **identical** — same length, shape and banding. Two visibly different bodies would be two different chromosomes.
- **No centromere as a ball, bead, ring or clasp** with four loose rods stuck onto it. It is a **constriction** in continuous material.
- **No capping only two arm tips.** A two-chromatid chromosome has **four** ends; all four carry telomeres.
- **No LEFT-HANDED DNA double helix** wherever a helix appears — front/unbroken strand runs lower-left → upper-right at every crossing (a forward slash). A known, measured failure mode of flash on this atlas's DNA subject.
- **Do not conflate the two chiralities** — right-handed double helix, left-handed *superhelical* wrap around the nucleosome. The second is correct; the first must never be mirrored.
- **No flat untwisted DNA "ladder"** — the naked helix must visibly spiral.
- **No nucleosomes as blobs threaded on a wire.** The DNA wraps **around** the outside of each histone spool, not through it.
- **No spindle microtubules attaching to both kinetochores from the same side** — they sit back-to-back and face opposite poles.
- **No baked-in text, letters, numbers, chromosome indices, karyotype labels, scale bars or watermarks.**
- **No border, frame, vignette, letterbox, mat, or sheet-of-paper-on-a-surface.** The background must reach all four edges.
- **No literal cartoon X-letter glyph, candy/toy shape, or smooth featureless capsule** with no constriction or texture.

**Composition choice, stated rather than left implicit:** the illustrated hero is drawn **submetacentric** — centromere clearly off-centre, giving a visibly short p arm and a long q arm. On a perfectly symmetric metacentric X there is no short arm to point a label at, so the p/q distinction becomes unlabelable and the render quietly teaches that the two arms are the same length. Submetacentric is also the commonest human configuration. A metacentric hero would be defensible — it is the iconic schoolbook shape, and several human chromosomes really are metacentric — but it is the weaker teaching choice here.

Every chromosome is individually separated and readable — sister chromatids and the centromere constriction are visible on the larger ones, and the differing centromere positions between groups (metacentric through acrocentric) are plainly visible, which is precisely the teaching point. It is also worth saying plainly what this image teaches by *contrast* with the illustrated styles: real chromosomes are far less X-like than the textbook glyph. Many here read as two narrow parallel chromatids barely splayed at all, short banded rods a few micrometres long — the tidy symmetric X is a schematic convention, not a photograph.

An **SEM of isolated metaphase chromosomes** was the brief's first-choice option and would have been the more striking image, since a real chromosome SEM shows a rough, knobbly, almost coral-like surface nothing like the smooth textbook X. It was **not used**: a search of Wikimedia Commons (Category:Human chromosomes, Category:Metaphase, and the scanning-electron-microscopy categories) surfaced no freely-licensed SEM of human metaphase chromosomes, and the well-known plates in circulation are copyrighted and unavailable under PD/CC0/CC BY/CC BY-SA. Rather than add a second NonCommercial outlier to this atlas, no SEM was used. An **unsorted metaphase spread** was also considered and passed over: it is in some ways a more honest view (chromosomes as they actually fell on the slide), but they overlap and clump, so individual chromatids, centromeres and arms are much harder to read — which the project's own verification rubric explicitly rejects.

Cleaning: the plate carries **no baked-in text, numbers, scale bar, watermark or letterboxing of any kind** — confirmed on a magnified crop and with a hard near-white contrast stretch — so `edit_image.py` was **skipped entirely** per instructions. Because a centre-crop to square clipped the outer columns (chromosome 1 on the left, the Y on the right), the plate was instead **padded, not cropped**, to 3084 × 3084 with its own sampled background colour (pure white, 255,255,255 — so no visible seam), then **LANCZOS downscaled 0.350×** to 1080 × 1080. This is a downscale of a high-resolution original: no upscaling, no invented pixels, no AI re-illustration.

## 3. Audience descriptions (EN + DE)

**Kids (GiantMicrobes-style).**  
🇬🇧 This X shape is a chromosome — but here's the trick: it only looks like an X for a few minutes, right before a cell splits in two. The rest of the time, all day and every day, that very same DNA is unwound and loose inside the nucleus, like a big tangled ball of wool, not an X at all. When it's finally time to divide, the wool gets wound around tiny spools called histones, and those spools pack together tighter and tighter until they squeeze into this neat little bundle — easy to grab and hand out without anything getting knotted. Look closely and you'll see it's really TWO identical strands standing side by side, pinched together in the middle at a spot called the centromere — that pinch is where a cell's towing ropes grab hold to pull the two copies apart, one for each new cell. The tips of all four arms wear little caps, called telomeres, that stop the ends from fraying — though they do get a tiny bit shorter every single time a cell divides. You have 46 of these packages in nearly every cell — 23 from your mum, 23 from your dad.  
🇩🇪 Diese X-Form ist ein Chromosom — aber hier ist der Trick: Es sieht nur für ein paar Minuten wie ein X aus, kurz bevor sich eine Zelle in zwei teilt. Die restliche Zeit, jeden Tag, liegt genau dieselbe DNA aufgerollt und locker im Zellkern, wie ein großes, verknotetes Wollknäuel — überhaupt kein X. Wenn es endlich Zeit zur Teilung ist, wird die Wolle um winzige Spulen namens Histone gewickelt, und diese Spulen packen sich immer enger zusammen, bis sie sich zu diesem ordentlichen kleinen Bündel zusammenpressen — leicht zu greifen und zu verteilen, ohne dass sich etwas verknotet. Schau genau hin, dann siehst du: Es sind eigentlich ZWEI identische Stränge, die nebeneinanderstehen und in der Mitte an einer Stelle namens Zentromer zusammengezwickt sind — genau dort fassen die Zugseile der Zelle an, um die beiden Kopien auseinanderzuziehen, eine für jede neue Zelle. Die Spitzen aller vier Arme tragen kleine Kappen, Telomere genannt, die verhindern, dass die Enden ausfransen — auch wenn sie bei jeder Zellteilung ein kleines bisschen kürzer werden. Du hast 46 dieser Pakete in fast jeder Zelle — 23 von deiner Mama, 23 von deinem Papa.

**Adults (popular science, health).**  
🇬🇧 A chromosome in its famous X shape is a snapshot of one very specific moment: a cell caught in the act of dividing. For the rest of the cell's life — the vast majority of it — this same DNA sits decondensed inside the nucleus as chromatin, an unwound tangle with no X in sight; it only coils down into this compact transport form during mitosis, once the DNA has already been copied. What you're looking at is really two identical sister chromatids, produced by that copying step, pinched together at the centromere and still holding hands until the moment the cell physically pulls them apart. Getting from a loose thread to that dense little X takes real engineering: DNA first winds around barrel-shaped histone proteins to form beads called nucleosomes, those beads pack into a thicker fibre, and that fibre loops and coils until the whole thing is squeezed roughly ten-thousand-fold smaller than it started. The caps on the four arm tips, the telomeres, matter well beyond division — because a cell's copying machinery can't quite finish the very last stretch of a chromosome end, telomeres shorten with every division, acting as a kind of biological odometer for a cell; stem cells and cancer cells get around the limit by switching an enzyme called telomerase back on, which is one reason telomere biology sits so close to ageing and cancer research.  
🇩🇪 Ein Chromosom in seiner berühmten X-Form ist eine Momentaufnahme eines ganz bestimmten Augenblicks: einer Zelle, die gerade beim Teilen erwischt wurde. Den Rest ihres Lebens — den weitaus größten Teil davon — liegt dieselbe DNA entspiralisiert im Zellkern als Chromatin vor, ein aufgelöstes Knäuel ohne jede Spur eines X; erst während der Mitose, nachdem die DNA bereits kopiert wurde, wickelt sie sich zu dieser kompakten Transportform auf. Was man hier sieht, sind eigentlich zwei identische Schwesterchromatiden, entstanden durch genau diesen Kopiervorgang, am Zentromer zusammengezwickt und noch verbunden, bis die Zelle sie im richtigen Moment tatsächlich auseinanderzieht. Vom losen Faden zum dichten kleinen X ist es ein regelrechtes Konstruktionsprojekt: Die DNA wickelt sich zunächst um fassförmige Histon-Proteine und bildet Kügelchen, sogenannte Nukleosomen, diese Kügelchen packen sich zu einer dickeren Faser zusammen, und diese Faser windet und schleift sich, bis das Ganze etwa zehntausendfach kleiner gepresst ist als am Anfang. Die Kappen an den vier Armspitzen, die Telomere, sind weit über die Zellteilung hinaus bedeutsam — weil die Kopiermaschinerie der Zelle das allerletzte Stück eines Chromosomenendes nicht ganz fertigstellen kann, werden Telomere bei jeder Teilung ein wenig kürzer und wirken so wie eine Art biologischer Kilometerzähler; Stammzellen und Krebszellen umgehen diese Grenze, indem sie ein Enzym namens Telomerase wieder aktivieren — ein Grund, warum die Telomerbiologie der Altersforschung und der Krebsforschung so nahesteht.

**Scientific.**  
🇬🇧 The X-shaped structure conventionally called 'a chromosome' is specifically a condensed METAPHASE chromosome: two identical sister chromatids, generated by S-phase DNA replication and held together by the cohesin complex, joined at a primary constriction (the centromere) whose position (metacentric, submetacentric or acrocentric) is specified epigenetically by CENP-A-containing nucleosomes rather than by the underlying alpha-satellite sequence alone. For the remainder of the cell cycle (G1, S, G2) — the great majority of it — the same DNA exists as decondensed interphase chromatin; the compact mitotic form appears only from prophase through metaphase/early anaphase, once the nuclear envelope has broken down (open mitosis). Compaction is hierarchical: the ~2 nm B-form double helix wraps ~1.65 left-handed superhelical turns (147 bp) around a histone octamer (2× H2A, H2B, H3, H4) to form a nucleosome core particle, linked by variable linker DNA and a single H1 into an ~11 nm 'beads-on-a-string' fibre; this packs into a ~30 nm chromatin fibre (a level well characterised in vitro but genuinely contested as a regular structure in living interphase nuclei) and is further organised into looped domains on a non-histone scaffold, condensin driving mitotic loop extrusion, to yield the ~700 nm metaphase chromatid — roughly 10,000-fold compaction versus ~1,000-fold in interphase. On each centromere assembles a kinetochore, a large multiprotein plate that is present once per chromatid (two per metaphase chromosome), oriented back-to-back so that sister kinetochores bi-orient toward opposite spindle poles, the geometry underlying accurate anaphase segregation and enforced by the spindle-assembly checkpoint. Telomeres, tandem TTAGGG repeats in humans, cap all four arm termini, preventing the DNA-damage response from processing natural ends as double-strand breaks; because conventional DNA polymerases cannot fully replicate a linear template's terminus (the end-replication problem), telomeres shorten with each division in most somatic cells, while telomerase reactivation — a recognised hallmark of cancer — restores replicative immortality in germline, stem and most malignant cells.  
🇩🇪 Die X-förmige Struktur, die man landläufig 'ein Chromosom' nennt, ist genauer gesagt ein kondensiertes METAPHASE-Chromosom: zwei identische, durch die DNA-Replikation in der S-Phase entstandene Schwesterchromatiden, die vom Cohesin-Komplex zusammengehalten werden und an einer primären Einschnürung (dem Zentromer) verbunden sind, dessen Position (metazentrisch, submetazentrisch oder akrozentrisch) epigenetisch durch CENP-A-haltige Nukleosomen festgelegt wird und nicht allein durch die zugrunde liegende Alpha-Satelliten-Sequenz. Während des übrigen Zellzyklus (G1, S, G2) — also die meiste Zeit — liegt dieselbe DNA als dekondensiertes Interphase-Chromatin vor; die kompakte mitotische Form erscheint erst von der Prophase bis zur Metaphase/frühen Anaphase, nachdem die Kernhülle aufgelöst wurde (offene Mitose). Die Kompaktierung erfolgt hierarchisch: Die etwa 2 nm dicke B-Form-Doppelhelix windet sich in etwa 1,65 linksgängigen Superhelix-Windungen (147 Basenpaare) um ein Histon-Oktamer (je 2× H2A, H2B, H3, H4) und bildet so ein Nukleosomen-Kernpartikel, das über variable Linker-DNA und je ein Histon H1 zu einer etwa 11 nm dicken 'Perlenkette' verknüpft wird; diese packt sich zu einer etwa 30 nm dicken Chromatinfaser (ein in vitro gut charakterisiertes, als reguläre Struktur im lebenden Interphase-Kern aber tatsächlich umstrittenes Niveau) und wird weiter zu Schleifendomänen auf einem Nicht-Histon-Gerüst organisiert, wobei Condensin die mitotische Schleifenextrusion antreibt, bis das etwa 700 nm dicke Metaphase-Chromatid entsteht — eine rund 10.000-fache Kompaktierung gegenüber etwa 1.000-fach in der Interphase. Auf jedem Zentromer lagert sich ein Kinetochor an, ein großer Multiprotein-Komplex, der einmal pro Chromatid vorliegt (also zweimal pro Metaphase-Chromosom) und Rücken an Rücken orientiert ist, sodass sich die Schwester-Kinetochoren zu entgegengesetzten Spindelpolen ausrichten — die Geometrie, die der korrekten Chromosomentrennung in der Anaphase zugrunde liegt und vom Spindel-Kontrollpunkt überwacht wird. Telomere, beim Menschen tandemartig wiederholte TTAGGG-Sequenzen, kappen alle vier Armenden und verhindern, dass die DNA-Schadensantwort natürliche Enden als Doppelstrangbrüche verarbeitet; da konventionelle DNA-Polymerasen das Ende einer linearen Vorlage nicht vollständig replizieren können (das Endreplikationsproblem), verkürzen sich Telomere in den meisten somatischen Zellen mit jeder Teilung, während die Reaktivierung der Telomerase — ein anerkanntes Kennzeichen von Krebs — in Keimbahn-, Stamm- und den meisten malignen Zellen replikative Unsterblichkeit wiederherstellt.
