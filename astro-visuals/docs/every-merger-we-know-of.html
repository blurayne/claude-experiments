Every merger we know of

Our galaxy is not a thing that formed. It is a thing that keeps happening — assembled from other galaxies, still eating two of them, and with its largest meals still ahead.

How we know: galactic archaeology

A galaxy that swallows another does not digest it evenly. The victim's stars keep their orbits for billions of years, spread into streams and shells that look like nothing in particular on the sky but stand out sharply when you plot stars by energy and angular momentum instead of position. Gaia measured the motions of more than a billion stars precisely enough to make that plot, and the Milky Way's history fell out of it in clumps. This is why almost everything below dates from 2018 or later: the evidence was always in the sky, but the instrument to read it is recent.

What we have already eaten

  • ~11 GyrKraken (also called Heracles) The earliest large merger we can still identify, buried deep in the inner Galaxy. It happened when the Milky Way was perhaps a quarter of its present mass, which makes it relatively the most violent thing that ever happened to us, even though the galaxy it brought was small.
  • ~10 GyrGaia-Enceladus / the Sausage The big one. A galaxy perhaps a quarter of the Milky Way's then-mass, on a head-on plunge — its stars ended up on long radial orbits, which is what gives the "sausage" its shape in velocity space. Most of the inner stellar halo is its debris, and the collision is the leading explanation for why the Milky Way has a thick disk at all: the pre-existing disk was heated and puffed up by the impact.
  • ~9 GyrSequoia, Thamnos, the Helmi streams Smaller courses in the same meal era, each identified as its own clump of stars with a shared history — Sequoia notably on retrograde orbits, running around the Galaxy the wrong way.

What we are eating now

The Sagittarius dwarf was found in 1994 on the far side of the galactic centre, mid-destruction. Its stream of shredded stars wraps the entire sky more than once. It has plunged through the disk several times, and those crossings are the leading suspect for structures we can measure today: the disk's warp, the vertical corrugations rippling through it, and possibly bursts of star formation — one of which may coincide with the Sun's own formation, which would make us, in a small way, a product of a galactic collision.

The Magellanic Clouds are the other ongoing case, and the more consequential one. Recent work suggests the LMC is far more massive than long assumed — massive enough that its gravity pulls the Milky Way's centre off course, which is precisely why it complicates predictions of the Andromeda encounter. It is expected to merge with us in roughly 2 billion years, well before Andromeda arrives.

What is coming

  • +2 GyrThe Large Magellanic Cloud merges The next real merger, and one that will feed the central black hole and disturb the disk long before Andromeda is close.
  • +4.5 GyrAndromeda's first passage ~95 kpc — the disks never touch. They trade bridges and tails, and dynamical friction begins draining the orbit.
  • +6.5 to +9 GyrMilkomeda Successive closer passages, shock-driven starbursts, and then coalescence into a single pressure-supported elliptical. The literature is genuinely unsettled on the odds and the date — see the About dialog's sources — and the page draws one honest track through that uncertainty rather than pretending to certainty.

And after Milkomeda?

The Local Group does not stop at two. M33 — Triangulum, the third-largest member — is bound to the pair and will join the remnant, along with the several dozen dwarf galaxies still orbiting both. Over the following tens of billions of years, the Local Group's members fall together into one large elliptical galaxy. That object is the end state of everything gravitationally bound to us.

Everything else goes the other way. Dark energy is accelerating the expansion of the universe, and the Local Group is not bound to the wider cosmos — the Virgo cluster, the galaxies of the Local Sheet, the several trillion galaxies of the observable universe are all receding, faster with time. In roughly 100 billion years, an observer inside the Local Group's remnant would see a sky with no other galaxies in it at all: a single island of stars in an apparently empty void, with no way to discover from the inside that the rest ever existed. The cosmology we take for granted is a thing that can only be learned during a particular window of cosmic time, and we happen to live in it.

The remnant itself fades. Mergers exhaust cold gas — through starbursts, and by heating what remains into the million-degree circumgalactic halo that eROSITA has now mapped around us, gas which the page can draw. With nothing left to make stars from, the elliptical reddens as its massive blue stars die and are not replaced. What is left is a red-and-dead island: long-lived low-mass stars, their remnants, and time.
