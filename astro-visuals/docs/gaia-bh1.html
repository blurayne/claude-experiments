Gaia BH1

The nearest black hole known: 1,575 light years away toward Ophiuchus, ten solar masses, and completely dark. A star very like the Sun goes round it every 186 days, and its wobble on the sky is how Gaia found it.

The scenario Gaia BH1 — the nearest black hole lands today at a week a second, so the star completes a lap in under half a minute, and the view row's Gaia BH1 entry frames the same orbit without touching the clock. The zoom buttons step in from there — the orbit, then the star's swing, then, twelve decades further in, the hole's own 146-kilometre shadow. There is nothing to see on the way but the star: the piece draws what is there.

What was found

Gaia measures where a star is to a few tens of microarcseconds, many times over. For most stars the positions fit a straight-line proper motion and the yearly parallax ellipse. For Gaia DR3 4373465352415301632, a G-type dwarf of 0.93 solar masses at 483 parsecs, they did not: the star traces an extra ellipse of its own, 1.3 AU across, every 185.6 days. Something with nearly ten solar masses is pulling it round — and there is no light from it at all. El-Badry et al. 2023 (MNRAS 518, 1057) followed the star spectroscopically, confirmed the orbit from the velocity curve, and ruled out every alternative: no star of that mass could hide in the companion's light, and the object is dark in X-rays and radio. A stellar-mass black hole, in a wide, quiet orbit, in our own neighbourhood.

How it is drawn

The system rides the local sky exactly as the Gaia stars do — 483 parsecs along its line of sight, turning with the Sun's own orbital phase — and it is drawn in a frame of its own, the hole at the origin and the star on the relative orbit round it, because the view that shows the shadow is thirty thousand frames wide of the system's barycentre. The orbit's orientation is placed through the same observer-frame derivation the S-stars use, so its ellipse sits in the drawn sky as it sits in the real one.

At the orbit's scale the star is a point and the hole a mark; both are drawn as overdriven markers, a halo round a core, so they can be told from the field. Twelve decades further in the hole itself is drawn by the same ray-marched Schwarzschild shader as Sagittarius A* — with no disc, because there is none: Gaia BH1 is X-ray dark, and a bright disc would be an invention. What remains is the shadow, and a faint ring at the photon sphere standing in for the sky the hole would bend round itself, which the piece does not lens for real. It is a very dark thing, drawn as one.

Is it really the nearest?

Of the confirmed ones, yes. Gaia BH3 (590 pc) is the most massive stellar black hole known in the Galaxy, at 33 solar masses, and Gaia BH2 (1,160 pc) the widest; A0620−00, the nearest of the X-ray binaries, is 1,060 pc off. A dynamical argument has proposed black holes in the Hyades cluster at 150 pc, but that is an inference from the cluster's structure, not a detection, and the piece draws detections.
