The Virgo Supercluster, and the web beyond

Pull back past the Local Group and the galaxies stop being individuals. They come in groups, the groups in clouds and filaments, and the filaments enclose voids: the cosmic web. The piece draws the measured version of it — 8,826 groups of galaxies out to 3,500 kilometres a second, some 150 million light years — and names the clusters the charts name.

Two entries in the view row: Virgo Supercluster, the chart's framing — a cylinder standing on the supergalactic plane, sixty million light years across, the Local Group in the middle and the Virgo cluster's knot fifty million light years off toward one side — and The cosmic web, the whole measured volume from above the plane. The zoom ladder's last rungs land on both. With the labels on: the named groups and clusters with their distances, the brightest nearby galaxies in grey, a drop-line from each named group to the plane.

What the points are

Every point is a catalogue row. The groups are Kourkchi & Tully's 2017 catalogue (ApJ 843, 16): they took the 2MASS Redshift Survey's galaxies out to 3,500 km/s, gathered them into groups by a calibrated linking procedure, and gave each group its supergalactic coordinates, its total near-infrared luminosity, and — where its members have measured distances in the Cosmicflows-3 compilation — a distance. 1,951 groups have one; the piece places the other 6,875 at their Local-Sheet velocity divided by the Hubble constant (74.6 km/s/Mpc, Cosmicflows-4's value), which is a model of distance, not a measurement: a galaxy's velocity has its own motion in it, and toward Virgo that motion is hundreds of kilometres a second. Those groups are drawn a shade bluer, and the table flags them. A group's point is sized by its luminosity — a rich cluster a bright knot, a lone galaxy a faint speck — and the named clusters carry a glow the size of their turnaround radius, recovered from their luminosity by the catalogue's own relation.

The nearby galaxies are Karachentsev, Makarov and Kaisina's Updated Nearby Galaxy Catalog (AJ 145, 101): 869 galaxies within about eleven megaparsecs with names, distances and types — Centaurus A, M81, M83, NGC 253, IC 342, the Maffei pair behind the dust of Cassiopeia, M101, M94 — coloured by type (the ellipticals warm, the spirals blue-white, the irregulars blue) and the bright ones named.

Cloud-9

Near M94, fourteen million light years away, is a cloud of hydrogen with a million solar masses of gas and — after the Hubble Space Telescope looked, in 2025 — no stars in it at all. Cloud-9 is the first good example of a RELHIC, a reionisation-limited HI cloud: a small dark-matter halo that gathered gas in the early Universe but was never massive enough to make that gas form stars once the ultraviolet background heated it. The standard cosmology predicts such objects in numbers; this is the first that has been found and checked. It is drawn as a dashed outline beside M94, at the marker's size — its real gas disc is a few thousand light years across — and nothing inside the outline.

What the box means

The nearby Universe is flattened. De Vaucouleurs noticed in the 1950s that the bright galaxies of the sky lie along a great circle, and named the plane through it the supergalactic plane; the Local Group, the Virgo cluster, the Ursa Major and Coma clouds all sit near it. The chart's cylinder stands on that plane, with its axis at the supergalactic pole (galactic l = 47.4°, b = +6.3°) — which is nearly in the Milky Way's own plane, so the supercluster's "up" is almost sideways to the Galaxy's. The piece derives the frame from those two numbers and nothing else, and the unit tests hold it to the textbook positions of M87 and M81. Beyond the box the points continue to the catalogue's edge, and the view called "the cosmic web" shows them from above the plane: the Virgo–Centaurus filament, the Fornax wall below, and the emptiness of the Local Void on the far side of the Milky Way's plane — which is also where the Galaxy's dust hides everything, the "zone of avoidance", so part of that emptiness is ours, not the Universe's. The article says so because the picture cannot.
