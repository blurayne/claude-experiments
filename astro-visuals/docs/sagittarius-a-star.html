Sagittarius A* and the S-stars

At the centre of the Milky Way, 27,000 light years from here, a black hole of 4.3 million solar masses sits behind thirty magnitudes of dust. We know it is there because we have watched stars go round it.

The scenario The stars round the black hole lands in 2011, with the clock at a year a second, looking along the line of sight from Earth with celestial north up — the view the orbits are published in. S2 laps the hole in sixteen seconds, swinging in to 120 AU at its 2018 pericentre; S55 in thirteen, S38 in nineteen. The view row's Galactic Centre entry frames the same scene without moving the clock, and the zoom buttons step in from there: the ellipses, then a single star's swing, then the hole itself.

The hole

Sagittarius A* is a compact radio source at RA 17h 45m 40s, Dec −29° 00′ 28″. The GRAVITY interferometer's fit to several stellar orbits at once (GRAVITY Collaboration 2022) puts 4.297 million solar masses at that point, 8,277 parsecs away. Its Schwarzschild radius — the radius at which escape speed reaches light speed — is 0.085 AU, twelve million kilometres, a fifth of Mercury's orbit. The shadow a black hole casts is larger than its horizon: light passing within 2.6 Schwarzschild radii is captured, so the dark disc on the sky is 0.44 AU across. At 8.3 kpc that is 53 microarcseconds. The Event Horizon Telescope measured a ring 51.8 ± 2.3 μas across in 2022 — the same number, from a different instrument and a different physics.

The piece draws all of that at its true size: the shadow is 0.44 AU wide, the S-star orbits thousands of AU wide, and the Sun 8.3 kpc off. The hole is the scene's origin. The Sun is 900 scene units out, and a single-precision number there is good to about a hundred AU — the whole of S2's pericentre — so the Centre is drawn in a frame of its own, with the eye's position relative to the hole computed in doubles and every position uploaded relative to it. Nothing here is compressed to make it visible; the zoom is how you cross it.

The S-stars

Fourteen stars are drawn, on the orbits published for them. Thirteen are from the twenty-five year VLT and Keck monitoring collected in Gillessen et al. 2017 (ApJ 837, 30, Table 3). S2's is the interferometric solution from GRAVITY Collaboration 2020 (A&A 636, L5), the paper that measured its Schwarzschild precession. The elements are the observer's: the semi-major axis as an angle on the sky, the inclination to the plane of the sky, the node east of north, the argument of pericentre, the epoch of pericentre. To place them the piece derives the sky's north and east at Sgr A* from the same J2000 rotation that places the real stars, so the drawn ellipses sit in the drawn sky exactly as they sit in the real one; the galactic plane comes out at position angle 31.4° east of north, which is Reid & Brunthaler's measured value.

Which way is depth?

Astrometry alone cannot tell an orbit from its mirror image about the plane of the sky: the ellipse on the sky is the same. The radial velocity breaks the tie. With depth counted away from the observer as r sin(ω+ν) sin i, S2 recedes at up to +4,000 km/s in the months before its 2018 pericentre and approaches at −1,900 afterwards — which is the curve GRAVITY measured (2018, A&A 615, L15, Fig. 2), and the piece's unit test holds the model to it. Every orbit drawn shares that convention.

What is left out

The orbits are Keplerian. S2's Schwarzschild precession — 12 arcminutes per orbit, the 2020 measurement — is a fraction of a degree per lap and is not drawn. Two much-publicised stars are absent on purpose: S62 and S4714, whose sub-ten-year orbits were claimed from single-telescope imaging and were not reproduced by GRAVITY's interferometry. S29, which passed pericentre in 2021, is not drawn because its full element set was not at hand to transcribe; it is on the list for the data workflow. The nuclear star cluster's thousands of other stars are the modelled crowd the piece has always drawn there.

How the hole is drawn

One billboard, and every pixel of it traces a light ray backward from the eye through the Schwarzschild geometry — the null geodesic in a Cartesian embedding, d²x/dλ² = −(3/2)h²x/r⁵, stepped with a leapfrog integrator. Rays that fall in paint the shadow; rays that bend round and strike the disc paint the disc, including the far side lifted over and under the hole, and the thin bright ring where light has circled the hole before landing. Nothing about the shape is authored: the shadow comes out 2.6 Schwarzschild radii wide because that is the photon capture cross-section, and it comes out that size at the hole's true distance.

The disc is the part that is a model. Sagittarius A*'s real accretion flow is faint, hot and thick — the EHT's ring is millimetre-wave emission from it, and in visible light there would be almost nothing to see. What is drawn is a thin, luminous Shakura–Sunyaev sheet from the innermost stable orbit (3 Schwarzschild radii) out to 12, its temperature falling as r−3/4, Doppler-beamed by the orbital speed and reddened by the gravitational redshift — the picture a bright, thin disc would present, drawn round a hole that does not have one, so that the lensing has something to lens. Its inclination is set at 45° to the line of sight, inside the EHT's bound of under 50°, and tilted enough for the bending of the far side to show; the sense of its rotation is a choice. The background sky is not lensed: the stars behind the shadow are simply hidden. The Gaia BH1 article describes the other case, a hole with no flow at all.
