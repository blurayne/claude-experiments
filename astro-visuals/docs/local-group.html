The Local Group

Two big spirals, a third smaller one, and a hundred and thirty dwarfs — most of them so faint that they were found in the last twenty years, and one, perhaps, with no stars in it at all. Everything within about four million light years, drawn where it is.

The view row's Local Group entry pulls the eye out to eleven million light years across, and the zoom ladder's last rung lands there too. With the labels switched on, the chart appears: a box round the whole Group, standing on the plane of the Milky Way, a line from every galaxy down to that plane, and each galaxy's name — the bright ones with their distance from us in a small box, the faint ones in grey. The distances switch in the settings takes the boxes off.

What is in it

The Local Group is the Milky Way, Andromeda, and everything bound to the pair: Triangulum (M33), the third spiral; the two Magellanic Clouds; Andromeda's court of companions (M32, M110, NGC 147, NGC 185, IC 10 and the Andromeda I–XXXVI dwarfs); our own court (Sagittarius, Fornax, Sculptor, Leo I and II, Draco, Ursa Minor, Carina, Sextans, and forty-odd ultra-faint dwarfs found since 2005 in the sky surveys); and the field dwarfs out on their own — Wolf–Lundmark–Melotte, IC 1613, Barnard's Galaxy, Leo A, Pegasus, Cetus, Tucana, Aquarius, the Phoenix dwarf. The piece draws every galaxy within 1.25 megaparsecs (4.1 million light years) of us that the Local Volume Database lists: 130 of them, of which 113 the database counts as confirmed galaxies; the rest are candidates — objects that may yet turn out to be star clusters — and are drawn fainter and named in grey.

The dark galaxy

In 2025 Xu and colleagues, using the FAST radio telescope, found that a clump of gas in a high-velocity cloud toward the Galactic anticentre — AC G185.0−11.5 — rotates like a small disc galaxy. It has no stars that anyone can find, no molecular gas, and by the relation between a galaxy's rotation and its mass it lies about 278 kiloparsecs away, well inside the Group. If it is what it appears to be, it is a dark galaxy: a halo of dark matter that gathered hydrogen but never lit up — a thing the standard cosmology predicts in numbers and that had never been seen this close. The piece draws it as a dashed outline the size of its gas disc, and nothing inside the outline, because nothing is what there is to see. Its distance is a model's, not a measurement, and the name says "candidate". (Cloud-9, the other starless cloud in the news, lies near M94 fourteen million light years away — outside the Group, and a subject for the next view out.)

How it is placed

Each galaxy is put in its true direction, through the same J2000 rotation every star and black hole in the piece goes through, and at its true distance from the catalogue's distance modulus. This needs saying because the rest of the piece does not draw Andromeda there. The merger model keeps separations true out to 83 kiloparsecs and compresses them logarithmically beyond, so that Andromeda at 765 kiloparsecs looms at the edge of the drawn sky rather than twenty-five disk diameters offstage — a disclosed choice, made for the views the piece was built around. The Group's layer therefore fades in only far outside those views, and as it fades in its members slide from the compressed depth to the true one while the model's Andromeda fades out. Once the Group is in view, it is to scale, and nothing is drawn twice.

Each galaxy keeps its angular size: it is drawn on the ellipse of its half-light radius, at the size that radius has from the Sun, in the orientation the catalogue gives. Brightness is not photometric. At their true surface brightness the ultra-faint dwarfs would be invisible at any distance — several are fainter than a single bright star — so every galaxy is drawn as a marker of uniform visibility, brighter for the luminous, with a floor of a few pixels. The Milky Way gets a marker of its own at the centre, since the model's stars are dust from this far out. The forms are the catalogue's: an exponential glow for a spheroidal, knots on it for an irregular, a disc with arms for the spirals, an outline for the dark one. The arms, the knots and the mottling are a model of what such things look like, not the galaxy's own image.

The box

The cylinder is the classic chart's device, and it does the one thing a flat picture of a three-dimensional cloud cannot: it says which way is up. Its axis is the Milky Way's, its mid-plane the Galactic plane extended, and every galaxy has a line dropped to that plane, so how far above or below the plane each one lies can be read off directly. Andromeda's whole court sits 21° below the plane; the Magellanic Clouds are far to the south of it; most of the ultra-faints cluster high above it, where the sky surveys that found them looked.
