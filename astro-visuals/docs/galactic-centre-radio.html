The Galactic Centre in radio

Turn a radio telescope on the middle of the Galaxy and the dust that hides it from every optical instrument simply is not there. What appears is a crowded place: glowing clouds of ionised gas strung along the plane, the shells of exploded stars, and long, thin, luminous filaments that cross the plane at right angles and are found nowhere else in the Milky Way.

The scenario The Galactic Centre in radio and the view row's Galactic Centre, radio entry frame the inner four degrees of the Galaxy — about 1,900 light years across — from the Sun's side, with celestial north up, which is how the map this follows is printed: the Galactic plane then runs from the upper left to the lower right. Zoom in and the view carries on to the S-stars and the black hole; zoom out and the field shrinks into the bulge and fades.

The map

In 2000 LaRosa, Kassim, Lazio and Hyman published a wide-field image of the Galactic Centre made with the Very Large Array at a wavelength of 90 cm (AJ 119, 207) — a mosaic of observations at a frequency low enough that the synchrotron emission of the filaments and the remnants dominates, and wide enough to hold the whole complex in one frame. It is the picture most people have seen of the Centre in radio, and the one that named several of the filaments. The piece does not use the picture. It uses the objects the picture is of: each is placed by its catalogue coordinates and drawn at its catalogue size, and the picture was consulted only for the two objects the map names without giving a designation, whose positions were measured against the objects that have one (marked below).

What the filaments are

The nonthermal filaments are the Centre's signature. They are up to a hundred light years long and a fraction of a light year wide, they shine by synchrotron radiation — electrons spiralling in a magnetic field — and almost all of them run perpendicular to the Galactic plane, which is why they are read as tracing a strong, ordered, vertical magnetic field in the inner few hundred light years. How the electrons are accelerated along them is still argued. MeerKAT's 2022 survey found nearly a thousand of them, ten times what this map shows; the piece draws the ones the map names.

How it is drawn, and what is model

Radio emission has no colour: nothing here would be visible to an eye at any distance. The field is therefore drawn the way radio maps are printed — intensity through a heat ramp, black through red and orange to white — and it is additive, so the things that overlap on the sky brighten one another as they do in the map. Every object is a quad in its own patch of sky at Sagittarius A*'s distance, 8,277 parsecs, its outline the catalogue's ellipse or line, turned by its position angle. The forms are the catalogue's: a soft glow for an HII region, a limb-brightened shell for a remnant, a line for a filament, a head with a tail for the Mouse. The texture inside each — the clumps in a glow, the patches in a rim, the dark lanes in the band along the plane — is noise, a model of the mottling such things have, not the map's pixels. The band itself, the diffuse emission between Sgr B and Sgr C, is drawn as one soft ridge whose extent is the map's and whose texture is not.

One thing the map cannot give is depth. Everything is placed on the sky at the Centre's distance, in one plane, because the true line-of-sight distances of these objects are not known to anything like the precision the drawing would need — Sgr B2 is thought to lie some 130 pc in front of Sgr A* (Reid et al. 2009), the rest are ordered by absorption at best. Seen from the side, the field is therefore a flat sheet: that is what the data supports, and the piece draws no more than that.
