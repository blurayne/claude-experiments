Scale, honestly

Everything is drawn at true proportions, always: real semi-major axes, real diameters, the ~60° tilt of the ecliptic to the galactic plane, and a galactic orbit ~14 billion× wider than Neptune's. Seen from the galaxy the whole planetary system therefore collapses inside the Sun's single pixel — that is the true proportion, and the ☉ dive is how you cross it. There is one clock, and everything on screen reads from it: the civil year, the deep-time eras, the age of the solar system and the galactic year all count the same elapsed Earth years. Earlier versions ran the galaxy about 1.2 million times faster than the planets so both could be watched at once, which meant the civil year and the age of the solar system advanced at wildly different rates from the same moment — they no longer do.

The speed control carries that range instead. At the default of one year a second you watch Earth orbit and the galaxy stands still, because in one year it very nearly does. The ×103 to ×1012 buttons open it up: at ×106 a full galactic lap takes 225 seconds and the planets become smooth rings, at ×109 the Sun's whole life passes in a few seconds. Nothing is compressed behind your back — you choose the scale, and every readout stays true to it. The shuttle drives the clock by hand: +100% is the set speed forward, −100% the same speed backwards, anything between a fraction of it; at 0 it is not engaged and the clock belongs to play/pause. It outranks pause — a paused scene can be nudged either way — and it rests at 0 whenever the piece is opened. Run backwards, the swept paths retract with the clock.

The Oort cloud is the most symbolic object here, squeezed from both directions: really spanning ~3,300 Neptune-orbits (its ring is drawn ~700× too small versus the planets), yet at galaxy scale it would be under a pixel (drawn ~3,300× too big versus the galaxy). Its true outer edge lies ~1.6 light-years out — a third of the way to Alpha Centauri — because that is where the Sun's gravity loses the tug-of-war against galactic tides. It is the one solar-system structure whose size is set by the galaxy itself, and passing stars and the galactic tide are what nudge its comets sunward.

Since the piece always renders true proportions there is no scale toggle to find: the zoom is simply open. Scroll in (or hit ☉ dive) and descend ~9 orders of magnitude — past the Oort shell at its true ~1.6-light-year edge and through the Kuiper belt down to the planets, each drawn at its real diameter but clamped to a pixel so it stays findable. The view-width readout (bottom left) tracks the descent. At planetary zoom the background stars race past — that is the compressed galactic clock (~1.2 million real years per simulated year) made visible at true scale.

Gliese 710

The closest stellar encounter known, in either direction. Gliese 710 is a K7 dwarf about 62 light years away, closing at 14.4 km/s; Gaia's astrometry puts its closest approach 1.29 million years from now at 0.0676 pc — 0.22 light years, or 13,944 AU. That is well inside the Oort cloud, which reaches out to about 1.6 light years, so the star passes through the comet reservoir rather than by it. Expect it to shake comets loose for a few million years afterwards: a modest shower, not a bombardment, and no threat to the planets, which sit four thousand times closer to the Sun than the point it grazes.

The jump menu lands ten thousand years before the closest approach and sets a speed of a thousand years a second, so the pass unfolds over a few tens of seconds. The cloud brightens as the star crosses it, at its true separation.

The Oort cloud & the galactic tide

  • Everything in the solar system falls around the galactic center together, so the center's pull is not felt directly — what matters is the difference in pull across the system: the galactic tide.
  • At 1–2 light-years out that difference rivals the Sun's own weakening grip. The tide sculpts the cloud — slightly stretched toward the galactic center, squashed along the direction of motion — and sets its outer edge exactly where the tug-of-war is lost.
  • Objects nudged past that boundary are stripped and become independent orbiters of the galaxy. Interstellar visitors like 'Oumuamua and Borisov are presumably escapees from other stars' Oort clouds.
  • The traffic runs both ways: the tide (plus passing stars) is the main mechanism that torques Oort comets inward, delivering the long-period comets we see.
  • As a mass, the cloud is insignificant — trillions of objects totalling only a few Earth masses, ~0.001% of the Sun. It is a swarm of passive test particles marking the frontier where the Sun's gravity ends and the galaxy's begins.

Deep-time perspective

  • Even the full 7-million-year hominin story is only 0.031 galactic laps — every human-like creature that ever lived did so within the last 3% of the Sun's current orbit.
  • All 300,000 years of Homo sapiens amount to 0.0013 galactic years: the current lap was 99.9% complete when the first modern humans appeared.
  • Mammals (~200 Myr) span almost exactly one galactic year — when the first mammals appeared, the Sun stood roughly where it stands again today.
  • The solar system is ~20.3 galactic years old. Complex life on land arrived about two laps ago; the dinosaurs died out barely a quarter-lap ago.
  • The Sun bobs through the disk plane every ~45 Myr (shown ~3× exaggerated). Whether these plane crossings correlate with extinction events is speculated but unproven.

How many stars?

The simulation draws ~95,000 star points by default, ~463,000 in hi-fi and ~1.9 million in ultra. The real Milky Way holds an estimated 100–400 billion stars (often quoted as ~200 billion) — extrapolated from the galaxy's mass and the stellar mass function, because most stars are dim red dwarfs no survey can see across the disk. So even in ultra mode each drawn point stands in for roughly 100,000 real stars; in hi-fi it is ~430,000, in the default ~2 million. Rendering one point per real star at 60 fps is far beyond WebGL — for comparison, Gaia, the best real star catalog ever made, has measured about 2 billion stars: roughly 1% of the galaxy. And Gaia maps the sky from inside — the far half of the disk hides behind dust, so the face-on shape drawn here follows the structural measurements (bar length and angle, arm tangents, maser parallaxes, disk profile) rather than any single catalog.

Stellar birth & death

With life cycle on, the arms do what real arms do: gas piles into the wave's inner edge, collapses inside dark clouds, and lights up as blue OB clusters. Massive stars live only 3–30 Myr — a few simulated years on this clock, so an entire stellar life plays out before your eyes: reddish protocluster, blue association, red supergiant, collapse, supernova. The Milky Way's measured star-formation rate is ~2 solar masses (a handful of stars) per year; birth points here match that rate scaled to the point sampling. Old low-mass stars die too, disk-wide: they swell into red giants, puff teal planetary nebulae, and leave slowly fading white dwarfs.

  • Live count switches the two status-bar counters from running totals to what is happening in this moment — stars currently igniting, and stars currently dying — stepping up and down as each event begins and ends. Those are counts of drawn events, so they follow the sampled fraction rather than the real numbers; the totals below are the real ones.
  • The counters in the status bar run at the measured rates rather than counting the drawn points: − supernovae at ~2 per century, + stars born at ~4 a year (a ~2 solar-mass yearly formation rate over a ~0.5 solar-mass mean). Both are tallies since the simulation started, which is why they climb so fast — a single simulated year is ~1.2 million real ones.
  • The Galaxy hosts ~2 supernovae per century. On the compressed clock that is ~24,000 per simulated year — a continuous glitter — so only a sampled fraction is drawn as featured explosions.
  • A supernova peaks near 5×10⁹ solar luminosities: for a few weeks one star rivals billions, approaching the output of its entire galaxy. Its flash (weeks) and expanding remnant (~50,000 years, ~10,000 km/s) both last far less than a pixel of simulated time, so they are stretched here to be seeable at all, and remnants are drawn several times too large — the real Crab Nebula is 11 ly across, well under one scene unit. A remnant is drawn as what it is, a hollow shell: brightest at its rim, where the line of sight runs longest through it, knotted with filaments, thin and sharp while young and thick and frayed when old. What the blast is made of is honest as far as it goes: it expands while it dims, and it cools from blue-white through amber to a red ember, which is the real colour history. The radiating spikes are not. A star has none — they are what a telescope or an eye adds to any point too bright for it, and they are drawn here for the same reason the flash is stretched: without them a collapse and a bright star look identical.
  • Variables: a few percent of the drawn star points pulse and redden at minimum light, standing in for the Cepheids, RR Lyrae and Mira-type pulsators (Miras swing by up to ~8 magnitudes as they cool and reheat); the HII nebulae breathe and drift in hue, as real nebular illumination genuinely does (Hubble's Variable Nebula changes within weeks). Their real day-to-year cycles are far below this clock's resolution — like the supernova flashes, the shimmer you see is a stretched sample.
  • Trails are measurements, nothing else, and they follow the zoom. Close in, where the planets stand apart from the Sun, the orbit and helix switches choose what they show, separately or together: helix draws each planet's true swept path through space — at one year a second the Sun covers 48 AU while the Earth loops once around it, so Mercury winds tightly, Earth coils visibly and the giants barely wave, exactly as reality has it — and orbit draws the closed path around the Sun instead. Where the sampling cannot resolve an orbit, the orbit ring stands in for the helix, since an inaccurate helix is not worth drawing. Zoomed out, the whole system collapses into the Sun's single point and only the Sun's own trail remains; a stylized planetary corkscrew used to be drawn there, and was retired for the reason above: by the time the Sun moves one Earth-orbit radius at galactic scale, the Earth has gone round 440,000 times, and no drawn coil could be anything but an invention.
  • Zoomed all the way in — the floor is about eight solar radii across — the Sun stops being a point and becomes a living disc: churning granulation under limb darkening, prominence arcs rising and falling on a slow magnetic-storm cycle, a streaked corona. The surface detail is drawn with shader noise and is illustrative; the disc's size is not — it is the Sun's true diameter at the true distance.
  • Choosing the Solar System view (top left) is a camera move, not a mode change: it descends nine orders of magnitude to a view about 75 AU across — the whole planetary system, Kuiper belt included, at its true scale, which is why the galaxy shrinks to a band of sky and the Sun to a nearby star. The camera follows the Sun exactly and holds the line from the Sun to the galactic centre, so the core stays behind the planets rather than sweeping past as the Sun orbits; dragging turns the view relative to that line. Time keeps running: at one year a second you can watch the planets orbit, and the background genuinely streams past, because 230 km/s of real solar motion crosses most of a 75 AU view every year. At higher speeds the orbits blur beyond drawing, so each planet's trail gives way to its orbit ring — the closed path itself, which does not change over those windows. Zooming out again is the same journey in reverse; nothing else is altered.
  • What remains is a neutron star (drawn as a fading blue point) or a black hole (nothing). The last naked-eye supernova in our galaxy was Kepler's, in 1604; statistically we are overdue, and Betelgeuse (~550 ly away, already a red supergiant) is a leading candidate.

Star formation, ice ages, and Andromeda

The Galaxy's star formation is winding down: the cold gas is being used up faster than it is replaced, so the rate here decays with a ~6 Gyr e-folding time — roughly twice today's rate early on, about half by an age of 8 Gyr. Then comes Andromeda: a first passage ~4.5 Gyr from now at ~95 kpc that the disks barely feel, then — dynamical friction shrinking each swing — close passages at ~30 and ~13 kpc whose shocks drive starbursts, and coalescence ~8.8 Gyr out, after which the gas-poor remnant forms almost nothing. Watch the arms light up late, then go quiet. Once the two disks are one blob the arm names and Andromeda's own name step down and a single label takes over: Milkomeda, the name Cox & Loeb gave the remnant in 2008.

The galactic environment panel is the honest edge of this: Earth's temperature is set by the Sun, not by where the Sun sits in the Galaxy — interstellar starlight at Earth is around a ten-millionth of sunlight. What galactic position plausibly does change is the cosmic-ray flux: crossing a spiral arm means more nearby supernovae, and the Sun's bob through the dense mid-plane adds more again. Shaviv and Svensmark proposed that this modulates low cloud cover and so the climate, which would match the ~140 Myr spacing of the great glaciations — and the arm crossings here fall at about that interval. The link is contested; the temperatures shown follow that hypothesis, not a settled result, and the interface frosts over when the model dips into a glacial epoch. That whole argument has an expiry date: it rests on crossing spiral arms, and the merged remnant is an elliptical with none, so the arm term fades out with the merger rather than ticking on over a galaxy that no longer has arms.

Andromeda itself is drawn the way the Milky Way is: from a photographic probability map, deprojected from its 77° inclination to face-on — and, since v2.56, one instrument per channel. The Hubble PHAT+PHAST panorama (heic2501a, ~200 million resolved stars; NASA, ESA, B. F. Williams, Z. Chen, L. C. Johnson and the PHAT/PHAST teams, CC BY 4.0) is a strip along the major axis, so it is kept exactly where it has coverage and nowhere else. Luminance and colour across the rest of the disk come from a wide-field optical image (Adam Evans, CC BY 2.0); the dust lanes from Herschel/Planck far-infrared emission (ESA/NASA/JPL-Caltech), which is bright exactly where cold dust is, turned into darkening; the star-forming rings from GALEX ultraviolet (NASA/JPL-Caltech), turned into the blue excess the HII sampler reads. Each picture is deprojected on its own and registered to the panorama by its 10-kpc ring and by cross-correlation; M32's position and the ring's lopsided brightness settle which end of the axis is which. Stars are then sampled from luminance, dust from local darkness, HII from blue excess and drawn in Hα pink. Only sky-plane positions are measured; depths, thickness and halo are modelled, and so is the bulge, which a 77° deprojection cannot recover (it stretches into a column). That composite was then completed by hand and by AI (v2.58): the bulge zone and the seams the pipeline could not close were finished face-on over the measured picture, so what you see there is a completion, not a measurement. So are the line-of-sight placements of M32, M110 and the Giant Southern Stream. The disk stands at its measured orientation — spin pole at galactic (242°, −30°), 120° from ours — turning its real way, ~7% faster than we do. The picture's arm-to-interarm contrast is modest, so when its stars, HII knots and haze are drawn they are weighted toward its ridges — pixels brighter than their own surroundings at arm scale — and an arm's stars are drawn brighter: the arms you see are the map's, favoured, not invented.

The encounter follows the Gaia-era orbit: today's 765 kpc and −110 km/s, a first passage ~4.5 Gyr out near 95 kpc, a 290 kpc rebound, then friction-shrunk passages at ~30 and ~13 kpc and one relaxed elliptical ~8.8 Gyr from now. Separations inside ~83 kpc are drawn to scale — every passage honestly spaced — and log-compressed beyond, which is why today's Andromeda looms at the edge of the sky instead of 25 disk-diameters offstage. Two honest caveats: the tides and the final relaxation are parametrised leading-order effects, not an N-body solution; and the 2025 ensemble reruns (Sawala et al., Nature Astronomy) give only about even odds that the merger happens within 10 Gyr at all once the Large Magellanic Cloud's pull and the measurement errors are included. What is drawn is the median of the merging half of those futures.

Does the merger touch the solar system?

Barely, and not where you would expect. Comparing Andromeda's tidal pull — the difference in its gravity across the solar system — against the Sun's own hold, with its centre 30 kpc away: at Earth's orbit the ratio is 1.3×10−17, at Neptune's 3×10−13. Even for a close passage at 1 kpc it is 3×10−13 at Earth. The planets do not notice. Two galaxies pass through each other and the orbits stay exactly as they are, because stars are so tiny against the distances between them that direct collisions are essentially impossible.

The Oort cloud is another matter, as it is the one structure the Galaxy already governs. The Sun loses the tug-of-war beyond ~6.8 ly with Andromeda at 30 kpc — safely outside the cloud's 1.6 ly edge — but at a 1 kpc passage that boundary closes to 0.23 ly, and most of the cloud is stripped away into intergalactic space. The comets go first.

What does change is where the Sun ends up. Its ordered 230 km/s circular orbit is scattered into a random one in the merged elliptical. N-body work on this encounter finds a median final distance near 30 kpc against today's 8, with roughly a 12% chance of being flung into the tidal tails and 3% of ending up bound to Andromeda instead. The Sun here follows that median, drifting outward after the collision. The rotation curve is flat, so its speed stays near 230 km/s and the laps lengthen instead — out to 3.75× today's 225 Myr — which is why the galactic-year counter visibly slows as the orbit widens. Once the remnant has relaxed there are no laps left to count at all: an ordered circular orbit has become a random one, so the counter gives way to the reading that still means something, the Sun's distance from the centre.

What's real, what's modeled

  • Planets are not dragged behind the Sun — the viral "vortex" video is wrong. With the 60° tilt they run ahead of the Sun for half of every orbit, exactly as shown.
  • The disk stars rotate with a flat rotation curve (~230 km/s at almost every radius) and shear differentially — but the bar, arms and Local Spur ride a rigid density-wave pattern (corotation drawn at ~19,000 ly), so stars stream through the arms and the pattern never winds up: density-wave theory's answer to the winding problem. Current measurements actually give the bar and the spiral different pattern speeds, and N-body simulations suggest real arms may be transient and recurring rather than permanent waves — a live debate, simplified here to one pattern.
  • Bar (half-length ~15,000 ly, 28° to the Sun–center line), arms sprouting from the bar tips (Scutum–Centaurus, Perseus + Sagittarius, Norma), and the Sun's Local Spur follow Milky Way measurements.
  • The warped outer disk and its slow retrograde precession follow Gaia's findings.
  • Music: “Galactic Year” I & II by barbedgreenroom3, shipped alongside this page. Supernovae play one of five recorded blasts and star births one of four soft ignitions, picked at random; the drone and the nebula exhales are synthesized live in the browser. Only half of all births sound, or at these rates they would never stop. Each effect has its own switch.
  • The asteroid belt (~1,500 points at 2.1–3.3 AU) carves out the Kirkwood gaps at Jupiter's 3:1, 5:2 and 7:3 resonances, and its orbital periods are true Kepler in both scale modes.
  • The galaxy's face-on layout follows the well-known face-on Milky Way illustration, used as a density map: stars are placed where it is bright and coloured from its pixels, dust lanes where it is dark, HII regions where it is pink. The copy shipped here is mirrored so the arms trail the rotation and rotated so its bar sits at the scene's measured 28° — an illustration too, of course: no one has photographed our galaxy from outside. Opened offline, the procedural layout stands in.
  • Andromeda's map is photographic, not an illustration: the Hubble PHAT+PHAST panorama, the largest photomosaic Hubble has produced, deprojected to face-on by the build tooling and reduced to a 448-pixel probability map. Its rings, its offset inner ring, its dust lanes and its big warm bulge are where the mosaic puts them; the vertical structure is modelled. Opened offline, a schematic Andromeda stands in.
  • Stars are drawn additively, which is what starlight does — but a screen stops at white, so a galaxy core, where thousands of sprites land on one pixel, used to clip into a featureless white hole, worst of all during the merger. The scene is rendered into a half-float buffer that holds those sums, and core glare under Visuals sets how much headroom they get on the way back down: the curve is exact below the knee and rolls off above it, so cores keep their gradient and colour. At 100% there is no compression and the old clipping returns.
  • The gaia sky layer is not modelled at all: it is the 100,000 brightest stars from AT-HYG 3.2, of which 99.9% carry Gaia DR3 parallax distances (Hipparcos covers the very bright ones Gaia saturates on), out to ~1,760 pc, rotated into galactic coordinates and placed relative to the Sun. Their colours come from measured colour indices and their sizes from apparent magnitude, so the solar neighbourhood — Alpha Centauri at 1.32 pc, Sirius at 2.64 — really is where it is. Earlier builds mirrored this sky left-for-right; the frame is now checked against the Sun's own orbital direction. At hi-fi quality and above a second tier loads: 400,000 more stars, complete to magnitude 10.4. Each star also carries its measured Gaia DR3 space velocity and drifts along it as time runs — watch the nearby stars stream at ×10³ — with the linear extrapolation honestly stopped at ±20 Myr. It covers the local sky only: Gaia sees the Galaxy from inside and the far side of the disk hides behind dust, which is why everything beyond it stays modelled. Below the picture's resolution the innermost parsecs add the nuclear stellar disc (~200 pc) and the nuclear star cluster around Sgr A* — linked structures that grow together, fed by gas the bar drives inward. The bubble of real stars rides the Sun's orbital frame — the side facing the galactic centre keeps facing it as the Sun goes round — and star sprites use Gaia Sky's two-part profile: a wide corona around a hot core that saturates to white, with sub-pixel stars keeping their flux rather than their size. Nothing on screen is magnified, so they hold their true places at every zoom.
  • Surface temperature is today's mean scaled by the fourth root of the Sun's output, with the disputed cosmic-ray/cloud term on top. That is an equilibrium argument, not a climate model: it has no runaway greenhouse, no atmospheric loss and no ocean, so treat the far-future numbers as the right order of magnitude rather than a forecast.
  • The Sun's own life runs on the standard track: the faint-young-Sun relation on the main sequence, which it leaves at ~10.9 Gyr, a climb up the red giant branch to ~256 solar radii at ~12.17 Gyr — past Earth's orbit, which is where the scenario takes you — then the helium flash, a second climb, and a white dwarf by ~12.4 Gyr. Sun strength reads its luminosity against today's. Once the Earth is inside that surface the panel stops reporting on it, because there is nothing left to report. The disc's colour is not painted on: its temperature follows from the model's own luminosity and radius as a blackbody, which is why the giant is red, the horizontal-branch Sun orange, and the nebula's central star blue-white. As the surface reaches each inner planet — Mercury first, then Venus, then the Earth; Mars, at 1.5 AU, is outside the 256 solar radii the giant reaches and survives — the planet flares white for a moment and is gone for good, its path with it. The flare is a marker, not a prediction: a planet entering the envelope spirals in over centuries with no flash anyone could see. The planetary nebula that follows is drawn at its true size — about a light-year across by the end, so the view from the planets is from inside it, where a shell shows nothing — and as the event it is: the envelope goes first, dense and dusty and lit warm by the still-cool star, in a burst of light that is the star's glare scattered off the fresh ejecta rather than any shock; then the exposed core heats and its ionisation front sweeps outward, [O III] teal inside the front and warm dust still beyond it, while the fast wind hollows a cavity, leaving the limb-brightened, filamentary shell with Hα at its rim, cometary knots where the front passed and a faint halo of the earlier, slower wind; then it thins and dissolves. It is stretched in time like every death here, a third of a gigayear for something that really lasts twenty thousand years. The Sun's label reads Anthropic Nebula while it is on screen.
  • Earth as a globe, and the Moon. Close in — the Earth view, or two presses of the zoom from it — Earth is drawn as a sphere shaded in the fragment: the Sun's light, a terminator softened and reddened by the air, clouds with their shadows, Rayleigh blue thickening toward the limb, a glint on open water, and on the night side the cities, in the twenty thousand years around now that have them. Its coastlines are today's real ones (the GSHHG shoreline data), cut into seven plates that each ride a rotation about a fixed pole through time — a schematic of the published reconstructions, not a plate model: each pole is the great circle from a plate's seat today to its seat in Pangaea, the angles set so the continents land where the maps put them, and the future runs the same arcs back: Pangaea at 250 Myr ago, the Atlantic opening, India and Australia running north, and ahead the Atlantic closing again into Pangaea Proxima around +250 Myr. Before the map has meaning the surface is noise. The plates creep a degree in ten million years, so the Plate tectonics scenario runs the clock at ten million years a second, and Pangaea, Pangaea Proxima and the oceans boiling away are events of their own in the list — the first two still portraits at a year a second over the supercontinent, the third the same face at ten million years a second while the seas withdraw; at anything past a few weeks a second a frame spans whole days and the day/night line would land somewhere new each time, so the globe is then lit by the daily mean for its latitude instead. The view row also flies to the Moon herself, followed the same way and lit the same way, her orbit drawn behind her. Tick spin lock under the view row (or in the settings' visuals) and the camera turns with the planet, so the same face stays in view while the clock runs — the way to watch the plates drift. Where the map has no depths, the oceans retreat toward the regions farthest from any coast as they go. Through the clock it shows the Hadean's molten crust, the methane-hazed orange Archean until the Great Oxidation, the Huronian and Cryogenian snowballs, ice caps from the rock record up to today — the Ordovician, the Karoo and the Cenozoic ice ages, hothouse between them, Pangaea's world without a cap — and from the piece's own climate model beyond today (the HUD's glacial badge is that model's word about arm crossings, not the globe's), the dry interiors of Pangaea and of Proxima, land greening from the Devonian, the moist greenhouse drying the oceans a billion years ahead, and the crust melting again under the red giant. The Moon orbits it at its true distance, receding as it always has — 3.8 cm a year now, far faster when young — on a power law fitted to formation at the Theia impact rather than a dynamical model, its period from Kepler's law; its orbit is drawn once it spans more than a few pixels.
  • Planet 9? is the one body here that may not exist: a ~6 Earth-mass planet on a ~400 AU orbit, proposed to explain the clustered orbits of the far Kuiper belt. Its orbit is drawn from those estimates and its question mark is part of its name. Switch it off in Visuals if you would rather keep to what has been seen.
  • The dark clouds multiply what lies behind them down, and since v2.76.0 they do it the way interstellar dust does — blue absorbed most, red least, in the 0.82 : 1 : 1.32 proportion of the standard RV = 3.1 extinction law — so the lanes read brown against the disk and the Great Rift red-brown across the band, instead of the grey-violet a neutral darkening left over the pink haze. The HII knots lean toward Hα red rather than magenta.
  • On a first visit a thirty-millisecond off-screen probe draws the star field into a hidden buffer a few times, two frames in, and sets the quality row from the time one pass takes: lowest, low or medium, never more, and the pixel ratio drops to 1× when even that is slow. The choice is saved; change it here any time. The quality row raises the drawn star count five-fold (~460,000 points) and scatters discrete dark molecular clouds (Dunkelwolken) through the disk on top of the arm-edge dust lanes — closer to the Milky Way's real texture, at the cost of GPU load. The row runs on to ×8 at a hundred and sixty-fold — some seventeen million sprites — which will not fit on most machines; a failed allocation drops back to the last setting that worked. Only one heavy density is held at a time. The star gain slider does two things at once, because they are the same intent: it raises a floor under the faintest stars' colour, and it widens the smallest sprites. The second matters more than it sounds — a star clamped to a single pixel loses most of its light to the grid, so widening the floor recovers far more brightness than lifting colour alone. Both leave anything already bright untouched. From inside the disk — the dive and the Solar System views — the band's light all lies behind the local clouds, so haze, HII regions and core are drawn first and the dust over them: that is the Great Rift you see splitting the band and hiding the centre, as it does from Earth. From outside, the arms' HII knots sit on the lanes and are drawn on top of them. One exception among the stars: the nuclear disc and the star cluster around Sgr A* — a few hundred sprites that from 8 kpc land on a single pixel — are not drawn from inside the disk, since from Earth they sit behind some thirty magnitudes of extinction; the bulge's star cloud around them draws as ever.
  • The inter-arm disk follows an exponential profile with the measured ~8,500 ly scale length, plus a thicker, older, yellower second disk at ~3× the scale height — the thin/thick disk structure Gaia confirmed.
