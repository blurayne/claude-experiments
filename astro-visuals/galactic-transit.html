Scale, honestly

Everything is drawn at true proportions, always: real semi-major axes, real diameters, the ~60° tilt of the ecliptic to the galactic plane, and a galactic orbit ~14 billion× wider than Neptune's. Seen from the galaxy the whole planetary system therefore collapses inside the Sun's single pixel — that is the true proportion, and the ☉ dive is how you cross it. There is one clock, and everything on screen reads from it: the civil year, the deep-time eras, the age of the solar system and the galactic year all count the same elapsed Earth years. Earlier versions ran the galaxy about 1.2 million times faster than the planets so both could be watched at once, which meant the civil year and the age of the solar system advanced at wildly different rates from the same moment — they no longer do.

The speed control carries that range instead. At the default of one year a second you watch Earth orbit and the galaxy stands still, because in one year it very nearly does. The ×103 to ×1012 buttons open it up: at ×106 a full galactic lap takes 225 seconds and the planets become smooth rings, at ×109 the Sun's whole life passes in a few seconds. Nothing is compressed behind your back — you choose the scale, and every readout stays true to it.

The Oort cloud is the most symbolic object here, squeezed from both directions: really spanning ~3,300 Neptune-orbits (its ring is drawn ~700× too small versus the planets), yet at galaxy scale it would be under a pixel (drawn ~3,300× too big versus the galaxy). Its true outer edge lies ~1.6 light-years out — a third of the way to Alpha Centauri — because that is where the Sun's gravity loses the tug-of-war against galactic tides. It is the one solar-system structure whose size is set by the galaxy itself, and passing stars and the galactic tide are what nudge its comets sunward.

Since the piece always renders true proportions there is no scale toggle to find: the zoom is simply open. Scroll in (or hit ☉ dive) and descend ~9 orders of magnitude — past the Oort shell at its true ~1.6-light-year edge and through the Kuiper belt down to the planets, each drawn at its real diameter but clamped to a pixel so it stays findable. The view-width readout (bottom left) tracks the descent. At planetary zoom the background stars race past — that is the compressed galactic clock (~1.2 million real years per simulated year) made visible at true scale.

Gliese 710

The closest stellar encounter known, in either direction. Gliese 710 is a K7 dwarf about 62 light years away, closing at 14.4 km/s; Gaia's astrometry puts its closest approach 1.29 million years from now at 0.0676 pc — 0.22 light years, or 13,944 AU. That is well inside the Oort cloud, which reaches out to about 1.6 light years, so the star passes through the comet reservoir rather than by it. Expect it to shake comets loose for a few million years afterwards: a modest shower, not a bombardment, and no threat to the planets, which sit four thousand times closer to the Sun than the point it grazes.

The jump menu lands ten thousand years before the closest approach and sets a speed of a thousand years a second, so the pass unfolds over a few tens of seconds. The cloud brightens as the star crosses it, at its true separation.

The Oort cloud & the galactic tide

  • Everything in the solar system falls around the galactic center together, so the center's pull is not felt directly — what matters is the difference in pull across the system: the galactic tide.
  • At 1–2 light-years out that difference rivals the Sun's own weakening grip. The tide sculpts the cloud — slightly stretched toward the galactic center, squashed along the direction of motion — and sets its outer edge exactly where the tug-of-war is lost.
  • Objects nudged past that boundary are stripped and become independent orbiters of the galaxy. Interstellar visitors like 'Oumuamua and Borisov are presumably escapees from other stars' Oort clouds.
  • The traffic runs both ways: the tide (plus passing stars) is the main mechanism that torques Oort comets inward, delivering the long-period comets we see.
  • As a mass, the cloud is insignificant — trillions of objects totalling only a few Earth masses, ~0.001% of the Sun. It is a swarm of passive test particles marking the frontier where the Sun's gravity ends and the galaxy's begins.

Deep-time perspective

  • Even the full 7-million-year hominin story is only 0.031 galactic laps — every human-like creature that ever lived did so within the last 3% of the Sun's current orbit.
  • All 300,000 years of Homo sapiens amount to 0.0013 galactic years: the current lap was 99.9% complete when the first modern humans appeared.
  • Mammals (~200 Myr) span almost exactly one galactic year — when the first mammals appeared, the Sun stood roughly where it stands again today.
  • The solar system is ~20.3 galactic years old. Complex life on land arrived about two laps ago; the dinosaurs died out barely a quarter-lap ago.
  • The Sun bobs through the disk plane every ~45 Myr (shown ~3× exaggerated). Whether these plane crossings correlate with extinction events is speculated but unproven.

How many stars?

The simulation draws ~95,000 star points by default, ~463,000 in hi-fi and ~1.9 million in ultra. The real Milky Way holds an estimated 100–400 billion stars (often quoted as ~200 billion) — extrapolated from the galaxy's mass and the stellar mass function, because most stars are dim red dwarfs no survey can see across the disk. So even in ultra mode each drawn point stands in for roughly 100,000 real stars; in hi-fi it is ~430,000, in the default ~2 million. Rendering one point per real star at 60 fps is far beyond WebGL — for comparison, Gaia, the best real star catalog ever made, has measured about 2 billion stars: roughly 1% of the galaxy. And Gaia maps the sky from inside — the far half of the disk hides behind dust, so the face-on shape drawn here follows the structural measurements (bar length and angle, arm tangents, maser parallaxes, disk profile) rather than any single catalog.

Stellar birth & death

With life cycle on, the arms do what real arms do: gas piles into the wave's inner edge, collapses inside dark clouds, and lights up as blue OB clusters. Massive stars live only 3–30 Myr — a few simulated years on this clock, so an entire stellar life plays out before your eyes: reddish protocluster, blue association, red supergiant, collapse, supernova. The Milky Way's measured star-formation rate is ~2 solar masses (a handful of stars) per year; birth points here match that rate scaled to the point sampling. Old low-mass stars die too, disk-wide: they swell into red giants, puff teal planetary nebulae, and leave slowly fading white dwarfs.

  • Live count switches the two status-bar counters from running totals to what is happening in this moment — stars currently igniting, and stars currently dying — stepping up and down as each event begins and ends. Those are counts of drawn events, so they follow the sampled fraction rather than the real numbers; the totals below are the real ones.
  • The counters in the status bar run at the measured rates rather than counting the drawn points: − supernovae at ~2 per century, + stars born at ~4 a year (a ~2 solar-mass yearly formation rate over a ~0.5 solar-mass mean). Both are tallies since the simulation started, which is why they climb so fast — a single simulated year is ~1.2 million real ones.
  • The Galaxy hosts ~2 supernovae per century. On the compressed clock that is ~24,000 per simulated year — a continuous glitter — so only a sampled fraction is drawn as featured explosions.
  • A supernova peaks near 5×10⁹ solar luminosities: for a few weeks one star rivals billions, approaching the output of its entire galaxy. Its flash (weeks) and expanding remnant (~50,000 years, ~10,000 km/s) both last far less than a pixel of simulated time, so they are stretched here to be seeable at all, and remnants are drawn several times too large — the real Crab Nebula is 11 ly across, well under one scene unit.
  • Variables: a few percent of the drawn star points pulse and redden at minimum light, standing in for the Cepheids, RR Lyrae and Mira-type pulsators (Miras swing by up to ~8 magnitudes as they cool and reheat); the HII nebulae breathe and drift in hue, as real nebular illumination genuinely does (Hubble's Variable Nebula changes within weeks). Their real day-to-year cycles are far below this clock's resolution — like the supernova flashes, the shimmer you see is a stretched sample.
  • Trails are drawn only as far back as the sampling can resolve — roughly two hundred orbits per body. A true helix along the Sun's galactic track is undrawable: by the time the Sun has moved one Earth-orbit radius, the Earth has gone round some 440,000 times. At high speed the helix trails therefore switch to an honest emblem of that motion — the coil count is set by the slider, not by physics, and the coil radius is symbolic — while at low speed, where the true orbits are resolvable, the real paths are drawn. Turn "stylized" off to keep only what is measurable.
  • The dive holds the camera on the line from the Sun to the galactic centre, so the core stays behind the planets rather than swinging out of frame as the Sun carries on around its orbit. Dragging still turns the view; it just turns relative to that line now.
  • What remains is a neutron star (drawn as a fading blue point) or a black hole (nothing). The last naked-eye supernova in our galaxy was Kepler's, in 1604; statistically we are overdue, and Betelgeuse (~550 ly away, already a red supergiant) is a leading candidate.

Star formation, ice ages, and Andromeda

The Galaxy's star formation is winding down: the cold gas is being used up faster than it is replaced, so the rate here decays with a ~6 Gyr e-folding time — roughly twice today's rate early on, about half by an age of 8 Gyr. Then comes Andromeda, ~4.5 Gyr from now: the encounter shock-compresses the remaining gas into a starburst of several times the present rate, and afterwards the gas-poor merged remnant forms almost nothing. Watch the arms light up and then go quiet.

The galactic environment panel is the honest edge of this: Earth's temperature is set by the Sun, not by where the Sun sits in the Galaxy — interstellar starlight at Earth is around a ten-millionth of sunlight. What galactic position plausibly does change is the cosmic-ray flux: crossing a spiral arm means more nearby supernovae, and the Sun's bob through the dense mid-plane adds more again. Shaviv and Svensmark proposed that this modulates low cloud cover and so the climate, which would match the ~140 Myr spacing of the great glaciations — and the arm crossings here fall at about that interval. The link is contested; the temperatures shown follow that hypothesis, not a settled result, and the interface frosts over when the model dips into a glacial epoch.

Andromeda's approach is compressed: at 2.5 million light years it would sit 83,000 units away, far outside the drawn sky. Its bulge, inclined disk and ring-like arms are schematic, and the encounter is a leading-order sketch — the tidal pull grows with radius, so the outer disk is drawn into a bridge toward the companion while the core barely moves. A real merger is an N-body problem, and the real one will take a couple of billion years to settle.

Does the merger touch the solar system?

Barely, and not where you would expect. Comparing Andromeda's tidal pull — the difference in its gravity across the solar system — against the Sun's own hold, with its centre 30 kpc away: at Earth's orbit the ratio is 1.3×10−17, at Neptune's 3×10−13. Even for a close passage at 1 kpc it is 3×10−13 at Earth. The planets do not notice. Two galaxies pass through each other and the orbits stay exactly as they are, because stars are so tiny against the distances between them that direct collisions are essentially impossible.

The Oort cloud is another matter, as it is the one structure the Galaxy already governs. The Sun loses the tug-of-war beyond ~6.8 ly with Andromeda at 30 kpc — safely outside the cloud's 1.6 ly edge — but at a 1 kpc passage that boundary closes to 0.23 ly, and most of the cloud is stripped away into intergalactic space. The comets go first.

What does change is where the Sun ends up. Its ordered 230 km/s circular orbit is scattered into a random one in the merged elliptical. N-body work on this encounter finds a median final distance near 30 kpc against today's 8, with roughly a 12% chance of being flung into the tidal tails and 3% of ending up bound to Andromeda instead. The Sun here follows that median, drifting outward after the collision — watch the galactic-year counter slow as its orbit widens.

What's real, what's modeled

  • Planets are not dragged behind the Sun — the viral "vortex" video is wrong. With the 60° tilt they run ahead of the Sun for half of every orbit, exactly as shown.
  • The disk stars rotate with a flat rotation curve (~230 km/s at almost every radius) and shear differentially — but the bar, arms and Local Spur ride a rigid density-wave pattern (corotation drawn at ~19,000 ly), so stars stream through the arms and the pattern never winds up: density-wave theory's answer to the winding problem. Current measurements actually give the bar and the spiral different pattern speeds, and N-body simulations suggest real arms may be transient and recurring rather than permanent waves — a live debate, simplified here to one pattern.
  • Bar (half-length ~15,000 ly, 28° to the Sun–center line), arms sprouting from the bar tips (Scutum–Centaurus, Perseus + Sagittarius, Norma), and the Sun's Local Spur follow Milky Way measurements.
  • The warped outer disk and its slow retrograde precession follow Gaia's findings.
  • Music: “Galactic Year” I & II by barbedgreenroom3, shipped alongside this page. Supernovae play one of five recorded blasts and star births one of four soft ignitions, picked at random; the drone and the nebula exhales are synthesized live in the browser. Only half of all births sound, or at these rates they would never stop. Each effect has its own switch.
  • The asteroid belt (~1,500 points at 2.1–3.3 AU) carves out the Kirkwood gaps at Jupiter's 3:1, 5:2 and 7:3 resonances, and its orbital periods are true Kepler in both scale modes.
  • The galaxy's face-on layout follows the well-known face-on Milky Way illustration, used as a density map: stars are placed where it is bright and coloured from its pixels, dust lanes where it is dark, HII regions where it is pink. The copy shipped here is mirrored so the arms trail the rotation and rotated so its bar sits at the scene's measured 28° — an illustration too, of course: no one has photographed our galaxy from outside. Opened offline, the procedural layout stands in.
  • The gaia sky layer is not modelled at all: it is the 100,000 brightest stars from AT-HYG 3.2, of which 98.7% carry Gaia DR3 parallax distances (Hipparcos covers the very bright ones Gaia saturates on), out to ~1,760 pc, rotated into galactic coordinates and placed relative to the Sun. Their colours come from measured colour indices and their sizes from apparent magnitude, so the solar neighbourhood — Alpha Centauri at 1.32 pc, Sirius at 2.64 — really is where it is. Earlier builds mirrored this sky left-for-right; the frame is now checked against the Sun's own orbital direction. At hi-fi quality and above a second tier loads: 400,000 more stars, complete to magnitude 11.2. Each star also carries its measured Gaia DR3 space velocity and drifts along it as time runs — watch the nearby stars stream at ×10³ — with the linear extrapolation honestly stopped at ±20 Myr. It covers the local sky only: Gaia sees the Galaxy from inside and the far side of the disk hides behind dust, which is why everything beyond it stays modelled. Below the picture's resolution the innermost parsecs add the nuclear stellar disc (~200 pc) and the nuclear star cluster around Sgr A* — linked structures that grow together, fed by gas the bar drives inward. The bubble of real stars rides the Sun's orbital frame — the side facing the galactic centre keeps facing it as the Sun goes round — and star sprites use Gaia Sky's two-part profile: a wide corona around a hot core that saturates to white, with sub-pixel stars keeping their flux rather than their size. Nothing on screen is magnified, so they hold their true places at every zoom.
  • The quality row raises the drawn star count five-fold (~460,000 points) and scatters discrete dark molecular clouds (Dunkelwolken) through the disk on top of the arm-edge dust lanes — closer to the Milky Way's real texture, at the cost of GPU load. The row runs on to ×8 at a hundred and sixty-fold — some seventeen million sprites — which will not fit on most machines; a failed allocation drops back to the last setting that worked. Only one heavy density is held at a time. The star gain slider does two things at once, because they are the same intent: it raises a floor under the faintest stars' colour, and it widens the smallest sprites. The second matters more than it sounds — a star clamped to a single pixel loses most of its light to the grid, so widening the floor recovers far more brightness than lifting colour alone. Both leave anything already bright untouched.
  • The inter-arm disk follows an exponential profile with the measured ~8,500 ly scale length, plus a thicker, older, yellower second disk at ~3× the scale height — the thin/thick disk structure Gaia confirmed.
